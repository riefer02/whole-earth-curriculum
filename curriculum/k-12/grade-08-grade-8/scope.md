# Grade 8 Scope

## How to read this scope

Each `unit_id` in the frontmatter resolves to a unit file under `units/`.
`start_day`/`end_day` map the unit onto the 180-day school year; the list is a
continuous sequence from day 1 to day 180 with no gaps or overlaps. `domain` and
`pillar` name the primary domain(s) and pillar(s) each unit serves. `domain_weighting`
expresses the intended balance of instructional time across the ten domains, in
percent of the year. `lesson_count` counts the core lessons; the days between lessons
carry flex time, assessment, integration, and celebration. `assessment_plan`
describes how a learner's growth is seen and recorded at this level.

## Year at a glance

The Grade 8 year is a spiral that deepens what Grade 7 began — and it is the year the
world resolves into systems, and the learner into a person who can hold more than one
thing at once. Where Grade 7 learned to hold contradiction at once and to hear the
reasons behind a rule, Grade 8 begins to *analyze* — to pull a reaction apart, to weigh
competing accounts, to model a relationship with a line, and to critique a work with
evidence rather than mere taste. This is the top of the 6–8 band: formal operations now
work with growing confidence, so abstract and hypothetical reasoning, source
evaluation, and real ethical dilemmas become the daily work, always met with structure,
agency, and hope.

The year opens inward. *Feelings, Identity & Purpose* builds on Grade 7's precise
naming of mixed and conflicting feelings: now learners *analyze* a strong emotional
reaction by separating the event, the thought, and the feeling, and explain how emotion
can both inform and distort judgment, using a personal example. They explain how
multiple and intersecting identities — culture, gender, ability, and language — shape a
whole self, and analyze where self-esteem comes from, distinguishing inner worth from
outside approval. They manage impulses deliberately, choosing a response rather than
reacting in emotionally charged moments, and explain the difference between short-term
and long-term consequences and weigh them in a decision. Identity formation now runs at
full strength, so every self is honored as a whole made of many intersecting parts,
never a single label, and every question is held as a question.

*My Body, Movement & Wellness* then reads the body with new inward precision, building
on Grade 7's naming of internal signals. Learners explain the basic role of the nervous
system in sensing and responding — the fight, flight, or rest response — and describe
how stress affects the body over time, naming healthy ways to help the body return to
calm. They design a short movement routine and explain how each part builds strength,
endurance, or flexibility, and demonstrate fair play and inclusive practices that
welcome peers of all abilities into movement and games. The body is taught with dignity
and joy, honoring the wide range of bodies and abilities, and the rapid, uneven growth
of these years is named as normal, never shameful. *Relationships, Empathy & Conflict*
turns outward into the practice of care at the moment peers matter most. Where Grade 7
set and respected boundaries and repaired relationships, Grade 8 learns to *facilitate*:
learners analyze how tone, body language, and digital messages can change meaning in
communication, and manage conflict in a group by facilitating discussion and seeking a
solution all can accept. They analyze a conflict from multiple perspectives, including
ones they disagree with, and recognize bias and stereotypes that can block empathy and
question them. They analyze how power and resources are shared within a community and
who is left out, take part in strengthening belonging in their school or local
community, analyze how systems of care and mutual aid operate and what makes them fair
or unfair, and distinguish charity from mutual aid and solidarity, explaining the
difference.

The heart of the year is given to the two great tools of school, now wielded with a
full critical edge: argument and number. *Reading, Writing, Argument & Many Languages*
builds from Grade 7's evaluation of a speaker's reasoning toward the analysis of
*rhetoric*. Learners analyze how a speaker's word choice, tone, and delivery shape
meaning and persuade an audience, and engage in a structured debate or deliberation,
arguing a position with reasons while listening to opposing views. They analyze how
point of view, tone, and figurative language shape a reader's understanding of a text,
and read texts from multiple cultures and time periods, comparing how each reflects its
context. They write in multiple forms — narrative, argument, and informational text —
with coherent organization and voice, and cite sources accurately in writing,
distinguishing their own words and ideas from those of others. They analyze how
language can include or exclude people, such as jargon or the use of one language over
another, and use digital translation tools critically, knowing their limits and when
human translation is needed — always in the learner's home language first, with
multilingualism held as wealth. *Functions, Linear Equations, Geometry & Data* grows
from Grade 7's proportions, rational numbers, and chance into the first true algebra and
the language of functions. Learners distinguish rational and irrational numbers and
approximate square roots by locating them on the number line, and use integer exponents
and scientific notation to represent and compare very large and very small quantities,
such as distances in space or the size of cells. They define, evaluate, and compare
functions and use function notation to describe how one quantity depends on another,
and solve linear equations in one variable and analyze a pair of simultaneous linear
equations by graphing. They describe translations, rotations, reflections, and
dilations and use them to show congruence and similarity, and use the Pythagorean
theorem to solve problems about right triangles and distances. They construct and
interpret scatter plots for two related variables and describe patterns of association,
and fit a line informally to data to model a relationship, evaluate the fit, and explain
that association does not prove causation. Mathematics remains sense-making with real
things, drawn from many traditions, for everyone.

From there the year looks out at the world through the lens of systems — and the
difference between a model and the thing itself. *Science: Systems, Evidence & Models*
deepens Grade 7's correlation-versus-causation into the full discipline of controlled
inquiry. Learners design a controlled experiment with a clear hypothesis, variables, and
a plan for repeated trials and controls, and explain how scientific models are revised
when new evidence appears, using a historical example. They explain how organisms
inherit and express traits through genes (DNA) and how the environment interacts with
genes, and analyze how changes in one population ripple through an ecosystem and can
reduce or restore balance. They explain atoms, elements, and compounds and how chemical
reactions rearrange atoms to form new substances, and explain electric and magnetic
fields and how they interact to produce forces and currents. They describe the life
cycle of stars, the formation of elements, and our place in the galaxy, and explain how
plate tectonics shapes continents, mountains, and ocean basins over millions of years.
Origins here are offered as *models with evidence*, held as inquiry alongside other
ways of knowing, never as creed. *Making, Composing, Devising & Designing* gives craft
to the imagination at the moment formal reasoning emerges fully: learners create a
series of artworks exploring one theme or idea, reflecting on how the work changes
across the series, and critique an artwork using evidence about its elements, context,
and purpose, distinguishing observation from judgment. They compose a piece in a chosen
style or tradition and reflect on the choices that shaped it, and critique a musical
work using evidence about its elements, context, and effect, separating observation
from judgment. They choreograph or devise a piece exploring one theme and reflect on
how the work changed through rehearsal, and critique a performance using evidence about
its movement, expression, context, and purpose. They design and make a working
prototype that solves a defined problem and revise it through testing and feedback, and
critique a design using evidence about its function, materials, fairness, and
environmental footprint. The arts of other cultures are met as living traditions to
honor with respectful questions, never as specimens.

*Healthy, Safe & Strong: Substance & Self-Care* returns to the body at a turning point,
building on Grade 7's media and body image and first aid. Learners plan and prepare a
simple balanced meal using safe food-handling practices, and explain how eating
patterns, not only what one eats, affect health, digestion, and wellbeing. They explain
how substances such as tobacco, alcohol, and drugs affect the body and brain and how to
make safe, informed choices, and build a personal self-care routine covering sleep,
hygiene, movement, and connection, and track how it affects wellbeing. Every body, every
pace, and every family's way of eating is honored; the body is taught with dignity,
never shame. The final third steps into the wider human story with the tools of the
historian and the ethicist. *Trade, Knowledge Systems & the Human Story* evaluates
competing explanations for one major historical change and supports a reasoned judgment
with evidence, and analyzes how a long-running human practice, such as trade, farming,
or writing, changed over centuries and what stayed the same. Learners compare two
knowledge traditions in depth and explain what each offers and where they agree and
differ, and analyze a case of cultural exchange and blending — foods, languages, or
beliefs meeting — and what new things grew from it. They analyze how long-distance
trade networks, such as the Silk Roads or Indian Ocean routes, connected peoples and
moved goods, ideas, and beliefs, and analyze how environmental change, such as drought,
flood, or a shift in climate, has driven people to move and adapt, in the past and
today. They analyze slavery and the transatlantic slave trade as systems built on the
denial of human dignity and describe how enslaved people resisted, and explain how
enslaved and colonized peoples kept their humanity, culture, and hope alive under
oppression, with specific examples. History is told as a global story from many
perspectives, never one people's triumph.

*Ethics, Justice & Civic Power* turns those questions inward to the self and the group.
Learners evaluate a real-world moral dilemma by weighing consequences, duties, and
fairness, and defend a reasoned position, and explain how values and moral codes differ
across cultures and argue for respectful engagement with those differences. They
compare different ideas of justice — equal treatment, need, and restitution — and apply
them to a real case, and analyze how power and access shape who gets their rights
protected and who does not. They analyze a social movement — its goals, strategies, and
effects — and evaluate what made it succeed or struggle, and explain how digital tools
change civic participation, weighing the risks and benefits of organizing online. They
evaluate a real conflict and weigh peaceful, restorative, and other responses,
defending a reasoned choice, and analyze how groups and societies have pursued peace
and reconciliation after conflict, using a historical example. Questions are held as
questions, never answered as dogma. *Media, Technology & Our Earth Home* closes the
outward year by gathering everything together. Learners analyze the benefits and harms
of a technology for different groups of people and weigh them, and explain how design
decisions involve trade-offs among cost, safety, access, and the environment, using a
real example. They analyze how a photo, video, or story can be framed or edited to
change its meaning and explain how to check it, and evaluate whether an image or video
might be altered or misleading, such as a deepfake, and describe one way to check. They
explain how an ecosystem responds to a disturbance, such as fire or flooding, and what
helps it recover — resilience — and compare two biomes or climate zones and explain how
the living things there are adapted to their conditions. They explain what
sustainability means — meeting today's needs without robbing the future — and apply it
to one resource, and analyze a community's practice for caring for land or water, from
any culture, and what makes it work. The year closes in *All of Me, All of Us*,
gathering every whole person, the caring community they have built, and the living
Earth — and looking ahead to Grade 9, where a coherent personal narrative, the ethics
of technology and the environment, and reasoning across multiple ethical frameworks
will carry the same questions into high school.

Throughout, the four pillars are interwoven. Intellectual awareness (P3) carries the
most weight — argument and rhetoric, function and equation and data reasoning, and the
systems-and-models thinking of science are the year's central work — but emotional and
social awareness (P1) grows markedly, for identity formation and peers now matter
greatly, and a known, cared-for, and capable body-and-self remains the ground all
formal learning stands on (P2). Contextual and ecological awareness (P4) keeps rising:
history moves toward competing explanations, knowledge traditions, cultural blending,
trade networks, environmental migration, and slavery and resistance; civics toward real
dilemmas, ideas of justice, social movements, and digital civic power; and media and
ecology toward framed and altered images, ecosystem resilience, biomes, sustainability,
and place-based stewardship. Abstract and hypothetical reasoning now work with growing
confidence, always with a concrete anchor, structure, and hope. No high-stakes tests
anywhere: a learner's growth is seen through argument and cited reading, function and
data work with real things, investigations that control variables and weigh evidence,
made and critiqued and designed works, and a portfolio of what they say, make, and do.
