# Lesson 5 — Power and Access: Whose Rights Are Protected?

## Summary

Learners **analyze how power and access shape whose rights are protected and whose are not**,
using a real case of a right declared on paper but unevenly reached in practice. They trace
**barriers and levers**, and see that a right is only real when a person can reach it.

## Objectives

- Analyze how power and access shape who gets their rights protected and who does not.
  _(D09.S2.08.02)_

## Connection

A law says every child has the right to go to school. But one child lives a two-hour walk from
the nearest school, another cannot afford the fees, another is told the school is not for
children like them. The *right* is the same for all three — but it is not *reached* by all three.
That gap between a right on paper and a right in a person's life is what power and access shape.
Today you learn to trace that gap, and to name what opens it and what closes it.

## Materials

- Rights-and-access case sheet
- Power-and-access chart

## Preparation

- Copy the case sheet and charts.
- Choose one concrete case a learner can reason about: a declared right (education, safe water,
  voting, health care, a fair hearing) that some people in a community can reach and others
  cannot. Keep it faithful and non-sensational; the point is analysis, not shock.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It delivers the second strand-2 goal. Two working
definitions anchor it: **power** is the ability to influence or decide — for oneself and for
others (S-275, Lukes); **access** is whether a person can actually reach a right, through cost,
distance, information, or rules. Hold these as *descriptions*, not verdicts (philosophy §5). The
egalitarianism lens is the spine: the lesson exists to notice **who is left out** and **why**,
not to blame individuals — barriers are often built into rules and structures, not into any one
person's intentions (this echoes the Grade-6 idea that systems can treat groups unfairly even
when no one intends harm). The ethics lens: naming an unfair gap is the first step toward closing
it, and the lesson ends with a **lever** — something that widens access — so learners leave with
agency, not despair (facilitation.md "holding difficult topics safely"). The global lens: the
same right is reached unevenly in every country; use examples from more than one region (the
right to vote granted at very different times — S-327; the right to safe water unevenly met —
S-065; the right to be heard — S-060, S-149). The community lens: access is built or blocked in
the places learners actually live. Keep the tone honest and calm: these are real and serious
facts, and the learner's job is to *see clearly and name one lever*, which is both true to the
world and age-appropriate.

## Procedure

1. **Meet the two ideas (8 min).** Two words today: **power** (the ability to influence or
   decide) and **access** (whether you can actually reach a right). Say each in your own words,
   then give one everyday example of each.
2. **Read the case (8 min).** Read the rights-and-access case: a right declared for everyone —
   education, water, voting, health care, or a fair hearing — that some people reach and others
   do not.
3. **Trace it (15 min).** On your chart, answer three questions: *Who reaches this right? Who
   does not? What stands in the way — cost, distance, information, or a rule?* Name the
   **barriers** one by one.
4. **Find the power (8 min).** For each barrier, ask: *who or what keeps this barrier in place,
   and who has the power to remove it?* Write one sentence naming the power behind the barrier.
5. **Name a lever (10 min).** A **lever** is something that widens access — a rule changed, a
   path built, information shared, a group organized. Write one lever for this case and say how
   it would help.
6. **Feedback and close (6 min).** Share your lever with a partner. Ask: *does it remove a real
   barrier, or only wish it away?* A right on paper is only real when a person can reach it —
   and access can be widened. Next: how social movements widen access together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort pre-made "barrier" and "lever" cards before writing your own.
- **Extension:** Connect the case's barrier to a similar barrier in another country, or design a
  second lever that reaches the most-left-out person.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one barrier and one lever, and link
  the barrier to a form of power rather than to an individual's fault?
- **Artifact:** The completed power-and-access chart and the lever, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a right people around you take for granted that is hard to reach for
someone else — and what would widen access. Bring one idea back.
