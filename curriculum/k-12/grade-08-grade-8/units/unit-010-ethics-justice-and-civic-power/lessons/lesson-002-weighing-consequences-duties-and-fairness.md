# Lesson 2 — Weighing Consequences, Duties, and Fairness

## Summary

Learners meet **three lenses** for weighing a moral dilemma — **consequences**, **duties**, and
**fairness** — study a **worked example**, and then weigh two more dilemmas themselves,
defending a **reasoned position** in writing and talk. The three lenses are tools for thinking,
not a machine that prints one "correct" answer.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a real-world moral dilemma by weighing consequences, duties, and fairness, and
  defend a reasoned position. _(D09.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

You find a wallet on the ground with money in it and a name inside. No one is watching. What do
you do? Almost everyone feels the pull of several answers at once — keep it (no one would know),
return it (it belongs to someone), give it to an adult (let someone else decide). That pull is
the heart of ethics: a real dilemma is not a choice between good and evil but between two or
more *good-seeming* things that cannot all be done. Today you learn three lenses for looking at
such a moment clearly before you decide.

## Materials

- Dilemma cards
- Three-lens chart

## Preparation

- Copy the dilemma cards and three-lens charts.
- Prepare three short dilemmas (see the worked example and practice list below). Choose ones a
  learner can recognize from ordinary life — school, family, neighborhood, public space — so no
  learner needs special knowledge to enter the conversation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It introduces the unit's first skill — **weighing a
dilemma with three lenses** — so it uses a **worked example and guided practice before
independent work** (S-011; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The three lenses map onto
long-standing ethical traditions: *consequences* (what happens to everyone affected —
consequentialism, S-386), *duties* (what I owe — deontology, S-387), and *fairness* (who gets
what and who is left out — distributive justice, S-388). Do **not** present these as rival
"teams" to pick from; present them as three questions to ask *together*, because most careful
judgments draw on all three. This honors philosophy §11 (inquiry, not creed) and philosophy §5
(distinguish evidence from values): the lenses describe *ways of reasoning*, not a ranking of
what is right. The egalitarianism lens lives inside the fairness question: always ask *who is
left out*. The ethics lens is the whole lesson. Keep the climate safe: no learner's answer is
judged; the standard is *does your reason hold up and do you hear other reasons*. A learner who
weighs only one lens is not wrong — invite them to try a second lens, never shame them
(mastery, not ranking).

## Procedure

1. **Meet the three lenses (10 min).** Look at the three-lens chart. Three questions to ask of
   any dilemma:
   - **Consequences** — what will happen to everyone affected, now and later?
   - **Duties** — what do I owe others — honesty, keeping promises, care?
   - **Fairness** — who gets what? is anyone left out or harmed?
2. **Worked example (12 min).** Watch and listen as we weigh a first dilemma together. *Dilemma:
   a friend asks you to lie to a teacher about where they were, to keep them out of trouble.*
   - **Consequences:** the lie might save your friend trouble now, but it could harm trust later,
     and hurt the person lied about.
   - **Duties:** you owe honesty to the teacher and care to your friend — two duties pull against
     each other.
   - **Fairness:** is it fair to the teacher, or to others who follow the rules, to be deceived?
   Then hear the **position**: "I would not lie, because honesty matters and the lie would
   quietly hurt trust — but I would stand with my friend and help them face it." Notice: a
   reasoned position *gives reasons*, and it can be challenged.
3. **Guided practice (13 min).** In pairs, weigh a second dilemma aloud: *you find money in the
   library with no name on it.* Fill the three columns together, then each say one position and
   one reason.
4. **Independent weigh (12 min).** Choose a third dilemma from the cards — *a scarce resource
   must be divided among people with unequal need*. Weigh all three lenses on your own chart,
   then write a four-sentence position: what you would do, and **one reason from each lens**.
5. **Feedback and revisit (5 min).** Trade charts with a partner. Ask: *does their position give
   a reason from each lens?* If a lens is missing, say so kindly, and they try again.
6. **Close (3 min).** The three lenses are not a tie-breaker that always agrees with itself —
   they are a way to *see more clearly*. Next: how values and moral codes differ across cultures.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("The consequence of this is…", "My duty here is…", "It
  is fair/unfair because…").
- **Extension:** Write the strongest counterargument to your own position, then answer it in one
  sentence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self/peer):** Does the learner's written position give a reason from consequences,
  duties, and fairness, and can they defend it when a peer questions it?
- **Artifact:** The completed three-lens chart and the four-sentence position, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home about a small dilemma you faced this week and weigh it out loud with the
three lenses. Ask them: which lens do they lean on first, and why?
