# Lesson 3 — Values and Moral Codes Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners **compare how values and moral codes differ across cultures**, using living examples —
hospitality, respect for elders, honesty, care for the group, and care for the land — and
practice **respectful engagement**: understanding is not the same as agreeing, and difference is
not a defect to be fixed.

## Objectives

- Explain how values and moral codes differ across cultures and argue for respectful engagement
  with those differences. _(D09.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

In some homes, you leave your shoes at the door; in others, you keep them on. In some places a
meal is shared from one plate; in others everyone has their own. In some communities elders are
greeted first and spoken to with special words; in others age is hardly marked. None of these is
"right" and the others "wrong" — they are different answers to the same deep question: *how do
we show respect, and what do we owe each other?* Today you learn to look at those differences
with curiosity and care instead of judgment.

## Materials

- Value cards
- Values comparison chart

## Preparation

- Copy the value cards and comparison charts.
- Prepare two short, respectful one-paragraph descriptions of two communities and the values
  they hold dear (for example, a hospitality-centered community and a land-stewardship-centered
  community). Use real, well-documented examples and avoid stereotypes (see Resources).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. Its job is the second strand-1 goal: **explain that
values and moral codes differ across cultures and argue for respectful engagement** — not to
rank cultures. Hold the line on the three commitments that make this safe and honest
(philosophy §4, §11, and facilitation.md "holding difficult topics safely"):
**(1) description before judgment** — learners describe what a community values *before* they
evaluate it; **(2) no ranking** — no culture's moral code is "better" or "more advanced" than
another, and the lesson says so plainly; **(3) understanding ≠ agreement** — respectful
engagement means listening and asking, not surrendering your own judgment; a learner may
disagree with a practice and still treat its people with respect. The global lens is the whole
lesson; use examples from many regions and from both cultural and faith traditions (Ubuntu in
Southern Africa, filial respect in East Asian traditions, land stewardship in many Indigenous
traditions, hospitality in Southwest Asian and Mediterranean traditions, *ahimsa* — do no harm —
in Buddhist and Jain traditions, and the Golden Rule, which recurs across many of the world's
religions and wisdom traditions). Hold these side by side as equals to be understood, never
ranked (philosophy §11). The egalitarianism lens: notice that *within* any culture people
disagree about values — no culture is one voice. The ethics lens: respect for persons across
difference is itself a value we practice, not preach. If a learner makes a sweeping or
dismissive claim about a people, name it gently and redirect to the specific value and its
reason. Cite the UNESCO Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (S-389) as a shared
international *commitment* (a value), not as a scientific fact. Keep the wonder box open.

## Procedure

1. **Notice differences near you (8 min).** With a partner, name one everyday rule or custom
   that differs between two families or places you know (shoes, food, greetings, music). What
   value might sit behind each one?
2. **Meet five values (10 min).** Look at the value cards: **hospitality**, **respect for
   elders**, **honesty**, **care for the group**, **care for the land**. For each, say one real
   example of a community that holds it dear. Then notice: the same value shows up in many
   cultures, but *which* values a community puts first differs.
3. **Compare two communities (15 min).** Read the two short descriptions. On your chart, note
   for each community: *what it values most, why, and what it teaches children*. Then write one
   place they **agree** and one place they **differ**.
4. **Practice respectful engagement (12 min).** In pairs, role-play a visit between a person
   from each community. Rules of the exchange: **listen first, ask before judging, and say back
   what you heard** before you respond. Understanding is not agreeing — but it must come first.
5. **Argue for it (7 min).** Write three sentences: why does respectful engagement with another
   culture's values matter? Use one concrete example from your comparison.
6. **Close (3 min).** Values differ; that is not a problem to solve but a fact of human life to
   meet with respect. Next: comparing ideas of justice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence frame ("They value ___ because ___; we agree on ___, and
  differ on ___.").
- **Extension:** Find a case where two values *within one community* pull in different directions
  (honesty vs. care for the group) and explain the tension.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Does the learner's comparison name a real value for each community, and
  does their argument for respectful engagement use a concrete example rather than a slogan?
- **Artifact:** The completed comparison chart and the three-sentence argument, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a custom from their family or community that a newcomer might
misunderstand, and the value behind it. What would help a newcomer meet it with respect?
