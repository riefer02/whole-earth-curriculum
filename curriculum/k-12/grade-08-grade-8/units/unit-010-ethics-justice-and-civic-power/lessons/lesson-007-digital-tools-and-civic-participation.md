# Lesson 7 — Digital Tools and Civic Participation: Risks and Benefits

## Summary

Learners **explain how digital tools change civic participation** — **reach, speed, and
coordination** on one side; **misinformation, surveillance, and harassment** on the other — and
**weigh the risks and benefits of organizing online**, ending with a reasoned judgment rather
than a simple verdict for or against.

## Objectives

- Explain how digital tools change civic participation, and weigh the risks and benefits of
  organizing online. _(D09.S3.08.02)_

## Connection

A phone in your pocket can reach a thousand people in a minute. Before phones, people organized
with drums, bells, posters, and word of mouth — slower, but harder to spy on and harder to
falsify. The phone changed what is possible: a march can be called in an hour, aid can be
coordinated across a city, a voice that was never heard can suddenly be heard everywhere. But
the same speed carries false stories, and the same reach can be watched. Today you weigh both
sides — not to decide that digital tools are good or bad, but to decide *how to use them well
when you act with others*.

## Materials

- Digital-participation case sheet
- Risk-benefit chart

## Preparation

- Copy the case sheet and risk-benefit charts.
- Choose one real, documented case of people organizing online — for example, the use of social
  media to coordinate protest or spread information during the 2010–11 Arab Spring uprisings
  (S-391), or a digitally coordinated relief effort. Prepare the facts plainly, including both a
  benefit and a harm, so the weighing is honest.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It delivers the second strand-3 goal, and it carries the
**technology lens** explicitly (philosophy §4). Teach the change as a *trade-off* to weigh, not
a hero/villain story: digital tools give **reach** (many people, cheaply), **speed** (instantly),
and **coordination** (many hands organized at once) — and they also enable **misinformation**
(false stories spread as fast as true ones), **surveillance** (the tools that connect can also
be watched), and **harassment** (a loud crowd can drown or threaten a single voice). The
egalitarianism lens: ask *who can use the tools and who cannot* — a digital divide means the
same tools widen some voices and silence others. The critical-thinking lens: the skill here is
the same source-checking habit from media literacy (S-063) — *check before you share*.
The ethics lens: organizing online is a moral act; the organizer's duty includes protecting the
people they organize with. The global lens: the Arab Spring is one well-documented case among
many — do not present it as the only story, and note that digital organizing happens (and is
blocked) on every continent (S-391). Keep the tone measured: these are real risks, but the
lesson ends with **safeguards** so learners leave with agency, not fear (facilitation.md). The
standard is a *reasoned judgment*, not a verdict that online organizing is always worth it or
never worth it.

## Procedure

1. **Feel the change (8 min).** In pairs, list three ways people organized *before* phones
   (drums, posters, word of mouth, letters) and three ways they organize *now*. What changed?
2. **Meet the two columns (8 min).** Read the risk-benefit chart. **Benefits:** reach, speed,
   coordination. **Risks:** misinformation, surveillance, harassment. Give one everyday example
   of each.
3. **Read the case (8 min).** Read the digital-participation case: people organizing online to
   demand change or coordinate help. Notice what the tools made possible *and* what went wrong
   or could go wrong.
4. **Weigh it (15 min).** Fill your chart: list the **benefits** and the **risks** you see in
   the case. Then write one sentence saying which side you think weighs heavier *in this case*,
   and why.
5. **Build a safeguard (10 min).** Choose one risk from your chart and write one **safeguard** —
   checking a source before sharing, protecting participants' names, agreeing on rules for
   speaking. How would it help?
6. **Feedback and close (6 min).** Share your safeguard with a partner. Ask: *does it address a
   real risk, or only a made-up one?* Digital tools change civic life — your job is to use them
   with your eyes open. Next: evaluating a real conflict.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort pre-made "benefit" and "risk" cards before writing your own.
- **Extension:** Design a second safeguard and connect it to a specific risk you named in the
  case.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the learner's chart name at least one benefit and one risk, and
  does their judgment give a reason rather than only a verdict?
- **Artifact:** The completed risk-benefit chart and the safeguard, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how people shared news and organized when they were young, and what they
think phones changed — for better and for worse. Compare their two sides with yours.
