# Lesson 4 — Three Ideas of Justice: Equal Treatment, Need, and Restitution

## Summary

Learners **compare three ideas of justice** — **equal treatment**, **need**, and
**restitution** — and apply each to one real case of sharing a scarce resource. They discover
that justice is more than one thing, and that careful answers often **blend the three**.

## Objectives

- Compare different ideas of justice (such as equal treatment, need, and restitution) and apply
  them to a real case. _(D09.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

A drought leaves a village with less water than everyone needs. Who should get it — everyone
the same share, those who are thirstiest or most at risk, or those whose crops were destroyed
and must be made whole again? Each answer feels fair in a different way. That is the discovery
behind today's lesson: **justice is not one rule but several ideas**, and they sometimes point
in different directions. You will learn to tell them apart and to use them on a real case.

## Materials

- Justice case sheet
- Three-ideas chart
- Counters (low-tech)

## Preparation

- Copy the case sheet and three-ideas charts.
- Choose one concrete, age-appropriate case a learner can reason about without specialized
  knowledge — for example, dividing scarce water, food, or medicine after a disaster, or
  repairing a harm done within a school. Prepare the facts plainly (who is affected, what is
  scarce, what was lost).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It opens strand 2. The three ideas are **distinct and
real**, so teach them explicitly with a worked example before independent application (S-011):
**equal treatment** (everyone the same — a core of formal equality), **need** (those in greater
need get more — the distributive idea), and **restitution** (those who were harmed are made
whole again — the repair idea; S-161, S-022) — an idea with deep roots in Indigenous peacemaking
circles and Ubuntu, not only in Western theory. These map onto long-standing debates in the theory
of justice (S-388). Hold them as *ideas to compare*, not a menu where one is "correct"
(philosophy §11). The egalitarianism lens is central: ask *who is left out by each idea* — equal
treatment can ignore unequal need; need can ignore past harm; restitution alone may not feed
anyone tomorrow. The ethics lens: choosing is itself a moral act, and the standard is a *reasoned
choice*, not a "right" answer. The critical-thinking lens: the same word "fair" hides three
different ideas — learning to name them is the skill. When learners disagree, that is the point:
have them name *which* idea of justice they are using, and the disagreement often becomes
clearer. Distinguish description from value: the three ideas describe *ways* of being just; the
learner's choice among them is a value judgment to be defended, not graded (philosophy §5).

## Procedure

1. **Meet the three ideas (10 min).** Read the three-ideas chart. Three ways to answer "what is
   just?" —
   - **Equal treatment:** everyone gets the same share.
   - **Need:** those who need more get more.
   - **Restitution:** those who were harmed are made whole again.
2. **Worked example (12 min).** Listen as we apply all three to one case: *a drought leaves a
   village with less water than everyone needs.*
   - **Equal treatment:** every household gets the same amount.
   - **Need:** the sick, the young, and those with the least get more.
   - **Restitution:** farmers whose crops died first get water to replant.
   Notice each is *fair in a different way* — and each leaves someone out.
3. **Apply to a new case (15 min).** Read the case sheet. On your chart, write what each idea of
   justice would do, and **who benefits and who is left out** under each.
4. **Defend a choice (12 min).** In pairs, tell each other which idea — or which **blend** of the
   three — you would choose, and why. A blend is allowed and often best: "equal shares as a
   floor, extra for the most vulnerable, and some set aside to repair the damage."
5. **Feedback (4 min).** Ask your partner: *did I name the idea I was using? did I notice who is
   left out?* If not, say so kindly and try again.
6. **Close (2 min).** Justice is more than one thing. Next: how power and access shape whose
   rights are actually protected.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Physically divide 20 counters three ways to *feel* equal treatment, need, and
  restitution before writing.
- **Extension:** Design a hybrid plan that uses all three ideas and justify the proportions you
  give to each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Can the learner name which idea (or blend) their plan uses, and can they
  say who benefits and who is left out?
- **Artifact:** The completed three-ideas chart and the defended choice, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how they would divide a scarce thing fairly — and which they would weigh
more, equal shares or greatest need. Do they also think about repairing past harm? Compare
answers.
