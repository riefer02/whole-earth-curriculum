# Lesson 6 — How a Social Movement Works: Goals, Strategies, Effects

## Summary

Learners **analyze how social movements work — goals, strategies, and effects** — using real
examples (the **Green Belt Movement**, the **Chipko movement**, the **Salt March**, and others),
and **evaluate what made each succeed or struggle**. They see that movements are ordinary people
acting together, judged by what they do and what they change.

## Objectives

- Analyze a social movement (its goals, strategies, and effects) and evaluate what made it
  succeed or struggle. _(D09.S3.08.01)_

## Connection

When the trees near a village were about to be cut down, the villagers — many of them women —
walked into the forest and wrapped their arms around the trunks. They did not fight; they
*hugged the trees* until the cutters stopped. That is the Chipko movement in India in the 1970s,
and it is a social movement in miniature: ordinary people, a clear goal, a clever strategy, and
a lasting effect. Today you learn to take a movement apart — its **goals**, **strategies**, and
**effects** — so you can judge for yourself what makes one work.

## Materials

- Movement case cards
- Movement analysis chart

## Preparation

- Copy the movement case cards and analysis charts.
- Prepare two or three short, accurate profiles (see Resources for sources). Use movements from
  different regions and different aims, so the lesson is genuinely global — for example, the
  Green Belt Movement (Kenya, tree-planting and land; S-059), the Chipko movement (India, forest
  protection; S-328), the Salt March (India, nonviolent resistance to an unjust tax; S-390),
  and, as further options, the 1952 Defiance Campaign in South Africa (S-146) and the peaceful
  resistance of Parihaka (S-144).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It opens strand 3. Teach the **three-part frame** —
*goals* (what is unfair and what would be fair), *strategies* (how people act: organize,
petition, protest, build allies), *effects* (what changed, who benefited, who was left out) —
with a worked example first, then let learners apply it (S-011). The egalitarianism lens is
central: a movement is *ordinary people* acting together, often those with the least power, and
the evaluation always asks **who benefited and who was left out**. The global lens: use movements
from several continents and treat none as the "default" story; resistance to injustice is a
human pattern, not one nation's export (philosophy §4). The community lens: connect to a change
learners have seen or could make in their own school or neighborhood. The ethics lens:
strategies are *chosen*, and learners weigh them — nonviolence, disruption, and their costs —
as questions, not as a script (philosophy §11). Keep the history faithful and dated; do not
romanticize any movement or flatten its struggles (a movement can partly succeed and partly
fail). The standard is a *reasoned evaluation*, not agreement with the movement's aims. Be ready
for the fact that some movements used force or were met with force; name it honestly and hold
it as one more thing to weigh, without glorifying violence (facilitation.md).

## Procedure

1. **Meet the frame (8 min).** Three questions to ask of any movement: **What is its goal?**
   (what is unfair, and what would be fair) **What are its strategies?** (how people act
   together) **What were its effects?** (what changed, who benefited, who was left out).
2. **Worked example (12 min).** Listen as we analyze the **Chipko movement** together. *Goal:*
   protect the forests villages depend on. *Strategies:* villagers — especially women — embraced
   the trees, nonviolently, to stop the cutting. *Effects:* tree-cutting was halted in places;
   the idea spread and helped inspire later forest protection. Then evaluate: *it succeeded
   because its strategy was peaceful, visible, and rooted in the people most affected.*
3. **Analyze a second movement (15 min).** Read a movement profile — the Green Belt Movement or
   the Salt March. On your chart, record its **goals**, **strategies**, and **effects** in your
   own words.
4. **Evaluate (10 min).** Write three sentences: did it succeed or struggle, and why? Name at
   least one thing that helped it and one thing that made it hard.
5. **Peer feedback (7 min).** Trade charts. Ask: *did they name a goal, a strategy, and an
   effect — and give a reason for their evaluation?* Offer one kind suggestion.
6. **Close (3 min).** Movements are ordinary people acting together over time. Next: how digital
   tools change civic participation.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sequence a movement's events on a drawn timeline before filling the chart.
- **Extension:** Compare two movements and name one thing one did that the other could not, and
  why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Does the learner's chart name a goal, a strategy, and an effect, and
  does their evaluation give a reason rather than only a verdict?
- **Artifact:** The completed movement analysis chart and evaluation, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a change they have seen people work for together — in their
neighborhood, workplace, or country. What was the goal, how did people act, and what changed?
