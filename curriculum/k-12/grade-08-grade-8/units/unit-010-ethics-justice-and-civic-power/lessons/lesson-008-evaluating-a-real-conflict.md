# Lesson 8 — Evaluating a Real Conflict: Peaceful, Restorative, and Other Responses

## Summary

Learners **evaluate a real conflict** and weigh **peaceful, restorative, and other responses** —
talking, listening, and repairing harm; avoidance; and escalation — tracing each response's
likely outcome before **defending a reasoned choice**. They learn that conflict is not the
enemy; unaddressed harm is.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a real conflict and weigh peaceful, restorative, and other responses, defending a
  reasoned choice. _(D09.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

Two communities share one river. The upstream community takes more water for its fields; the
downstream community watches its crops dry. Both are angry, and both have a point. What should
happen next — talk it out, walk away and endure, or take the water by force? That is a real,
recurring human conflict, and the choices in it are the same choices in a schoolyard fight or a
family quarrel. Today you learn to *evaluate* such a moment: to lay out the possible responses,
trace where each one leads, and defend a choice with reasons.

## Materials

- Conflict case sheet
- Responses chart

## Preparation

- Copy the case sheet and responses charts.
- Choose one real, concrete conflict a learner can reason about: communities sharing a river,
  neighbors disputing a boundary, or a school conflict over exclusion. Present both sides'
  needs fairly; do not stack the case so one side is obviously right.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner. It opens strand 4. The three response families are
**talk/listen/repair** (restorative — S-022, S-161), **avoidance** (peace but no repair), and
**escalation** (force that deepens harm). Teach them as *options to weigh*, not as a moral
ladder with one obviously right rung (philosophy §11): sometimes walking away keeps people safe
in the moment even though it leaves harm unaddressed, and sometimes force is what people resort
to when no one will listen — the lesson's job is to *trace the outcomes honestly*, then let the
learner choose and defend. The social-emotional lens: feelings — anger, fear, hurt — are part of
conflict, and naming them before choosing is part of good judgment (S-001). The community lens:
conflicts live in real places and relationships, not in the abstract. The egalitarianism lens:
ask *whose voice is heard and whose is not* in each response — restorative practice only works
if the less powerful are genuinely heard. The ethics lens: the standard is a *reasoned choice*,
not a "correct" answer; the deeper aim is that harm is named and repaired, not that someone
"wins." Keep the topic safe: use a case, not a real conflict among the learners themselves, and
remind them that naming a response is not the same as acting on it (facilitation.md "holding
difficult topics safely"). Distinguish description from value: *what each response does* is a
fact to trace; *which is most just* is the learner's judgment to defend (philosophy §5).

## Procedure

1. **Meet the responses (8 min).** Three ways people answer a conflict: **talk, listen, and
   repair the harm**; **walk away** (peace, but the harm stays); **escalate with force** (the
   harm deepens). Give one everyday example of each.
2. **Read the conflict (8 min).** Read the conflict case — two sides, both hurt and angry, each
   with a real need. Name each side's need in one sentence.
3. **Trace the outcomes (15 min).** On your chart, write what would likely happen under each
   response: *talk/listen/repair*, *walk away*, *escalate*. For each, ask: *is the harm
   addressed? is anyone's need met? does trust grow or shrink?*
4. **Weigh and choose (12 min).** Which response — or blend — would you choose, and why? Write a
   four-sentence position that names the response, the outcome you expect, and one reason that
   matters to you.
5. **Peer feedback (8 min).** Trade positions. Ask: *did they trace an outcome, not just name a
   response? did they hear both sides' needs?* Offer one kind suggestion.
6. **Close (4 min).** Conflict is not the enemy; unaddressed harm is. Next: how whole societies
   pursue peace and reconciliation after conflict.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Act out two responses and their outcomes before writing; use sentence starters
  ("If we choose ___, then ___").
- **Extension:** Identify the *need behind each side's position* and design a response that
  meets both needs at once.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Does the learner's position trace a real outcome for the chosen response
  and acknowledge both sides' needs?
- **Artifact:** The completed responses chart and the defended position, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home about a small conflict you saw or heard about this week. What response was
chosen, and what happened? Would a different response have repaired more?
