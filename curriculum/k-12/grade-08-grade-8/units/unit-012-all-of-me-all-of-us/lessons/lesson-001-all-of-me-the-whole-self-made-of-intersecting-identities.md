# Lesson 1 — All of Me: The Whole Self Made of Intersecting Identities

## Summary

Learners recall the Grade-8 idea that identity is made of many parts, and open the year's closing
capstone by mapping their own **intersecting identities** — culture, gender, ability, language, and
one of their choosing — into a **whole self** they are still becoming. This is the first half of the
unit's first essential question.

## Objectives

- Explain how multiple and intersecting identities — such as culture, gender, ability, and language —
  shape a whole self. _(D01.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

Think of a moment when you were more than one thing at once: a child *and* a teammate, a person who
speaks one language at home and another at school, someone who loves music *and* worries about an
exam. No single label can hold all of you. Your identity is not one box you tick; it is the place
where many parts of you meet and shape one whole person. Today you look at that meeting place — and
begin to say who you are becoming.

## Materials

- Whole-self map sheet
- Recall cards

## Preparation

- Copy the whole-self map sheet and recall cards.
- Retrieval: from Unit 1 (Feelings, Identity & Purpose), recall (a) identity is made of many parts,
  (b) identities intersect rather than sit side by side, and (c) inner worth is different from
  outside approval. Today we rebuild that idea as a map of a whole self.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the **opening and retrieval pass** of the
capstone, so the goals are modest: **(1) recall that identity is many parts that intersect; (2) name
the four intersecting identities the standard names — culture, gender, ability, and language — plus
one of the learner's own choosing; (3) map them into one whole self.** Do not push for a finished
autobiography today; the reflection deepens across the unit and is gathered in Lesson 5. The
**critical-thinking** lens: "which parts of me are mine, and which are labels others stuck on me?" is
the habit carried forward (S-334). The **egalitarian** lens: no identity ranks above another, and a
person is never *just* one label — intersectionality names how overlapping identities shape
experience *together*, not separately (S-334). The **global** lens: what counts as a core identity —
clan, faith, nation, craft — differs across cultures; let learners name their own. The **technology**
lens: online platforms often reduce a whole person to a few checkable labels; today we push back by
mapping the whole. Identity is offered as a *growing story*, never a finished verdict (S-155); and
"who I am becoming" names the *possible selves* a person imagines hoping to become and fearing to
become — imagined possibilities, not a fixed verdict (S-399). Spacing note (S-039): this recall pass
is what makes the capstone stick.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (8 min).** In a small group, review three recall cards — **identity has many parts**,
   **identities intersect**, **inner worth vs. outside approval** — reading them together aloud (or
   having them read to you). For each, say in one sentence what you remember from Unit 1.
2. **Meet the four (7 min).** Four identities the whole year has circled back to: **culture**
   (where and how you grew up), **gender** (how you experience and express being you), **ability**
   (what your body and mind can do, and how the world meets it), **language** (the words you think,
   speak, and dream in). Say each in your own words.
3. **Map your whole self (15 min).** On the whole-self map, write *you* in the center. In each of
   the five spaces — culture, gender, ability, language, and one identity you choose — write one
   short, honest note about that part of you. (No writing needed? Say each part aloud and have a
   partner or facilitator write it, or build the map from found objects — a stone for each identity,
   placed to touch in the middle.)
4. **Where two meet (12 min).** Pick two of your identities and write one sentence about how they
   combine *in you*. Example: "My love of my home language and my love of science meet when I
   explain a discovery to my grandmother." (Worked example — change the details to your own life.)
5. **Share one thread (8 min).** With a partner, share one identity and one place where two of yours
   meet. Then listen to your partner's. Notice: every person in the room is a whole made of many
   parts.
6. **Close (5 min).** Today you began the answer to "who am I becoming?" Next: we look at what you
   will *choose* rather than *react* to.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give sentence starters ("The part of me that ___ meets the part that ___ when…").
  Choose two identities instead of five.
- **Extension:** Write how *three* identities intersect in one real moment of your life.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the four intersecting identities and give one
  example of two of them meeting in their own life?
- **Artifact:** The completed whole-self map, kept in the portfolio and returned to in Lesson 5.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What are two or three parts of who you are, and where do they meet?" Bring one
sentence of their answer (no private details needed) to the next lesson.

## Resources

- On intersectionality as overlapping identities shaping experience together, not separately:
  Crenshaw (1989), "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (S-334).
- On identity as a lifelong, developing story rather than a finished fact: Erikson, *Childhood and
  Society* (S-155).
- On "who I am becoming" as mapping imagined possible selves, not a fixed verdict: Markus & Nurius
  (1986), "Possible Selves" (S-399).
- On the payoff of spaced retrieval: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
- On explicit modeling before independent work: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011).
