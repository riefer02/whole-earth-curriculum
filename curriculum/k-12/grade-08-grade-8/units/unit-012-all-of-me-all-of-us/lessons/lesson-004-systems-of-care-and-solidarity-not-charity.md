# Lesson 4 — Systems of Care and Solidarity (Not Charity)

## Summary

Learners analyze how a **system of care** works — who gives, who receives, who decides — and what
makes it fair or unfair, then **distinguish charity from mutual aid and solidarity**, choosing to
stand *with* others rather than only give from above.

## Objectives

- Analyze how systems of care and mutual aid operate, and what makes them fair or unfair.
  _(D02.S4.08.01)_
- Distinguish charity from mutual aid and solidarity, and explain the difference. _(D02.S4.08.02)_

## Connection

When someone is hungry, one neighbor might hand them a meal — a one-way gift. Or neighbors might
share what they grow, cook together, and decide *together* how everyone gets fed. The food may be
the same; the difference is the **power** and the **dignity**. In charity, one person gives and the
other only receives; in solidarity, people meet one another's needs as equals, sharing the giving
*and* the deciding. Today you learn to tell them apart — and to notice which one a real community is
actually practicing.

## Materials

- Care-system analysis sheet
- Care case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the analysis sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: from Unit 3, recall the idea of mutual aid and the charity-versus-solidarity
  distinction. Today we make that distinction precise and apply it to real systems of care.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The goal: **each learner analyzes one real system of
care with three questions — who gives, who receives, who decides — and can explain the difference
between charity and mutual aid/solidarity.** Hold the distinction carefully and non-morally: charity
is not "bad," and a hungry person fed by a gift is still fed; the *point* is that sharing power is
different from sharing only goods (S-345). The **egalitarian** lens is the heart here: the difference
is who holds power — in charity the giver keeps the power and the receiver stays passive and often
must prove need; in mutual aid and solidarity people decide together as equals (S-345). The **global**
lens: mutual aid is a widespread, documented human practice — harambee in Kenya, gotong royong in
Indonesia, mink'a/minga in the Andes, barn raising in North America — each a named tradition in one
place, not a universal claim about "all people" (S-164); the term itself was popularized by Kropotkin
(S-163). The **ethics** lens: what does a system of care do to a person's dignity — does it lift
people or keep them dependent? The **technology** lens: digital tools can organize mutual aid fast
(neighbors coordinating help in a crisis) *and* can be used to surveil who "deserves" help. The
**environment** lens: many systems of care are also systems of *land* care — a shared garden, a
managed watershed, a disaster kitchen — caring for people and place at once. Keep it concrete and
hopeful: learners leave with the tools to *notice* power, not with despair about it.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (6 min).** What did you plan last time — and what is the word for people helping one
   another as equals, not from above? (Mutual aid / solidarity.) Say what you remember about the
   difference between a gift and standing together.
2. **Name the three questions (8 min).** Every system of care answers three questions, whether it
   says so or not: **Who gives? Who receives? Who decides?** Write them where everyone can see. These
   three questions are your analysis tool.
3. **Worked example (8 min).** Analyze one case together. A **food bank**: who gives (donors), who
   receives (people who need food, often after proving need), who decides (the people who run it, not
   the people who use it). Is it fair? It meets a real need — *and* the receivers hold little power.
   Now a **community garden**: who gives (everyone who plants and waters), who receives (everyone who
   eats), who decides (everyone, together). Notice the difference.
4. **Analyze one case (12 min).** In pairs, take one care case card (or hear it read aloud) — some
   come from named traditions in one place each (harambee in Kenya, gotong royong in Indonesia,
   mink'a/minga in the Andes, barn raising in North America). Run the three questions, then decide:
   *what makes this system fair, and what makes it unfair?* Write one sentence for each.
5. **Charity, mutual aid, solidarity (12 min).** Sort what you found. **Charity** = one-way, the
   giver keeps the power. **Mutual aid** = needs met together, as equals. **Solidarity** = standing
   *with* others over time, sharing the risk and the deciding. Which one does your case most look
   like — and what would shift it toward solidarity?
6. **Peer check (5 min).** Trade analyses. Give one piece of evidence for your reading and ask one
   question of your partner's.
7. **Close (4 min).** You now have a tool for noticing who holds the power in every act of care. Next
   and last: we gather the whole year — every whole person, the caring community, and the living
   Earth — and look ahead.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Analyze only the worked example, using the three questions with a partner; write one
  sentence for each question.
- **Non-visual / tactile path:** Act the difference out — one person stands above and hands down
  (charity, one-way), then everyone stands in a circle passing an object two ways while deciding
  together (solidarity). Say the three questions aloud instead of writing them.
- **Extension:** Compare two systems of care and argue, with evidence, which is fairer and why — then
  name one way the less-fair one could shift toward solidarity.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self/peer):** Can the learner answer all three questions for one system and explain,
  in one sentence, how solidarity differs from charity?
- **Artifact:** The care-system analysis sheet, kept in the portfolio as the unit's
  belonging-and-solidarity reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time neighbors helped one another as equals — who gave, who received,
and who decided? Bring one sentence of their answer to the final lesson.
