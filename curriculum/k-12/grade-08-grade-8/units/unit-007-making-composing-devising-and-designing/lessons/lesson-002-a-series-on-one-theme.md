# Lesson 2 — A Series on One Theme: The Idea That Changes

## Summary

Learners choose one theme and make a **series** of three or four artworks exploring it, deliberately
letting the work change across the series, then reflect in writing on what changed and why. They meet
the example of artists who work in series — Jacob Lawrence's _Migration Series_ and Hokusai's
_Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji_.

## Objectives

- Create a series of artworks exploring one theme or idea. _(D07.S1.08.01)_
- Reflect on how the work changes across the series. _(D07.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever drawn the same thing twice and noticed the second one came out differently — bolder,
simpler, or more sure? That is not a mistake; that is a **series** trying to happen. An artist who
returns to one theme — the same mountain, the same face, the same feeling — is not repeating; they are
digging deeper. Each new work changes the idea a little, and the *change* is the meaning. Today you do
this on purpose.

## Materials

- Theme brainstorm sheet
- Series planner

## Preparation

- Copy the theme brainstorm sheet and series planner.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall creating an artwork that communicates a message and revising it after
  feedback — today we stretch that one work into a series.
- Optional: print or project the unit map graphic as a reminder of where this lesson sits.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a series is several works on one
theme in which the *change* is the point — the learner plans three or four works, makes them, and then
reflects in writing on what changed (line? color? scale? feeling?) and why.** This is a *making* skill,
so model the pattern first, then let learners work independently (explicit instruction carries novice
skills — S-011): show a quick three-frame example of one simple subject (a tree, a cup, a face) getting
bolder across three tries, then invite learners to choose their own theme. The global lens: series are
a worldwide practice — Jacob Lawrence's 60-panel _Migration Series_ (1940–41) told the story of the
Great Migration of African Americans from the rural South to the urban North (S-363), and the Japanese
printmaker Katsushika Hokusai made _Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji_ (c. 1830–32), a series on one
mountain seen many ways (S-364). Name these as *examples to honor with questions*, not specimens. The
egalitarian lens: a series needs only paper and a mark-making tool; no expensive supplies are required.
The intellectual lens: reflection — noticing *what changed and why* — is where the learning lives. The
ethics lens: there is no single "right" way for the series to change; each learner's change is their
own, and all are valid. Keep assessment mastery-based: a series that is not finished is "not yet,"
never a failure.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: what did you make to carry a message, and how did you change it
   after feedback? Today we stretch one work into a **series**.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** A **theme** is one big idea; a **series** is several works on that theme
   in which the work changes. Look at two examples: Jacob Lawrence painted 60 panels about one story —
   migration (S-363); Hokusai made 36 views of one mountain (S-364). What changed across each artist's
   series?
3. **Choose a theme (7 min).** On the brainstorm sheet, list three themes that matter to you — a place,
   a feeling, a person, a season, a question. Circle the one you want to explore.
4. **Plan the series (10 min).** On the series planner, sketch a plan for three or four works. Decide
   one thing you will *change* across the series: line, color, scale, viewpoint, or feeling.
5. **Make the series (20 min).** Make each work in turn. Let the change happen; don't force it to stay
   the same. If a work surprises you, follow it.
6. **Reflect (5 min).** Write two or three sentences: what changed across your series, and why do you
   think it changed? Which work do you understand best now?
7. **Close (5 min).** Share one work with a partner and say what changed. The change is the meaning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Make a series of two works and use a sentence starter ("From the first to the second,
  my ___ got ___ because…").
- **Extension:** Deliberately change one *element* (line, shape, color) across all four works and write
  why each choice carries the theme further.
- **Access (non-visual):** Make the series as textures and shapes instead of images — clay, string, or
  folded paper — so each change can be felt by hand as well as seen; reflect on how the *feel* changed,
  not only the look.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner make three or four works on one theme and name what
  changed?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed series (or photographs of it) plus the written reflection,
  kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, find one subject you see every day — a door, a plant, a window — and sketch it twice, a day
apart. Notice what changed between the two. Bring your two sketches to the next lesson.
