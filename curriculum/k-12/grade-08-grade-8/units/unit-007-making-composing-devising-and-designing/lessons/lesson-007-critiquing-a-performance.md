# Lesson 7 — Critiquing a Performance: Movement, Expression, Context, Purpose

## Summary

Learners critique a performance with **evidence**, using four lenses — **movement, expression,
context, and purpose** — and separating observation from judgment. They watch performances from
different traditions, treating each as a living practice to honor with questions.

## Objectives

- Critique a performance using evidence about its movement, expression, context, and purpose.
  _(D07.S3.08.02)_
- Separate observation from judgment. _(D07.S3.08.02)_

## Connection

You already "read" performances every day: you can tell a lot from how someone stands, how fast they
move, what their face and hands are saying — before anyone speaks a word. Gesture, posture, and spacing
carry meaning without words (a skill you met in Grade 7). A **critique** just makes that reading
deliberate: *what did the body do? what did it express? where and why was it performed?* When you can
name those things, you stop being carried along by a performance and start understanding *how* it
carried you.

## Materials

- Performance notice sheet
- Two performances to critique

## Preparation

- Copy the performance notice sheet; choose and prepare two short performances (live or recorded) from
  different traditions.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how gesture, posture, and spacing communicate meaning without words —
  today we turn that into a full, evidence-based critique.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a performance critique names what
the body did — movement (levels, speed, space, timing), expression (face, force, feeling), context
(where, when, what tradition), and purpose (what it means to say) — and separates observation from
judgment, with judgment citing evidence.** This is a *skill*, so **model the critique once on a first
performance, then let learners practice in pairs** (explicit instruction carries novice skills — S-011).
The critical-thinking lens: "separate what you saw from what you felt about it" is the evidence habit
applied to the moving body. The ethics lens: critique describes the work, never the performer — and
never a performer's body, identity, or ability — and it can disagree without disrespect. The egalitarian lens: every body and every ability is honored — a
performance can be large or small, seated or standing, and stillness is movement too; no learner's
evidence-based judgment is "better." The global lens: the same four lenses help you watch across
traditions, but *context matters* — a gesture or a dance means something inside its own tradition, so
honor that context rather than ranking it by one tradition's rules (S-056). Keep the tone generative:
critique deepens watching, it does not shut it down.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how do gesture, posture, and spacing carry meaning without words?
   Today we turn that into **critique**.
2. **Meet the four lenses (10 min).** Look at the performance notice sheet. Four lenses: **movement**
   (levels, speed, space, timing), **expression** (face, force, feeling), **context** (where, when,
   what tradition), **purpose** (what it means to say). These are the words that make a critique
   precise.
3. **Modeled critique (10 min).** Watch the first performance twice. Together, name what you saw (its
   movement and expression), state its context, and describe its purpose — *with a reason drawn from
   what you observed*.
4. **Practice in pairs (20 min).** Watch a second performance from a different tradition. Fill in the
   four lenses. Rule: every judgment sentence must name something you saw.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap sheets. Check: is each judgment backed by evidence? Is observation
   separate from judgment? Fix what is missing.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one evidence-based sentence: "I saw ___, so I think it means ___." That is
   a critique.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one thing you saw move and one feeling it gave you, with a sentence starter ("The
  ___ moved ___, so I felt ___").
- **Extension:** Compare how two traditions use movement or drama to express one shared theme, and
  write one reason for the difference.
- **Access (non-visual):** A performance can be observed by listening — footfalls, breath, voice, music
  — and rhythm, spacing, and energy can be felt or heard, not only seen. A partner's spoken description
  ("she rises from a chair, then moves quickly left…") is a full path into all four lenses.
- **Access (performance anxiety):** When a classmate's devised piece is the subject, performing it for
  critique is voluntary and needs the performer's consent. A learner may decline and instead have their
  rehearsal reflection critiqued, or critique only the outside performances.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a movement or expression observed and connect a
  judgment to it?
- **Peer/portfolio artifact:** The completed performance notice sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, watch a short performance (a dance, a game, a scene) and write one evidence-based sentence: "I
saw ___, so I think it means ___." Bring it to the next lesson.
