# Lesson 3 — Critiquing an Artwork: Evidence, Not Mere Taste

## Summary

Learners learn to critique an artwork with **evidence**, not mere taste, by working a four-step frame —
**describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate** — that separates **observation** (what is actually there)
from **judgment** (what it means and whether it works). They practice on artworks from different
cultures, treating each as a living tradition to honor with questions.

## Objectives

- Critique an artwork using evidence about its elements, context, and purpose. _(D07.S1.08.02)_
- Distinguish observation from judgment. _(D07.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

Two people can look at the same picture and disagree — one says "I love it," the other says "I don't
get it." Neither has said much yet. A **critique** goes further: it names *what is actually there*
(the shapes, the colors, the way the space is arranged) and *why it produces the effect it does*. The
difference between "I like it" and "this feels calm because the lines are long and horizontal" is the
difference between mere taste and evidence. That skill serves you everywhere — in choosing what to
trust, what to buy, and what to make.

## Materials

- Critique frame sheet
- Two artworks to critique

## Preparation

- Copy the critique frame sheet; choose and print or project two artworks from different cultures.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how perspective, framing, and emphasis shape what a viewer notices —
  today we turn that noticing into a full, evidence-based critique.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a critique separates *observation*
from *judgment* and requires judgment to cite evidence.** Use the four-step frame — **describe** (name
the elements you actually see: line, shape, color, value, texture, space), **analyze** (say how those
elements are arranged and relate), **interpret** (say what the work seems to mean or be for — its
context and purpose), **evaluate** (say whether and why it works, citing the evidence you gathered).
The elements of art and principles of design are *one* tradition's vocabulary for the first two steps (S-365) — other traditions describe their arts in their own words — but naming what is there before judging is a move shared across cultures.
This is a *skill*, so **model the frame once on a projected artwork, then let learners practice in
pairs** (explicit instruction carries novice skills — S-011). The critical-thinking lens: "cite the
evidence" is the whole point — a judgment without evidence is just a preference. The ethics lens:
critique is honest and kind; it describes the work, never the maker, and it can disagree respectfully —
and when a classmate's work is the subject, it is critiqued only with their consent, saying what is true
and kind.
The egalitarian lens: every learner's judgment is valid *if* it is supported; no learner's taste is
"better." The global lens: critique works the same way across cultures — name what is there, honor its
context, then judge. When critiquing the art of another culture, ask respectful questions about its
purpose rather than ranking it by one tradition's rules (S-056). Keep the tone generative: critique
builds, it does not tear down.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how does framing or emphasis change what a viewer notices? Today
   we turn noticing into **critique**.
2. **Meet the four steps (10 min).** A critique has four moves: **describe** (what do I see?),
   **analyze** (how are the elements arranged?), **interpret** (what does it mean or what is it for?),
   **evaluate** (does it work, and why?). The first two are **observation**; the last two are
   **judgment** — and judgment must cite evidence from the first two. Trace the frame on the sheet.
3. **Modeled critique (10 min).** Together, critique one artwork aloud: describe its line, shape,
   color, and space; analyze how they are arranged; interpret its context and purpose; then evaluate it
   *with a reason drawn from what you saw*.
4. **Practice in pairs (20 min).** With a partner, critique a second artwork from a different culture.
   Fill in each of the four steps. Rule: every judgment sentence must name at least one observation.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap sheets. Check: is each judgment backed by evidence? Is observation
   kept separate from judgment? Fix what is missing.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one evidence-based sentence. "I see…, so I think…" is a critique; "I just
   like it" is a start.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Complete only describe and analyze, with a sentence starter for judgment ("Because I see
  ___, I think the work feels ___").
- **Extension:** Critique the same artwork twice — once with evidence, once with only taste — and write
  two sentences on which critique is more useful and why.
- **Access (non-visual):** The subject can be a tactile work explored by hand, or an artwork described
  aloud by a partner ("top left, a large red circle; below it, three long curved lines…"). Because
  "describe" is already spoken and written, a learner who does not see works the same four steps through
  touch or listening.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name an element and connect a judgment to that
  observation?
- **Peer/portfolio artifact:** The completed critique frame sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, choose any image — a label, a poster, a photo, a painted wall — and write one evidence-based
sentence about it: "I see ___, so I think ___." Bring it to the next lesson.
