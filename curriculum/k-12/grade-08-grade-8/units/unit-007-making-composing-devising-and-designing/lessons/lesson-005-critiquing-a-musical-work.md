# Lesson 5 — Critiquing a Musical Work: Elements, Context, Effect

## Summary

Learners critique a musical work with **evidence**, using the eight **elements of music** — melody,
rhythm, harmony, timbre, dynamics, tempo, texture, and form — plus **context** and **effect**, and
separating observation from judgment. They listen to works from different traditions, treating each as
a living practice to honor with questions.

## Objectives

- Critique a musical work using evidence about its elements, context, and effect. _(D07.S2.08.02)_
- Separate observation from judgment. _(D07.S2.08.02)_

## Connection

You have said "this song makes me happy" or "that music is sad." That is a start — but *why* does it
feel that way? Is it the fast **tempo**, the high **melody**, the loud **dynamics**, the sound of a
particular voice or instrument (**timbre**)? Music does not move you by magic; it moves you through
specific, nameable choices. When you learn to name them, you stop being someone music *happens to* and
become someone who can *hear how* it works — and that skill deepens every song you love.

## Materials

- Elements of music card
- Two musical works to critique

## Preparation

- Copy the elements of music card; choose and prepare two short recorded works (or plan two live
  performances) from different traditions.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how sound is used in media to shape mood and meaning — today we turn
  that into a full, evidence-based critique.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a musical critique names the
elements heard — melody (the tune), rhythm (beats and rests), harmony (notes sounding together),
timbre (tone color), dynamics (loud and soft), tempo (fast and slow), texture (how parts layer), and
form (the shape) — then states the work's context and its effect, and separates observation from
judgment, with judgment citing evidence.** The eight elements are standard music-education vocabulary
(S-368). This is a *skill*, so **model the critique once on a first work, then let learners practice in
pairs** (explicit instruction carries novice skills — S-011). The critical-thinking lens: "name what
you hear, then say what effect it has" is the evidence habit applied to sound. The ethics lens: a
critique describes the work, never the musicians, and can disagree without disrespect — and when a
classmate's piece is the subject, it is critiqued only with their consent, saying what is true and kind. The egalitarian
lens: every listener's evidence-based judgment is valid; no one's taste is "better." Music is felt and
read as well as heard — vibration, a score, or a drawn shape are full ways in for Deaf and
hard-of-hearing learners, and the same elements apply. The global lens:
the same eight elements help you listen across traditions, but *context matters* — a rhythm or scale
means something inside its own tradition, so honor the work's context rather than ranking it by one
tradition's rules (S-056). Keep the tone generative: critique deepens listening, it does not shut it
down.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how does sound shape mood and meaning in a film or ad? Today we
   turn that into **critique**.
2. **Meet the elements (10 min).** Look at the elements of music card. Eight elements: **melody,
   rhythm, harmony, timbre, dynamics, tempo, texture, form**. Say each with its one-word reminder.
   These are the words that make a critique precise.
3. **Modeled critique (10 min).** Listen to the first work twice. Together, name what you hear (its
   melody, rhythm, tempo, timbre), state its context (where and when it comes from), and describe its
   effect — *with a reason drawn from the elements*.
4. **Practice in pairs (20 min).** Listen to a second work from a different tradition. Fill in the
   critique frame: elements, context, effect. Rule: every judgment sentence must name an element you
   heard.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap frames. Check: is each judgment backed by evidence? Is observation
   separate from judgment? Fix what is missing.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one evidence-based sentence: "I heard ___, so it feels ___." That is a
   critique.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name two elements you hear and use a sentence starter ("The ___ is ___, so the music
  feels ___").
- **Extension:** Compare two works on one shared theme (celebration, grief) and write how each uses the
  elements differently to create its effect.
- **Access (non-auditory):** The same elements can be read by other senses — tempo and rhythm from a
  score, a drawn waveform, or felt vibrations; dynamics from how strong the vibration is; form from the
  piece's written or drawn shape. A critique can cite what is felt or read, not only what is heard.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name an element heard and connect a judgment to it?
- **Peer/portfolio artifact:** The completed critique frame, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, listen to any song and write one evidence-based sentence: "I hear ___, so I feel ___." Bring
it to the next lesson.
