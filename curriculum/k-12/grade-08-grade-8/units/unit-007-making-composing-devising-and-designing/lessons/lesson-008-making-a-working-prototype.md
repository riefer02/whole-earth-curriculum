# Lesson 8 — Designing and Making a Working Prototype

## Summary

Learners design and make a **working prototype** that solves a defined problem by working the design
cycle — **ask, imagine, plan, create, test, improve** — and revising it through testing and feedback,
keeping a testing log of what changed and why.

## Objectives

- Design and make a working prototype that solves a defined problem. _(D07.S4.08.01)_
- Revise the prototype through testing and feedback. _(D07.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever fixed something with tape, propped a door open with a stone, or folded paper to hold
something it was never meant to hold? Then you have made a **prototype** — a first, working version of
an idea, made to be tested and changed. A designer does not wait until an idea is perfect; they build a
rough version *right away* and let testing tell them what to fix. The prototype is not the finished
thing. It is a question you ask with your hands.

## Materials

- Design cycle sheet
- Testing log

## Preparation

- Copy the design cycle sheet and testing log; gather found and scrap materials and simple tools.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall designing something with accessibility in mind and testing it for
  barriers — today we run the full design cycle from problem to prototype.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **the design cycle — (1) ask (what
problem, for whom?), (2) imagine (many possible ideas), (3) plan (choose one, list steps), (4) create
(build a working prototype), (5) test (try it, get feedback), (6) improve (revise, then test again) —
and a prototype is a first working version made to be revised.** This is the same engineering design
process used by makers and engineers worldwide (S-370). This is a *skill*, so **model the cycle once on
a tiny example** (build a quick paper water-carrier or a stand for a book, test it, change one thing),
then let learners work their own cycle (explicit instruction carries novice skills — S-011). The
critical-thinking lens: testing is how a maker finds out what actually works, rather than guessing. The
egalitarian lens: design must ask *who is left out* — a prototype that one person can use but another
cannot is not finished; build with accessibility in mind (this extends Grade 7's work, and the
universal-design principles are the frame — S-369). The environment lens: choose materials that can be
reused or that come from what is at hand, and ask what the thing costs the Earth (S-371). The technology
lens: a tool or machine is just a design made real — and every design is a set of choices with
consequences. Keep assessment mastery-based: a prototype that fails a test is *information*, not a
verdict — revise and try again.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: what did you design with accessibility in mind, and how did you
   test it? Today we run the full **design cycle**.
2. **Meet the cycle (10 min).** Look at the design cycle sheet. Six moves: **ask, imagine, plan,
   create, test, improve**. The loop repeats. A **prototype** is a first working version, made to be
   revised (S-370).
3. **Modeled cycle (10 min).** Together, take one problem (for example, *carry water without spilling*).
   Imagine two ideas, plan one, build a quick paper prototype, test it, and change one thing after
   seeing it fail. Notice the change.
4. **Design and make your own (20 min).** Choose a problem (from a list or your own): keep a cup from
   tipping, hold papers without a folder, shade a face from sun, carry several small things at once.
   Ask, imagine, plan, then build your prototype.
5. **Test and revise (10 min).** Test your prototype. On the testing log, record what you observed and
   what you changed. Test again — and change again if needed.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share your prototype and one thing testing taught you. A prototype that fails a
   test is not a failure; it is a lesson.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build a simple prototype for one familiar problem and record one test and one change.
- **Extension:** Design for a *specific user* (someone much younger, someone with limited grip, someone
  using one hand) and test whether that user can actually use your prototype.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner build a working prototype and name what testing changed?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The prototype (or photographs of it) plus the testing log, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, find something that is hard to use or that breaks easily. Sketch one small change that would
fix it. Bring your sketch to the next lesson.
