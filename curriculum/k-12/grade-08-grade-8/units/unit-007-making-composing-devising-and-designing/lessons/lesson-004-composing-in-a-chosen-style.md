# Lesson 4 — Composing in a Chosen Style

## Summary

Learners compose a short piece in a chosen style or tradition by choosing from its four ingredients —
**scale or mode, rhythm, texture and timbre, and form** — and then reflect in writing on the choices
that shaped it. They meet the idea that every tradition has its own scale and conventions, such as the
Indian raga and the Arab, Turkish, and Persian maqam.

## Objectives

- Compose a piece in a chosen style or tradition. _(D07.S2.08.01)_
- Reflect on the choices that shaped the piece. _(D07.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

You already "compose" every time you hum a tune that did not exist before, or tap a rhythm while you
wait. Composing is just that — choosing sounds on purpose. A **style** or **tradition** is a shared set
of choices: which notes a melody may use, what rhythm carries it, what voices or instruments sound like,
and how the piece is shaped from start to finish. When you compose *in a style*, you are borrowing a
language that a whole community has been speaking for a long time — and adding your own sentence to it.

## Materials

- Style ingredients card
- Composition choices sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the style ingredients card and composition choices sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall performing with attention to dynamics, phrasing, and ensemble — today
  we move from performing to *composing*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **composing in a style means choosing
from that style's ingredients — (1) scale or mode (which notes the melody may draw from), (2) rhythm
(the pattern of beats and rests), (3) texture and timbre (what the voices or instruments sound like),
and (4) form (a beginning, middle, and end) — and then reflecting on those choices.** This is a *skill*,
so **model a short example first** (compose a four-phrase melody together, naming each choice), then
let learners compose independently (explicit instruction carries novice skills — S-011). The global
lens is central and must be handled with care: different traditions use different scales and
conventions — Indian classical music is built on the **raga**, a melodic framework with a specific
scale, notes, and mood (S-366), and Arab, Turkish, and Persian music is built on the **maqam**, a modal
system with characteristic intervals (S-367). Teach these as *living traditions to honor with respectful
questions*, never as specimens to imitate carelessly — a learner should not pretend to "do a raga"
without understanding; they may *choose to explore a tradition they know or have been welcomed into*
(S-056). The egalitarian lens: composition needs only voice and body — no instrument is required. The
ethics lens: borrowing from a living tradition carries responsibility; name the tradition, ask before
taking, and give credit (a preview of Grade 10's appreciation-vs-appropriation work). The intellectual
lens: the choices reflection — *why this scale, this rhythm, this form* — is where the learning lives.
Keep assessment mastery-based: a piece that is not finished is "not yet," never a failure.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: what did you pay attention to when you performed — dynamics,
   phrasing, ensemble? Today we move from performing to **composing**.
2. **Meet the ingredients (10 min).** Look at the style ingredients card. A style has four ingredients:
   **scale or mode** (which notes), **rhythm** (beats and rests), **texture & timbre** (what it sounds
   like), and **form** (beginning, middle, end). Two examples: the Indian **raga** (S-366) and the
   Arab, Turkish, and Persian **maqam** (S-367) each have their own scales and moods.
3. **Modeled composition (10 min).** Together, compose a short four-phrase melody, naming each choice as
   you go: "we use these three notes, this rhythm, a clap for texture, and an A–B–A form." Notice the
   choices.
4. **Compose your own (20 min).** Choose a style or tradition you know — a song form from your family, a
   scale you like, a rhythm you feel. Compose a short piece (about 30 seconds to a minute) using voice,
   body, found sound, or an instrument. On the choices sheet, record each choice and its reason.
5. **Reflect (5 min).** Write two or three sentences: what did you choose, and why? What would you
   change if you had more time?
6. **Close (5 min).** Share your piece or your choices with a partner. A composer is someone who can say
   *why* the piece sounds the way it does.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compose a single phrase using three notes and one rhythm; record one choice and reason.
- **Extension:** Compose within a specific scale or mode, name the notes you used, and explain how that
  scale shapes the mood.
- **Access (non-auditory):** A piece can be composed as a drawn score — shapes and marks for rhythm,
  pitch, and form — or as a rhythm tapped on a drum or the body so it can be felt. "Texture and timbre"
  can be explored as vibration, or as different surfaces struck to feel their difference.
- **Access (performance anxiety):** Sharing the piece is a choice, never a requirement. A learner may
  instead share the choices sheet, a drawn or felt score, a recording, or the piece with a single
  trusted partner. The goal is the composing and the choices reflection — not performing in front of a
  group.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the ingredients and explain one choice they made?
- **Performance/portfolio artifact:** The performed or recorded piece plus the choices reflection, kept
  in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, find a song or chant your family knows. Ask what tradition it comes from and what makes it
sound the way it does. Bring one thing you learned to the next lesson.
