# Lesson 2 — Event, Thought, Feeling: Taking a Reaction Apart

## Summary

Learners learn to **analyze** a strong emotional reaction by separating its three parts —
the **event** (what actually happened), the **thought** (the story the mind tells about it),
and the **feeling** (what is felt because of that thought). They watch a worked example,
then practice with a partner and alone, discovering that the thought is the hinge.

## Objectives

- Analyze a strong emotional reaction by separating the event, the thought, and the
  feeling. _(D01.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

A strong feeling can arrive like a storm — all at once, with no visible parts. But every
storm has parts: the air that rises, the water that condenses, the wind that follows. A
reaction has parts too. The moment your heart drops when a friend walks past without a word
is not one single thing; it is an event, a thought, and a feeling stacked so fast you cannot
tell them apart. Today you learn to slow the storm down and name each part.

## Materials

- Event-thought-feeling frame card
- Reaction cards

## Preparation

- Copy the frame card and reaction cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the three movements of the year and the seed example
  (the crash in the hallway) — today you take it apart in full.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a strong reaction has
three parts — event, thought, feeling — and the thought is the hinge between the event and
the feeling.** This is a *skill* for novices, so teach it with explicit instruction: model
the frame once, out loud and step by step (a worked example — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark
2006, S-011), then guided practice before independent work. The frame draws on the ABC model
of cognitive-behavioral approaches (activating event, beliefs, consequences — see S-332),
used here descriptively, not therapeutically. The *ethics* lens: separating parts lets a
learner see that a reaction is not a single fact — it is partly a *story*, and stories can
be checked before they hurt someone. The *egalitarian* lens: no learner's feeling is judged
"too much" or "wrong"; the point is clarity, not ranking. The *global* lens: the same event
(spilled water, a silence, a raised voice) is read through different thoughts in different
families and cultures, so the *same* event really can mean different things to different
people. The *critical-thinking* lens: the frame is a tool for questioning one's own first
take, not for insisting on one "right" thought. Keep learners on small, safe reactions;
never require disclosure of painful material.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Without looking: what are the three movements of the year? What three
   parts did we preview in the crash example? Say them, then check against the map.
2. **Meet the frame (10 min).** Look at the frame card. A **reaction** has three parts.
   **EVENT** — what actually happened, visible to anyone. **THOUGHT** — the story you tell
   yourself about it. **FEELING** — what you feel because of that thought. Notice the thought
   sits *between* the event and the feeling.
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Worked example: *A friend walks past you without saying
   hello.* **Event:** she walked past, silent. **Thought:** "She is angry with me" (or
   "She didn't see me"). **Feeling:** hurt (or just puzzled). Watch how changing the *thought*
   changes the *feeling* while the *event* stays exactly the same.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, choose a reaction card. Fill the three
   boxes. Agree on the **event** first (that part should be the same for both of you), then
   each writes your own **thought** and **feeling** — they may differ, and that is the point.
5. **Independent work (10 min).** On your own, take apart one real, safe reaction of your own
   from this week. Self-check: does my EVENT box hold only what happened, with no story in it?
   Does my THOUGHT box hold the story, and my FEELING box the feeling it caused?
6. **Close (5 min).** Trade frames with a partner and read. The same event can produce
   different thoughts and feelings — the thought is the hinge.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give sentence starters ("The event was ___, I thought ___, so I felt ___")
  and a two-part starter (event, feeling) before adding the thought.
- **Extension:** Take one event and write *two* different thoughts about it, then name the
  feeling each thought produces — evidence that the thought, not the event, is doing the work.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place each part in its own box — event,
  thought, feeling — without blending the event and the thought?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed frame for one reaction, kept as the first piece of
  the unit's reaction-analysis portfolio.
- **Peer check:** A partner confirms the EVENT box holds only observable facts, not stories.

## Home connection

Take the frame home and, without naming anyone, take apart one small reaction from the day —
event, thought, feeling — and ask someone at home what *their* thought would have been about
the same event.
