# Lesson 3 — What the Feeling Tells Me: Emotion as Information

## Summary

Learners explore the **informing** half of emotion: how a feeling can carry useful
information for judgment. They map feelings onto the signals they send, work through a
modeled example, and write a **personal example** of a time a feeling told them something
true — one half of the "informs and distorts" standard.

## Objectives

- Explain how emotion can inform judgment, using a personal example. _(D01.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

A feeling is not always a problem to be fixed. Sometimes it is a **signal** — a message
from the part of you that noticed something before your thinking caught up. The unease on a
dark, empty road; the anger when a rule is plainly unfair; the quiet "yes" when a choice
feels right. These are not errors; they are information. Today you learn to listen for the
message a feeling is carrying.

## Materials

- Emotion-as-signal card
- Signal scenarios

## Preparation

- Copy the emotion-as-signal card and signal scenarios.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the three parts — event, thought, feeling — and where the
  thought sits. Today the *feeling* gets a job: carrying information.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **emotion can inform
judgment — a feeling is a signal carrying information, not a command to obey.** Research on
emotion and decision-making shows emotion is not the opposite of good judgment; it is often
an input to it (Lerner et al. 2015, S-333). Teach the signal-mapping as a worked example
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011) before independent work. The *ethics* lens: a
feeling can alert me to a wrong being done — anger at unfairness is moral information. The
*egalitarian* lens: whose feelings are *listened to* and whose are dismissed is not equal
everywhere; honoring a feeling as information is part of honoring a person. The *global*
lens: the *messages* feelings carry (danger, injustice, unease, care) travel across
cultures, even though the *expressions* differ — what is shown in one place may be hidden in
another. The *critical-thinking* lens: a signal can be wrong — next lesson we learn to check
it. Distinguish evidence from values: that emotion can carry information is a scientific
claim (S-333); that every learner's feeling deserves to be heard is a value this curriculum
holds — name it as one.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what are the three parts of a reaction, and which part
   is the hinge? Say it. Today we give the feeling a job.
2. **Meet the signals (10 min).** Look at the signal card. A feeling can be a **signal**:
   **fear** → "be careful"; **anger** → "a line was crossed"; **unease** → "something is
   off"; **joy** → "this matters." A signal *informs* — it tells you something — it does not
   *command* you to act.
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Worked example: *You feel uneasy about a shortcut home
   after dark.* The unease is **information**: it says "this route feels unsafe." Good
   judgment *listens* — you take the lit street or go with a friend — without letting the
   feeling boss you into panic. The feeling informed the choice; you still chose.
4. **Practice with a partner (15 min).** Choose a signal scenario. Name the feeling, name
   the message it carries, and say how it could inform a good judgment. Then share one of
   your own: a time a feeling told you something true.
5. **Write your personal example (10 min).** Write one short paragraph: *the feeling, the
   message it carried, and the judgment it informed.* This is the "informs" half of your
   reaction analysis — keep it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("I felt ___, and it told me ___") and a single
  signal to focus on.
- **Extension:** For one signal, say *when it would be trustworthy* and *when the same
  signal would mislead* — a bridge to the next lesson on distortion.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a feeling and the information it carried,
  and connect it to a judgment it informed?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The written personal example, kept for the unit's
  informs-and-distorts analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time a feeling warned or guided them well — and share your own
example.
