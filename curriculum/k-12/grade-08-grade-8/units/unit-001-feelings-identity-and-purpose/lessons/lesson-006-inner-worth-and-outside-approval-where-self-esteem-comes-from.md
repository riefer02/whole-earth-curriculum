# Lesson 6 — Inner Worth and Outside Approval: Where Self-Esteem Comes From

## Summary

Learners analyze **where self-esteem comes from**, distinguishing **inner worth** — rooted
in values, effort, and kindness — from **outside approval** — rooted in likes, grades, and
popularity. They sort examples, then write a personal analysis of which source their own
self-esteem leans on.

## Objectives

- Analyze where self-esteem comes from and distinguish inner worth from outside approval.
  _(D01.S2.08.02)_

## Connection

Self-esteem is the sense that you are okay — but where does that sense *come from*? You can
borrow it from outside: the like count, the grade, the praise, the number of people who
approve of you. Or you can build it from inside: the values you actually live by, the effort
you choose, the kindness you give. One of these stands in a storm; the other turns with every
wind. Today you learn to tell them apart — and to notice which one you lean on.

## Materials

- Worth-versus-approval card
- Scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the worth-versus-approval card and scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, recall the self-map and the "whole me." Today's question is
  *where the worth of that whole self comes from*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **self-esteem has two
sources — inner worth and outside approval — and inner worth is the steadier one.** The
distinction draws on research on *contingent self-esteem* — self-worth that depends on
meeting external standards is more fragile than worth that is less contingent (Crocker &
Wolfe 2001, S-335). Teach the sort as a worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006,
S-011) before independent work. The *ethics* lens: worth built on kindness and honesty is
worth that also serves others — it is not self-centered to be steady. The *egalitarian*
lens: approval is distributed unequally — some people get more likes, praise, and high
grades for reasons that have nothing to do with their worth — so building worth on approval
is building on an unfair floor. The *global* lens: what earns approval differs wildly by
place — one culture prizes marks, another courage, another service — so borrowed worth
depends on *where you happen to stand*. Many traditions also ground a person's worth in
*relationship* — kinship, belonging, and duty, as in the southern African idea of *ubuntu*
("a person is a person through other persons") or the Confucian self rooted in family and
community — and this relational worth is not the same thing as the fickle like count. Hold
both ways of grounding worth side by side and let learners weigh them (see
`docs/philosophy.md` §11). The *technology* lens: feeds and like counts are a
designed, always-on approval machine; naming them as a weather vane helps learners see the
machine for what it is. The *critical-thinking* lens: neither source is entirely right or
wrong — approval is real information — the skill is *weighing* which source you let decide
your worth. Distinguish evidence from values: that contingent self-esteem is more fragile is
a research finding (S-335); that every learner has equal worth regardless of any approval is
a value this curriculum holds — name it as one.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: what is the "whole me"? Now ask: where does my sense
   that I am okay come from? List your first three answers silently.
2. **Meet the two sources (10 min).** Look at the card. **Inner worth** is a pillar: built
   on values, effort, kindness — it stands when the wind blows. **Outside approval** is a
   weather vane: built on likes, grades, popularity — it turns with every wind. Both are
   real; one is steadier.
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Worked example: *Your self-esteem drops when a post gets
   few likes.* Where is the worth coming from? Outside approval — a number someone else
   controls. What would an inner-worth version sound like? "I made something I care about;
   the effort is mine." The feeling is the same event; the *source* of worth is different.
4. **Sort (15 min).** With a partner, sort the scenario cards into the two columns. For each,
   say *where* the worth is coming from. Then write your own two lists: one thing that makes
   you feel good about yourself *because of approval*, and one *because of your own values,
   effort, or kindness*.
5. **Analyze your own (10 min).** Write a short note: *which source do I lean on more, and
   what would change if I leaned a little more on the pillar?* Keep it private or share what
   you choose.
6. **Close (5 min).** Approval is real, but it is a weather vane. The pillar is yours to
   build.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give sentence starters ("I feel good when ___ approves; I feel good about
  myself when I ___") and a two-item limit.
- **Extension:** Trace one approval (a grade, a like, a prize) through time: what does it
  feel like the day it lands, and what is left of it a month later?

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner correctly identify the source of worth in a
  scenario and name one example of each source in their own life?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The two lists plus the personal analysis note, kept for the unit's
  identity portfolio.
- **Self-assessment:** "Which source do I lean on more, and what is one small step toward
  the pillar?" — one written line.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what they think *really* makes a person valuable — and listen for whether
their answer sounds like a pillar or a weather vane.
