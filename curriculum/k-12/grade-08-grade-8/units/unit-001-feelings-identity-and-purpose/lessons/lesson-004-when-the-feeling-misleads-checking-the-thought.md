# Lesson 4 — When the Feeling Misleads: Checking the Thought

## Summary

Learners explore the **distorting** half of emotion: how a feeling can skew judgment, and
how to **check the thought** behind it. They learn three common **thought errors**, work
through a modeled example, and write a **personal example** of a time a feeling misled them —
completing the "informs and distorts" standard begun in Lesson 3.

## Objectives

- Explain how emotion can distort judgment, using a personal example. _(D01.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

A feeling can carry truth, but it can also **distort** — like a magnifying glass that makes a
small thing look enormous, or a colored lens that tints everything one shade. You get one
low mark and the feeling says "I am a failure at everything"; a friend does not reply and the
feeling says "she hates me." The feeling *feels* like fact, but it is partly a **story**.
Today you learn to check the story before you believe it.

## Materials

- Check-the-thought card
- Thought-error scenarios

## Preparation

- Copy the check-the-thought card and thought-error scenarios.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall how a feeling can *inform* judgment. Today's question is
  the other half — how it can *distort* it — and what to do about it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **emotion can distort
judgment, and the remedy is to check the thought behind the feeling.** Emotion shapes
judgment in both directions — it informs and it biases (Lerner et al. 2015, S-333); fast,
emotionally charged first reactions can be vivid and wrong, which is why a slower check
matters (Kahneman's System 1/System 2 distinction, S-337). Teach the check as a worked
example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011) before independent practice. The *ethics*
lens: an unchecked thought can hurt other people — I accuse a friend of something she never
did. The *egalitarian* lens: no learner's thought error is a character flaw; distortion is a
normal human habit, and everyone gets to practice catching it. The *global* lens: what a
silence or a glance "means" differs by culture, so the check question — *is this thought true
and fair here?* — protects us from misreading people across difference. The *technology*
lens: feeds, likes, and edited images are built to trigger exactly these distortions —
comparison and catastrophizing — so the check is a shield against a designed pressure, not
only against our own minds. The *critical-thinking* lens: checking a thought is questioning
one's own first take — the same habit we apply to any claim. Keep examples small and safe;
never require disclosure of painful material, and hold that a learner is never judged for a
thought.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: how can a feeling *inform* judgment? Give one example.
   Now the flip side: can a feeling ever *mislead* you? Say a time you have seen it happen.
2. **Meet the check (10 min).** Look at the card. A **distorted** thought is one that is
   vivid but not true or fair. Three common **thought errors**: **catastrophizing** ("the
   worst will happen"), **mind-reading** ("I know what they think of me"), **all-or-nothing**
   ("perfect or ruined"). The check is one question: **Is this thought true and fair?**
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Worked example: *You get one low mark on a test.* Thought:
   "I am terrible at everything and always will be." Which error? Catastrophizing +
   all-or-nothing. The check: is it *true* (one mark, one test — not everything) and *fair*
   (you have other strengths)? Fairer thought: "This one was hard; I can study differently
   and improve." The feeling softened once the thought was checked.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, take a scenario. Name the thought, name the
   error, and write a **fairer thought** that is true and fair. Then try one of your own: a
   time a feeling misled you, and what a fairer thought would have been.
5. **Write your personal example (10 min).** Write one short paragraph: *the feeling, the
   distorted thought, the error it made, and the fairer thought.* Keep it beside your
   "informs" example from Lesson 3.
6. **Close (5 min).** A feeling can tell the truth and it can lie. You are not at its mercy:
   the check question is yours to ask.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a two-option check ("Is this thought true? Is it fair?") and sentence
  starters ("The thought was ___, which is an error because ___; a fairer thought is ___").
- **Extension:** Take one scenario and argue both sides — a case for *trusting* the feeling
  and a case for *checking* it — then say which you find stronger and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a thought error and produce a fairer
  thought that is both true and fair?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The written personal example, completing the unit's
  informs-and-distorts analysis.
- **Peer check:** A partner confirms the "fairer thought" is genuinely fair, not just kinder
  to oneself.

## Home connection

This week, catch one distorted thought of your own, run the check question on it, and tell
someone at home the fairer thought you found.
