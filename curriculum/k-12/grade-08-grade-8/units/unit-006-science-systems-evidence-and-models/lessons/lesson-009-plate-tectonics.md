# Lesson 9 — Plate Tectonics: Continents, Mountains, and Ocean Basins

## Summary

Learners explain how **plate tectonics** shapes **continents, mountains, and ocean basins** over
millions of years. They match the three boundary types — **divergent, convergent, transform** — to
what each builds, and connect the idea to the model-revision story of continental drift.

## Objectives

- Explain how plate tectonics shapes continents, mountains, and ocean basins over millions of years.
  _(D06.S4.08.02)_

## Connection

Look at a map of the world and you might notice that the east coast of South America seems to fit
the west coast of Africa like two puzzle pieces. That fit is not a coincidence. The ground beneath
your feet is not one solid shell — it is broken into great **plates** that move, so slowly you
cannot feel it, carrying continents apart and crashing them together to raise mountains and open
oceans. Today you learn how the face of the Earth was sculpted, one slow inch at a time.

## Materials

- Plate-boundary sheet
- Boundary cards

## Preparation

- Copy the plate-boundary sheet and boundary cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 5 and 7, recall where earthquakes and volcanoes occur and that the Earth's
  surface changes by slow and fast processes; from Lesson 3, recall that models are revised when
  evidence appears.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) Earth's outer layer is
broken into large plates that move very slowly; (2) at a divergent boundary plates pull apart and
new crust forms — mid-ocean ridges and widening ocean basins; (3) at a convergent boundary plates
collide — mountains rise, and where one plate sinks, deep ocean trenches and volcanoes form; (4) at
a transform boundary plates slide past each other — faults and earthquakes; (5) this has been
reshaping continents, mountains, and ocean basins for millions of years.** Use the plate-tectonics
source (S-186) and the erosion source for the slow-change context (S-185). The critical-thinking
lens and a beautiful cross-link: **plate tectonics is itself a model-revision story** — Alfred
Wegener proposed "continental drift" early in the 1900s, but it was mostly rejected until new
evidence (matching rocks and fossils across oceans, sea-floor mapping) revised and strengthened it
into the theory of plate tectonics (S-186). The global lens: the same plates shape every continent,
and the Ring of Fire — a belt of earthquakes and volcanoes around the Pacific — is where millions
of people live with the land's power, and many cultures have long understood and adapted to it. The
ethics lens: living with earthquakes and volcanoes is not evenly distributed — some communities
carry more risk, and preparedness and safe housing are matters of justice. The environment lens: the
slow drift also shapes climate and habitats over millions of years. Keep the timescale honest —
"millions of years" is a model of deep time built from evidence (S-185).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 5 and 7: where do earthquakes and volcanoes happen? From Lesson 3:
   what happens to a model when new evidence appears? Today both answers come together.
2. **Meet the plates (8 min).** Earth's outer layer is broken into large **plates** that move very
   slowly — about as fast as your fingernails grow. **Plate tectonics** is the model that explains
   how they move and what they build.
3. **Meet the three boundaries (15 min).** **Divergent** — plates pull apart; new crust forms, and
   ocean basins widen at mid-ocean ridges. **Convergent** — plates collide; mountains rise, and
   where one plate sinks, deep trenches and volcanoes form. **Transform** — plates slide past each
   other; faults form and earthquakes strike.
4. **Match and build (10 min).** With a group, match each boundary card to what it builds — a ridge,
   a mountain, a trench, a fault. Use your hands as two plates: together, apart, past.
5. **Meet the model revision (7 min).** Long ago, a scientist proposed **continental drift** — that
   continents move. Most others doubted it. Then new evidence — matching rocks and fossils across
   oceans, and maps of the sea floor — revised the model into **plate tectonics**. A model revised,
   not discarded (S-186).
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one landform you can now explain. The Earth is alive under our feet —
   slowly, powerfully, and for millions of years.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three boundaries pre-labeled and ask learners to match each to its
  landform.
- **Extension:** Connect plate tectonics back to Lesson 3: what evidence turned "continental drift"
  into "plate tectonics," and why was the old idea revised rather than thrown away?

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain the three boundary types and the landforms
  each builds over millions of years?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The labeled plate-boundary sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

If you can, look at a map with someone at home and find the shapes that seem to fit, or a mountain
range or ocean nearby, and ask: what slow forces shaped this?
