# Lesson 9 — Caring for Land and Water: Stewardship Practices Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners **analyze a community's practice for caring for land or water, from any culture, and what makes
it work**. They study real examples — Indigenous cultural burning in Australia, Andean terracing, and
Māori kaitiakitanga (guardianship) — and name the common threads: deep local knowledge, care across
generations, and treating land and water as beings owed care, not only resources to use.

## Objectives

- Analyze a community's practice for caring for land or water, from any culture, and what makes it work.
  _(D10.S4.08.02)_

## Connection

Every place on Earth has people who have learned, over many generations, how to live with it. Aboriginal
and Torres Strait Islander peoples in Australia light small, cool fires in the right season so big,
destructive fires cannot take hold. Andean farmers cut terraces into steep mountains so rain soaks the
soil instead of washing it away. Māori communities name themselves *kaitiaki* — guardians — of rivers and
forests. These are not relics; they are living answers to the question this unit has been asking. Today
you study how people care for the Earth, and what makes that care work.

## Materials

- Stewardship analysis sheet
- Practice cards

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and practice cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall that some ecosystems depend on fire and that Indigenous peoples have
  used fire to care for land for thousands of years. Today we study such practices in their own right.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **across cultures, communities hold
living practices for caring for land and water, and what makes them work is deep local knowledge, care
passed across generations, and a view of land and water as beings owed care — not only resources to
extract.** The worked examples (all real, all cited): **Indigenous cultural burning** in Australia — small,
cool, seasonally-timed fires that reduce fuel and encourage new growth, rooted in Aboriginal and Torres
Strait Islander knowledge (S-320, S-321). **Andean terracing** — stone terraces that slow water, hold
soil, and create farmable land on steep slopes, maintained by communities over centuries. **Māori
kaitiakitanga** — the practice of *guardianship* of rivers, forests, and coasts, holding people as
relatives of the natural world, not its owners (S-202). The **environment** lens is this lesson's core:
these practices are tested, effective care — they work because they match the place. The **global &
anthropological** lens is central too: this is not one culture's story but a worldwide pattern — every
region holds its own stewardship traditions, and we honor each as a living practice, never as a museum
specimen (philosophy §4). The **egalitarian** lens: Indigenous and local peoples have often been pushed
off their land, and recognizing their stewardship is a matter of justice, not charity. The **ethics**
lens: treating land and water as beings owed care is one answer to "what is right here" — and it differs
from treating them only as resources, a difference learners should weigh, not be told to adopt (philosophy
§11). The **technology** lens: these practices are technologies too — tools and knowledge for living —
that often cost the Earth less than the industrial ones this unit has weighed. Hold the examples with
respect and accuracy; where a practice is a living tradition of a specific people, name that people and
cite the source, and invite learners to bring their own communities' practices forward.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: how can fire *help* an ecosystem? Today we study people who use
   fire — and other tools — to care for land on purpose.
2. **Meet stewardship (5 min).** **Stewardship** is caring for land or water so it keeps thriving — for
   people now and later. Many cultures call it something stronger: *guardianship*.
3. **Study three practices (15 min).** Read the cards: **cultural burning** (Australia), **terracing**
   (the Andes), **kaitiakitanga** (Aotearoa New Zealand). For each, note what it does and what knowledge
   it uses.
4. **Name what makes it work (10 min).** Fill the organizer for one practice: what it does, the knowledge
   it rests on, who passes it down, and what makes it work. Common threads: deep local knowledge, care
   across generations, and seeing land and water as beings owed care.
5. **Weigh it (10 min).** Compare with the industrial resource use you weighed in Lessons 2 and 8. What
   does each approach assume the Earth *is* — a relative to care for, or a resource to use? What does each
   gain and give up?
6. **Close (5 min).** Caring for the Earth is not new, and it is not one people's idea — it is everywhere.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one practice organizer pre-filled and ask the learner to explain one thing that
  makes it work.
- **Extension:** Compare two practices and write what they share and where they differ, using evidence from
  both cards.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner analyze one practice and name at least one thing that makes
  it work?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed stewardship analysis sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an elder or neighbor: how did people care for this land or water when they were young? Bring one
practice (or one question) to share.
