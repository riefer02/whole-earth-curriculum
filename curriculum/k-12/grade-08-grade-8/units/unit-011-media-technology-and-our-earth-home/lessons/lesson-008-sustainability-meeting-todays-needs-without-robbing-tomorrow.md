# Lesson 8 — Sustainability: Meeting Today's Needs Without Robbing Tomorrow

## Summary

Learners explain what **sustainability means — meeting today's needs without robbing the future — and
apply it to one resource**, comparing how fast the resource is used against how fast it renews. They see
that sustainability is a balance, not a slogan.

## Objectives

- Explain what sustainability means (meeting today's needs without robbing the future) and apply it to one
  resource. _(D10.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

A water tank, a bank account, a forest — all three follow the same quiet rule: if you take out faster
than it comes back, you eventually run empty. A family that spends more than it earns must borrow from
tomorrow. A world that uses water, trees, and fish faster than they renew is doing the same to the
future. Today you learn the name for living inside that rule: **sustainability**.

## Materials

- Sustainability sheet
- Resource cards

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and resource cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall the basic science of climate change and how greenhouse gases trap heat.
  Today we learn the idea that frames how we use the Earth's resources at all.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **sustainability means meeting today's
needs without robbing the future — using a resource no faster than it can renew, and leaving the systems
that support life working.** The definition traces to the Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (1987):
meeting "the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own
needs" (S-395). The application skill: **pick one resource, compare how fast it is used against how fast
it renews, and judge whether that use is sustainable.** Worked example: **fresh water** — it renews
through the water cycle, but we can draw an aquifer faster than rain refills it, so the balance, not the
existence of water, is what decides. **Renewable** resources (sunlight, wind, regrowing forests, fish
stocks *if* used gently) can renew in a human lifetime; **nonrenewable** resources (fossil fuels,
minerals) cannot — using them is by definition spending the future's inheritance. The **environment**
lens is this lesson's core: the Earth's systems are the account we are drawing on. The **egalitarian**
lens: the "future" is not abstract — it is children everywhere, and the people least responsible for
overuse are often hit first and hardest (S-005). The **ethics** lens: using more than your share of a
shared resource is a question of fairness to others, now and later. The **global** lens: sustainability
is a worldwide question with local answers — a well in one village and a forest in another are the same
arithmetic in different places (S-004). The **technology** lens: technology can spend resources faster
or help us use them more gently — it is a lever, not an answer by itself (S-371). Keep the numbers honest
and cited; distinguish evidence (how fast resources renew) from values (what we owe the future).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how do greenhouse gases trap heat? Today we ask the bigger question
   that frames it: how do we use the Earth's resources at all?
2. **Meet the definition (10 min).** **Sustainability** means meeting today's needs without robbing the
   future — using a resource no faster than it can renew, and leaving the living systems that support us
   working. Say it in your own words.
3. **Meet renewable vs. nonrenewable (10 min).** A **renewable** resource (sunlight, wind, a regrowing
   forest, fish used gently) comes back within a human lifetime. A **nonrenewable** resource (coal, oil,
   minerals) does not. Which kind is riskier to spend, and why?
4. **Apply to one resource (15 min).** Worked example: **fresh water**. It renews through the water
   cycle — but draw an aquifer faster than rain refills it and it shrinks. Fill the sheet: what it
   provides, is it renewable, how fast is it used, one way to use it without robbing the future.
5. **Judge the balance (7 min).** Read the resource cards and pick one more (forest trees, fish, fossil
   fuels). Write your verdict: "Using ___ at this rate is / is not sustainable, because ___."
6. **Close (3 min).** Sustainability is not a feeling — it is a balance you can check.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one resource row pre-filled and ask the learner to state the verdict sentence.
- **Extension:** Quantify the balance for one resource with rough numbers (for example, "we use X liters
  a year; the aquifer refills Y") and defend a conclusion.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define sustainability and apply the use-vs-renew balance
  to one resource?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed sustainability sheet with one written verdict, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Pick one resource your household uses every day and ask: does it renew, and are we using it faster than
it does? Discuss one small change.
