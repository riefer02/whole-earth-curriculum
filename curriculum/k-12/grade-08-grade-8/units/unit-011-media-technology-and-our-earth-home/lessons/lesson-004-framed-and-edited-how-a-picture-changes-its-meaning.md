# Lesson 4 — Framed and Edited: How a Picture Changes Its Meaning

## Summary

Learners analyze how a **photo, video, or story can be framed or edited to change its meaning** — by
cropping, captioning, and choosing context — and learn **how to check it**: ask what is cropped out, what
the caption claims, and where the picture really came from. The skill is not distrust of every image but
honest checking of each one.

## Objectives

- Analyze how a photo, video, or story can be framed or edited to change its meaning, and explain how to
  check it. _(D10.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

A picture can be completely real and still tell a false story. Crop a crowd tightly and a small scuffle
looks like a riot; pull back and it is a mostly peaceful day. Add one caption and the same photo becomes
"fans celebrate" or "protesters clash." The photo never lied — the *framing* did. Today you learn to see
the frame, and to check what is outside it.

## Materials

- Framing-and-editing sheet
- Checking checklist

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall what makes a source trustworthy; from Grade 7, recall misinformation
  versus disinformation. Today we look at the *image* side.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **meaning lives in the frame, not only
in the image.** Three moves change meaning without any "faking": **(1) cropping** — leaving out what is
outside the frame; **(2) captioning** — telling the viewer what to think it shows; **(3) context** —
pairing an image with the wrong place, time, or event (a real photo from one protest attached to a
different one years later). The checking skill (S-217, S-151): **ask what is cropped out, what the caption
claims, and where the picture really came from** — find the original with a reverse image search or by
tracing the source, and check it against independent reporting. The **ethics** lens: changing a picture's
meaning to mislead is a choice with consequences — it can inflame fear or blame real people. The
**egalitarian** lens: who is shown, and who is cropped out, is never neutral — a crowd "of peaceful
people" or "of rioters" changes whose story is believed. The **global** lens: the same framing tricks work
in every language and country; media literacy is a global skill (S-217). The **technology** lens: editing
tools that once needed a studio now sit in every pocket, which makes checking more necessary, not less.
Keep examples real and age-appropriate; never ask learners to relive trauma. The goal is the *habit* of
checking, taught with worked examples before independent practice (S-011).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What makes a source trustworthy? (We can trace it and check it.) Images are
   sources too.
2. **Meet the three moves (10 min).** A photo's meaning can change three ways: **crop** (what is left
   out), **caption** (what the words tell you to see), and **context** (the wrong time or place). Say each
   in your own words.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Look at the sheet: one crowd scene, two crops. Cropped tight, it looks
   like a fight; pulled wide, it is mostly calm. With two different captions, the meaning flips again. Say
   exactly what changed.
4. **Check it (15 min).** Use the checklist: What is cropped out? What does the caption claim? Where and
   when was it taken? What does the original source say? For a real image, find the original with a
   reverse image search.
5. **Practice (7 min).** Apply the checklist to one more image (from the sheet or one you found). Write
   your verdict: "This image likely shows ___, because ___."
6. **Close (3 min).** You don't have to distrust every picture — just check each one the way you'd check a
   stranger's story.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the checklist with one row pre-filled and ask the learner to complete the rest.
- **Extension:** Find a real image online, run a reverse image search, and write a short report on where
  it really came from.
- **Access (non-visual):** The whole skill can be taught by listening. A partner reads two written
  accounts of one event — one that leaves out the crowd's calm majority, one that includes it — and the
  learner names what was "cropped out" (left out of the story). "Framing" a story is leaving out facts,
  and checking is tracing the source and comparing independent reports, which needs no sight.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one framing move (crop, caption, context) and
  one check for it?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed framing-and-editing sheet with a written verdict, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Find one photo in a feed or a newspaper and ask: what might be outside the frame, and what does the
caption want me to believe? Share your thinking.
