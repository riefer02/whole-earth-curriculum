# Lesson 2 — Who Benefits, Who Is Harmed: Weighing a Technology

## Summary

Learners **analyze the benefits and harms of a technology — a mobile phone — for different groups of
people** and **weigh them**, seeing that the same tool helps some people and burdens others. This is the
unit's first core skill: judging technology neither as pure good nor pure evil, but as a real trade.

## Objectives

- Analyze the benefits and harms of a technology for different groups of people, and weigh them.
  _(D10.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

Almost everyone you know has a phone — but not everyone uses one the same way, and not everyone gets the
same thing from it. For a farmer far from a bank, a phone can be a way to receive money safely; for a
child, the same phone can be a distraction or a doorway to strangers. And before the phone is even made,
someone else dug the minerals for it. One object, many people, very different outcomes. Today you learn
to **weigh** a technology by asking: *who benefits, and who is harmed?*

## Materials

- Benefits-and-harms sheet
- Technology case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the unit's organizing idea — we weigh technology for who it helps and
  harms.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a technology is never neutral — the
same tool helps some people and burdens others, and "good" or "bad" is too simple.** Use the mobile phone
as the worked example (S-211): *benefits* — a rural farmer receives and sends money by mobile phone
(M-Pesa and similar services let people without a bank branch store and move money), a person with a
disability uses voice and text to communicate, a migrant worker stays in touch with family. *Harms* — a
child faces distraction and strangers; discarded phones become **e-waste**, toxic when dumped (S-397);
and the minerals inside, such as cobalt, are sometimes mined in conditions that harm and exploit workers,
including children (S-398). The **egalitarian** lens is central: the benefits and harms fall unevenly, and
naming *who* carries each is the whole point. The **ethics** lens: once we see who is harmed, we can ask
what is owed to them. The **global** lens: the phone is a global object — mined on one continent, built on
another, sold and discarded across the world. The **technology & environment** lens: the same device that
connects people also leaves a trail of e-waste and carbon. Keep the example factual and cite the sources;
do not moralize — weigh honestly and let learners reach their own reasoned judgments (philosophy §11).
The goal is mastery of the *skill of weighing*, not a single "correct" verdict (philosophy §8).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what does it mean to weigh a technology? (To ask who it helps and
   who it harms — not just whether *you* like it.)
2. **Meet the technology (5 min).** Today's example: the **mobile phone**. One object, used by billions —
   but in very different ways.
3. **Meet the groups (10 min).** Read the case cards: a **rural farmer**, a **city worker**, an **elder**,
   a **child**, a **miner**, a **person with a disability**. For each, ask: what does the phone give them,
   and what does it take?
4. **Fill the sheet (15 min).** Worked example first: the farmer — *benefit*: mobile money lets her
   receive payment without traveling to a bank; *harm*: none obvious to her directly, but the phone's
   minerals were mined far away. Fill the rest of the rows in your group.
5. **Weigh it (10 min).** Step back. Is the phone "good" or "bad"? Neither. Say it precisely: "The phone
   helps ___ and harms ___, and the harms fall hardest on ___."
6. **Close (5 min).** A fair judgment of a technology names everyone, not just the user in the mirror.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the sheet pre-filled for one group and ask the learner to explain that one row in
  their own words.
- **Extension:** Choose a second technology (a plastic bottle, a bicycle, a fishing net) and weigh its
  benefits and harms for at least three groups.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one benefit and one harm for two different
  groups, and state a balanced judgment?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed benefits-and-harms sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Pick one object at home and ask: who made it, who uses it, and who is helped or harmed along the way? Note
one idea to share.
