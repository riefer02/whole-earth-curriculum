# Lesson 3 — Design Trade-offs: Cost, Safety, Access, Environment

## Summary

Learners explain how **design decisions involve trade-offs among cost, safety, access, and the
environment**, using the real example of lighting a home without grid electricity — a kerosene lamp
versus a solar lamp. They see that no design is best at everything, and that a good design names what it
is trading away.

## Objectives

- Explain how design decisions involve trade-offs among cost, safety, access, and the environment, using
  a real example. _(D10.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

When you choose between two ways to do one job — walk or ride, paper or screen — you are doing what every
designer does: trading one thing for another. The walk is slower but cleaner and free; the ride is faster
but costs money and fuel. Designers face the same four questions for every object: *How much does it
cost? Is it safe? Who can reach it? What does it do to the Earth?* Today you learn to name the trade
that lives inside every design.

## Materials

- Trade-off sheet
- Case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the benefits-and-harms skill; today we look inside the *design* behind
  those benefits and harms.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **every design trades one good for
another; there is no perfect design, only designs that are honest about what they trade.** The worked
example: **lighting a home without grid electricity.** A **kerosene lamp** is cheap to buy — low upfront
**cost** — but it is a fire **hazard** (unsafe), its smoke harms health (WHO household air pollution,
S-219), and burning kerosene pollutes the **environment** and emits carbon. A **solar lamp** is clean and
safe — better on **safety** and **environment** — but it costs more upfront, which limits **access** for
people who cannot afford it, and it depends on sunlight and a battery that will one day be waste. So the
trade-off is real: *low cost now* versus *safety and a clean environment*, with *access* caught in the
middle. The **egalitarian** lens: "access" is the corner that asks who is left out — a lamp that costs
too much stays out of the hands that need it most. The **ethics** lens: a designer who ignores safety or
the environment to save cost is making a moral choice, not just a technical one. The **global** lens: this
exact trade-off is being made right now in homes from Nairobi to the Himalayas, where off-grid solar is
growing fast. The **technology & environment** lens: the "environment" corner is not optional decoration —
it is one of the four things every real design must weigh (S-095, S-371). Keep the example factual; let
learners argue which corner matters most and why, without forcing a single answer (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: the same technology helps some and harms others. Today we ask a
   deeper question: *why* does a technology land that way? Because of the design.
2. **Meet the four corners (10 min).** A designer balances four things: **cost** (what it takes to make
   and buy), **safety** (does it hurt anyone?), **access** (who can actually reach and use it?), and the
   **environment** (what does it do to the living Earth?). Say each in your own words.
3. **Meet the case (10 min).** Read the case cards: two ways to light a home without grid electricity — a
   **kerosene lamp** and a **solar lamp**. For each, sort the facts into the four corners.
4. **Find the trade (15 min).** Worked example: the kerosene lamp — *low cost*, but *unsafe* (fire) and
   *bad for health and environment* (smoke, carbon). Now do the solar lamp: *safe and clean*, but *higher
   cost* and so *less access*. Write the trade in one sentence: "You can have ___ , but it costs ___ ."
5. **Weigh (7 min).** If you had to choose for a family with little money and no grid, which lamp would
   you design toward, and which corner would you protect most? Why?
6. **Close (3 min).** There is no perfect design — only designs that are honest about what they trade.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the trade-off sentence starter and ask the learner to fill in two corners only.
- **Extension:** Choose a second design decision and map its four trade-offs, then defend which corner
  matters most in that case.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the four corners and state one real trade-off in one
  sentence?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed trade-off sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Look at one thing your household uses every day and ask: what trade-off did its designers make among
cost, safety, access, and the environment? Discuss one.
