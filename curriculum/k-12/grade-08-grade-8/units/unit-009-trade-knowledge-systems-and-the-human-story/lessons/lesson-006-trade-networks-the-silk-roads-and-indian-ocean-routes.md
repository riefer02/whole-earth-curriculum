# Lesson 6 — Trade Networks: The Silk Roads & Indian Ocean Routes

## Summary

Learners analyze how **long-distance trade networks** — the **Silk Roads** overland and the **Indian
Ocean routes** by sea — connected peoples and moved **goods, ideas, and beliefs**. They build a
labeled trade-network map as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Analyze how long-distance trade networks, such as the Silk Roads or Indian Ocean routes, connected
  peoples and moved goods, ideas, and beliefs. _(D08.S3.08.01)_

## Connection

Imagine a single road or sea lane that carries not only boxes of things but also new words, new
recipes, new ideas about how to live, and new beliefs. That is what a **trade network** is: not just
a path for goods, but a web that ties distant peoples together. Today you trace two of the greatest
webs in human history — one across deserts and mountains, one across a monsoon sea — and see what
traveled along them.

## Materials

- Trade-network map sheet
- What-moved cards

## Preparation

- Copy the trade-network map sheet and the what-moved cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall that geography (rivers, oceans, deserts, mountains) connected or
  separated peoples and shaped the spread of ideas and goods (D08.S3.07.02); from Grade 6, recall
  the voyage of Zheng He across the Indian Ocean.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **long-distance trade networks
connected peoples across vast distances and moved not only goods but ideas and beliefs.** The two
networks: **(1) the Silk Roads** — overland routes across Central Asia linking East Asia (China) with
the Mediterranean world, carrying silk, spices, paper, and glass, and moving **Buddhism** from India
into Central and East Asia and **paper and the decimal numerals** westward (S-323, S-317, S-318,
S-264). **(2) the Indian Ocean routes** — a sea network driven by the **monsoon** (a seasonal wind
that reverses direction), sailed by **dhows** with lateen sails, linking East Africa, Arabia, India,
Southeast Asia, and China, carrying spices, gold, ivory, porcelain, and cloth, and moving **Islam**,
other beliefs, and later technologies across the ocean (S-207, S-323, S-265). The Ming Chinese fleets
of **Zheng He** (1405–1433) sailed this network as far as Arabia and East Africa (S-265, S-264). The
**global** lens: these networks made the medieval world far more connected than a one-region story
suggests — goods and ideas crossed between China, India, Persia, East Africa, and beyond. The
**technology** lens: the network itself was shaped by technology — camel caravans and oasis stops on
land, the dhow and the monsoon calendar at sea. The **ethics** lens: exchange was mostly mutual and
peaceful, but we are honest that not all movement was free (later in this unit we face the slave
trade that some routes later carried). The **egalitarian** lens: the people who actually moved the
goods — caravan guides, sailors, porters, merchants of many languages — were the real makers of the
network, often left unnamed in old records; we name them. Keep it honest: this is documented history,
told as connection, not as one people's triumph (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how did oceans, rivers, deserts, and mountains connect or
   separate peoples? Today we trace two great webs that *connected* them.
2. **Meet the Silk Roads (10 min).** The **Silk Roads** were overland routes across Central Asia,
   linking East Asia and the Mediterranean. Camel **caravans** carried silk, spices, paper, and glass.
   But they carried more than goods: **Buddhism** traveled from India into Central and East Asia, and
   **paper** and the **decimal numerals** traveled westward (S-323, S-317, S-318).
3. **Meet the Indian Ocean routes (10 min).** The **Indian Ocean** was crossed by **dhows** with
   lateen sails, riding the **monsoon** — a wind that reverses with the seasons. This sea network
   linked East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China, moving spices, gold, ivory,
   porcelain, and cloth — and carrying **Islam** and other beliefs. The fleets of **Zheng He**
   (1405–1433) sailed it from China to Arabia and East Africa (S-207, S-265).
4. **Build your map (15 min).** On the map sheet, label the two networks and place the what-moved
   cards: goods (silk, spices, porcelain, gold, ivory), ideas (paper, numerals), beliefs (Buddhism,
   Islam). Show which traveled overland, which by sea, and which by both. _(No map sheet? Lay the two
   routes in string or rope on a table or the floor, trace them by hand, and place the cards along
   them. A partner can also name each route aloud while you trace it — the map can be felt and heard,
   not only seen.)_
5. **Explain the connection (10 min).** Write two sentences: what did these networks *move*, and how
   did they *connect peoples* (not just places)? Then share with a partner.
6. **Close (5 min).** Long before any modern "globalization," the world was already connected — by
   the people who walked, sailed, and traded.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the map pre-labeled with the two routes and ask learners to place the cards
  on the correct route.
- **Extension:** Add a third route or network — the trans-Saharan routes, or the Inka road system —
  and name one thing it moved.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place goods, ideas, and beliefs on the correct network
  and state how the networks connected peoples?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The labeled trade-network map, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Look at one item at home and ask: what route might it have traveled to reach you — and what ideas or
beliefs traveled along routes like that long ago?
