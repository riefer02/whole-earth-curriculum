# Lesson 10 — Capstone: Trade, Knowledge & the Human Story — Putting It Together

## Summary

Learners synthesize the unit's **eight goals** into one web around **the human story, told from many
perspectives**, recalling each goal, writing one sentence and drawing one picture for each, and
choosing one goal to explain fully. This spaced-retrieval pass closes the unit.

## Objectives

- Recall and connect all eight goals: evaluating competing explanations, change and continuity,
  comparing knowledge traditions, cultural blending, trade networks, environmental migration,
  slavery and resistance, and the keeping of humanity, culture, and hope. _(D08.S1.08.01,
  D08.S1.08.02, D08.S2.08.01, D08.S2.08.02, D08.S3.08.01, D08.S3.08.02, D08.S4.08.01,
  D08.S4.08.02)_

## Connection

You began this unit by remembering what you already knew, and you have walked the human story across
continents — from Maya cities and Pacific canoes to the Swahili coast, the Silk Roads, the Ancestral
Pueblo, and the people who kept hope alive under slavery. Now you weave it together. A historian is
not someone who knows eight facts; a historian is someone who can see how the pieces connect into one
story — and can see themselves in it. Today you build that picture.

## Materials

- Capstone map sheet
- Eight goal cards

## Preparation

- Copy the capstone map sheet and goal cards.
- Retrieval: this whole lesson is a retrieval and synthesis pass — the "science of learning" payoff
  of the unit (S-039). Have the learner's earlier portfolio artifacts on hand.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the **closing capstone and synthesis**. The
goal: **each learner recalls all eight goals and connects them into one web around "the human story,
told from many perspectives."** The eight goals are: (1) evaluate competing explanations for a major
change (the Classic Maya collapse); (2) analyze change and continuity in a long-running practice
(trade); (3) compare two knowledge traditions (Polynesian wayfinding and Indian Ocean navigation);
(4) analyze cultural blending (the Swahili coast); (5) analyze trade networks (Silk Roads and Indian
Ocean routes); (6) analyze environmental migration (Ancestral Pueblo, past and present); (7) analyze
slavery and the transatlantic slave trade and resistance; (8) explain how enslaved and colonized
peoples kept humanity, culture, and hope. This is a **retrieval and spacing** moment — recalling
rather than re-reading is what makes learning durable (S-039). Keep assessment **mastery, not
ranking**: a learner who cannot yet recall a goal simply revisits that lesson's artifact and tries
again — "not yet" is information, never a verdict. The four lenses, together: the **critical-thinking**
lens asks what evidence supports each claim; the **ethics** lens asks what is right and who is
affected; the **egalitarian** lens asks who benefits and who is left out; the **global** lens asks
how this looks in many places and times; the **environment** lens asks what the living Earth has to
do with it. History is told as a global story from many perspectives, never one people's triumph
(philosophy §11). Celebrate the collective: each learner's map is a thread in one class web — the
human story itself.

## Procedure

1. **Recall, don't peek (8 min).** Without looking at notes, list the unit's eight goals from memory.
   What can you remember? (That is the point — recalling *is* the learning.)
2. **Check and fill (5 min).** Compare with the eight goal cards. For any goal you missed, open that
   lesson's artifact and add it. "Not yet" is not a failure; it is a map of what to revisit.
3. **Build your web (15 min).** On the capstone map, write one sentence and draw one picture for each
   of the eight goals. Connect them to the center: **the human story, told from many perspectives**.
   _(Expression is open: you may also say each sentence aloud to a partner or scribe, act it out, or
   build the web with string and found objects instead of drawing — the web can be spoken, moved, and
   felt, not only drawn.)_
4. **Choose a deep dive (15 min).** Pick the one goal that most moved you and explain it fully to a
   partner — what it means, what evidence supports it, and one way it touches real life today. Then
   listen to your partner's.
5. **Weave the class web (7 min).** As a group, add everyone's maps to one shared web. Notice how the
   goals touch: trade carried knowledge, knowledge traditions read the same sky, environmental change
   moved people, and people kept hope through it all.
6. **Close (5 min).** Look at your map. You did not memorize eight facts — you walked the human story
   and found your place in it. Carry it with you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work from the goal cards with pictures; choose one goal to explain instead of eight.
- **Extension:** Choose one goal and explain how it connects to at least two others (for example:
   trade networks carried the knowledge traditions, and environmental change drove the migrations
   that blended cultures).

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The completed capstone map — all eight goals, each with a sentence and a
  picture (or a spoken sentence and a described, acted, or object-built "picture") — kept in the
  portfolio.
- **Self/peer:** Learners self-check each goal is present and give one specific "I understood this
  when you said…" to a partner.
- **Mastery, not ranking:** A learner missing a goal revisits its artifact and completes it; the map
  is finished when it is finished, not before.

## Home connection

Show someone at home your capstone map and explain one goal end to end — including how it touches the
world you live in now. Teaching it is the surest sign you have learned it.
