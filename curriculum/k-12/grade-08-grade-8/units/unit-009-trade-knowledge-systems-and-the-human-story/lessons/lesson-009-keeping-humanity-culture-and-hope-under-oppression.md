# Lesson 9 — Keeping Humanity, Culture & Hope Under Oppression

## Summary

Learners explain how **enslaved and colonized peoples kept their humanity, culture, and hope alive**
under oppression — through **music** (spirituals, capoeira), **language** (creoles), **family and
community**, and **faith** — with specific examples. They learn that keeping culture was not only
survival but **creation**.

## Objectives

- Explain how enslaved and colonized peoples kept their humanity, culture, and hope alive under
  oppression, with specific examples. _(D08.S4.08.02)_

## Connection

Think of a song your family sings, a word in your home language, a story told at a gathering. These
small things carry who we are. When people were enslaved or colonized — when systems tried to strip
away their names, languages, and families — they held onto these things, and made new ones. Culture
was not just comfort; it was resistance, and it was hope. Today you see how people kept their
humanity alive.

## Materials

- Kept-alive sheet
- Example cards

## Preparation

- Copy the kept-alive sheet and the example cards.
- Retrieval: from the previous lesson, recall the forms of resistance, including keeping culture
  (D08.S4.08.01); from Grade 7, recall that art and storytelling are forms of resistance
  (D08.S4.07.01).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the **hopeful close** to the unit's hardest
chapters. The idea to land: **oppression tried to erase people's culture, and people refused to let
that happen — they kept and created culture, and that is how humanity and hope survived.** The four
channels with specific examples: **(1) music** — the **spiritual**, an African American religious
song born of the enslaved experience, carrying sorrow, faith, hope, and sometimes hidden messages
(S-385); and **capoeira**, an Afro-Brazilian art that joins movement, music, and play, created by
enslaved Africans and kept alive by their descendants (S-325); **(2) language** — **creole
languages** grew where African languages met European ones, becoming new, complete languages of their
own (for example, Gullah and Kreyòl); **(3) family and community** — enslaved people built kinship,
named "aunts" and "uncles" among those not of blood, and kept community when the law tried to break
families apart; **(4) faith** — people blended African spiritual traditions with others to form new
traditions (for example, Candomblé in Brazil and Vodou in Haiti), keeping connection to ancestors and
the sacred. Colonized peoples did the same: preserving and later **reviving their languages and
traditions** under pressure to abandon them. The **ethics** lens: we honor these traditions as
peoples' own living culture, never as specimens to study coldly. The **egalitarian** lens: the fact
that these cultures *survived* — and in many cases *thrive* — is a victory of the people who kept
them, not of the systems that tried to erase them. The **global** lens: the same pattern appears
across continents — in the Americas, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific — wherever peoples kept their
culture under colonial rule. The **community** lens: culture lives in people together — in song, in
story, in shared food and work. Keep the door to hope wide open: this is a lesson about what could
*not* be taken away (S-385, S-325).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From last lesson: what were the forms of resistance, and which one was
   "keeping culture"? Today we go deeper into how people kept culture — and hope — alive.
2. **Meet the four channels (10 min).** People kept their humanity through **music**, **language**,
   **family and community**, and **faith**. Read the four channel names on the sheet.
3. **Meet the examples (15 min).** Sort the example cards: **music** — the **spiritual**, born of the
   enslaved experience and carrying sorrow, faith, and hope (S-385), and **capoeira**, an
   Afro-Brazilian art of movement and music (S-325); **language** — **creole languages**, new and
   complete languages that grew where African and European languages met; **family and community** —
   kinship and care that the law could not break; **faith** — traditions that blended African
   spiritual ways with others, keeping connection to ancestors and the sacred.
4. **Fill the sheet (10 min).** Under each channel, write the example in your own words and one
   sentence on what it kept alive.
5. **Reflect (7 min).** In a group, name one thing in your own life — a song, a word, a story, a
   gathering — that carries your culture and hope. Notice the pattern: it is the same thing enslaved
   and colonized peoples did.
6. **Close (3 min).** Oppression could not take their humanity, because they kept their culture — and
   their culture kept them. That is what hope looks like.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each example to its channel, then choose one to retell in one sentence.
- **Extension:** Add a second example of your own — a language revival, a tradition of storytelling,
  or a music — and explain what it kept alive.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two channels with a specific example and
  explain what each kept alive — speaking of the peoples with respect?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed kept-alive sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Share one thing in your family or community that carries its culture and hope, and ask a family
member what it means to them.
