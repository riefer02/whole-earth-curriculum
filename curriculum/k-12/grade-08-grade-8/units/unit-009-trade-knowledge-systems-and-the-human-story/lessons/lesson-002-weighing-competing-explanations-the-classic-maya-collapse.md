# Lesson 2 — Weighing Competing Explanations: The Classic Maya Collapse

## Summary

Learners evaluate **competing explanations** for one major historical change — the **Classic Maya
collapse** (roughly 800–950 CE) — weighing three explanations (**drought, warfare, environmental
stress**) and the evidence for each, then supporting a **reasoned judgment**. They learn that
historians weigh evidence, and that several causes often work together.

## Objectives

- Evaluate competing explanations for one major historical change and support a reasoned judgment
  with evidence. _(D08.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

When something big goes wrong at school or at home — a garden fails, a team loses, a friendship
breaks — people often give different reasons: "it was the weather," "it was a bad decision," "it was
too much at once." Usually it is not one thing; it is several, working together. Historians face the
same puzzle about the past. Today you act as a historian and weigh the rival explanations for one
great change, deciding what the evidence best supports.

## Materials

- Evidence-and-explanation sheet
- Evidence cards

## Preparation

- Copy the evidence-and-explanation sheet and the evidence cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall that a major change has multiple causes and consequences
  (D08.S1.07.02) and that a claim needs evidence.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **historians evaluate competing
explanations by weighing evidence, and a reasoned judgment says which explanation the evidence best
supports — often several causes working together.** The worked historical example: the **Classic
Maya civilization** flourished from about 250 to 900 CE across Mesoamerica (present-day southern
Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador), building great cities (Tikal, Calakmul,
Copán, Palenque) with writing, astronomy, and a precise calendar (S-378, S-204). Then, beginning
around 800–950 CE, many southern-lowland cities were abandoned — the "Classic Maya collapse." Three
competing explanations: **(1) drought** — evidence from lake-sediment cores (for example, gypsum and
oxygen-isotope records at Lake Chichancanab) points to severe, repeated droughts around 800–1000 CE;
**(2) warfare and political instability** — monuments and inscriptions record mounting war between
rival city-states; **(3) environmental stress and overpopulation** — intensive farming and growing
populations may have worn down fragile tropical soils (S-378). Most scholars today hold that these
worked **together**, with drought as a trigger that magnified the others (S-378). The **ethics**
lens: it is honest and respectful to say "we are not certain; the evidence supports several causes"
rather than pretending to one clean answer. The **egalitarian** lens: the Maya did not "vanish" — Maya
peoples live today in the millions across the region, speaking Maya languages and carrying their
traditions; the "collapse" was the decline of certain lowland cities, not of a people. The **global**
lens: the Classic Maya are one of many brilliant civilizations (Mesoamerica, the Andes, West Africa,
the Indian Ocean world) in this unit — the human story is many stories. The **environment** lens: a
changing climate (drought) is a reminder that environmental change has shaped human history for
thousands of years — a thread we will pick up later in the unit. Keep it honest: this is documented
scholarship, and where scholars disagree, we hold the disagreement as a question, not a verdict
(philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: why does a major change usually have more than one cause? Today
   you weigh rival explanations for one change.
2. **Meet the change (8 min).** The **Classic Maya** built great cities in Mesoamerica with writing,
   astronomy, and a precise calendar (about 250–900 CE). Then, around 800–950 CE, many southern
   lowland cities were abandoned. Historians do not all agree on why — and that is our job today.
3. **Meet the three explanations (10 min).** Read the three candidate explanations: **(1) drought** —
   the rains failed; **(2) warfare** — rival cities fought until the system broke; **(3) environmental
   stress** — too many people and too much farming wore out the land. A good historian does not pick a
   favorite; a good historian asks: *what is the evidence?*
4. **Sort the evidence (12 min).** In a group, sort the evidence cards under the three explanations.
   Notice: some evidence fits more than one explanation. Note the strongest piece of evidence for
   each.
5. **Make a reasoned judgment (12 min).** Write your judgment: *which explanation does the evidence
   best support — and is it just one, or several working together?* Back it with at least two pieces
   of evidence. You may say "the evidence supports several causes."
6. **Close (8 min).** Share judgments. Notice that many scholars now hold the same view most learners
   reach: drought, warfare, and environmental stress worked **together**, with drought as a trigger.
   And remember: the Maya peoples did not vanish — millions of Maya live today.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three explanations pre-written and ask learners to match each piece of
  evidence to an explanation, then choose one piece of evidence to support a simple judgment.
- **Extension:** Apply the same weighing skill to a second change — the fall of the Western Roman
  Empire — and name two competing explanations with one piece of evidence each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner weigh at least two explanations against evidence and
  state a judgment with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed evidence-and-explanation sheet with a reasoned judgment,
  kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: when something changed in your family's life, did one cause explain it, or several?
Compare with the Maya collapse.
