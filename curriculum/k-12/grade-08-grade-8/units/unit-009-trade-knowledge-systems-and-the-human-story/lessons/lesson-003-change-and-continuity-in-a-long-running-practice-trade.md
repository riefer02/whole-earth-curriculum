# Lesson 3 — Change & Continuity in a Long-Running Practice: Trade

## Summary

Learners analyze how one long-running practice — **trade** — **changed** over centuries (scale,
speed, money, and technology) and what **stayed the same** (exchanging what is scarce, carrying
goods *and* ideas, and the trust and credit that make exchange possible). They build a
change-and-continuity analysis as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Analyze how a long-running human practice, such as trade, changed over centuries and what stayed
  the same. _(D08.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

Look at the label on your shirt, the spice in your kitchen, the cup you drink from. Almost
certainly, parts of them came from far away — sewn in one place, woven in another, grown or mined in
a third. That everyday fact is the tip of a practice thousands of years old: **trade**. Some things
about trade have changed beyond recognition; other things — the reason we trade at all — have not
changed one bit. Today you tell the difference.

## Materials

- Change-and-continuity sheet
- Trade-stage cards

## Preparation

- Copy the change-and-continuity sheet and the trade-stage cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall that rivers, oceans, deserts, and mountains connected or separated
  peoples (D08.S3.07.02), and from Grade 5, that trade and travel moved goods, ideas, plants, and
  animals (D08.S3.05.02).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a long-running practice shows both
change and continuity — historians ask what changed *and* what stayed the same.** The worked example
is **trade**. What **changed**: (1) *scale* — from local and regional exchange to networks spanning
continents (the Silk Roads overland and the Indian Ocean by sea, S-323, S-207); (2) *speed and
technology* — from foot, camel caravan, and monsoon dhow to sail, steam, and today's container
ships; (3) *money* — from barter and commodity money (cacao, salt, cowrie shells, beads) to coin,
paper money, and electronic credit. What **stayed the same**: (1) the *reason* — people exchange what
is scarce or absent in one place for what another place has; (2) *trade carries ideas, beliefs, and
technology, not only goods* (paper and numerals traveled the Silk Roads, S-317, S-318); (3) *trust,
reputation, and credit* — every exchange, ancient or modern, rests on people trusting that a promise
will be kept. The **global** lens: trade is a human universal — every region has its own networks and
traditions of exchange. The **technology** lens: each new technology (the wheel, the sail, the
container ship, the internet) changed *how* we trade — often for good and for strain, and never
equally for everyone. The **ethics** and **egalitarian** lenses: not all trade has been free — some
trade was built on theft and slavery (we face that squarely later in this unit), and the gains of
trade have never been shared equally; ask who benefits and who is left out. Keep it honest: this is
documented history, told as a pattern, not a single story of progress (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how did rivers, oceans, and mountains connect or separate
   peoples? From Grade 5: what moved along with goods when peoples traded? Today we watch one
   practice — trade — change and stay the same across centuries.
2. **Meet the stages (10 min).** Arrange the trade-stage cards in order: early exchange of obsidian
   and salt, Silk Road caravans, Indian Ocean dhows riding the monsoon, transatlantic sail, container
   ships today. What do you notice changes as you move through the stages?
3. **Find what changed (10 min).** Fill the left column: **scale** (local to global), **speed and
   technology** (feet and camels and sails to engines and ships and the internet), **money** (salt
   and shells and cacao to coins, paper money, and electronic credit).
4. **Find what stayed (10 min).** Fill the right column: the **reason** (exchange what is scarce),
   **trade carries ideas** (paper and numerals traveled with goods), and **trust and credit** (every
   exchange rests on a kept promise).
5. **Apply it (10 min).** Pick one modern object in the room. Trace its materials back through
   today's trade. Which of your "changed" and "stayed" ideas can you see in that one object?
6. **Close (5 min).** A practice can change almost entirely on the outside and stay the same at its
   heart. That is what "change and continuity" means.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the "changed" and "stayed" ideas pre-written and ask learners to sort them
  into the two columns.
- **Extension:** Repeat the analysis for a second practice — writing or farming — and name one
  change and one continuity for it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one change and one continuity in trade
  and say why the continuity matters?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed change-and-continuity sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Trace one thing at home — a food, a tool, a piece of clothing — and ask: how far did its pieces
travel, and what about that journey is new and what is ancient?
