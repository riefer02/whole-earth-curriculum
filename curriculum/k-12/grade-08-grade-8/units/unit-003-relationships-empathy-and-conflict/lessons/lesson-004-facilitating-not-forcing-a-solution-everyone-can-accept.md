# Lesson 4 — Facilitating, Not Forcing: A Solution Everyone Can Accept

## Summary

Learners learn to **facilitate** a group conflict rather than force or avoid it: **set the ground,
hear every side, name the needs and interests behind positions, and build a solution everyone can
accept**. They watch the steps modeled, then practice facilitating an invented conflict with a
**talking piece**, staying **neutral** so the group owns the outcome.

## Objectives

- Manage conflict in a group by facilitating discussion and seeking a solution all can accept.
  _(D02.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

When people disagree, two common moves are to *force* an answer ("do it my way") or to *avoid* the
problem until it festers. There is a third move: **facilitation** — helping a group talk so that
every side is heard and the group itself finds a way forward that everyone can live with. In a
family deciding chores, a class planning an event, a neighborhood sharing a water tap, a team
dividing work — a facilitator does not hand down the answer; they hold the space where the group
finds it. Today you learn that skill.

## Materials

- Facilitation steps card
- Facilitation scenario

## Preparation

- Copy the facilitation steps card and scenario.
- Retrieval: recall Lesson 3's lesson that checking beats assuming, and Grade 7's repair steps
  (listen, take responsibility, repair). Facilitation turns listening into a *structure* a whole
  group can use.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) to facilitate is to guide a
process, not to impose a solution — the facilitator stays neutral and the group owns the outcome;
(2) the four steps are: set the ground, hear every side, name needs and interests (not just
positions), and build a solution all can accept; (3) a solution "all can accept" is not "everyone
got everything" — it is one nobody is left unable to live with.** The step shape follows
restorative and mediation practice (IIRP, S-022) and the needs-language of Nonviolent Communication
(Rosenberg, S-148); model once as a worked example (S-011). **Safety: use only invented, low-stakes
  conflicts; never stage a real fight, and never force a learner to disclose a real dispute.**
  **Accessibility:** the talking piece passes to *however each person communicates* — speech, sign,
  writing, a device, or a gesture — and a facilitator may paraphrase by any of those modes; being
  heard does not require a speaking voice, and the circle and passed object already support learners
  who are deaf, non-speaking, or low vision (the turn is visible and tactile, not only audible). The
  ethics lens: the point is fairness, not victory — a facilitator cares that the process is just, not
that their own side wins. The egalitarian lens: a circle and a talking piece give the quieter and
less powerful a turn, so the loudest does not decide; this is the heart of the method. The global &
anthropological lens: the circle and talking piece have deep roots in Indigenous and community
traditions of peacemaking — among them Māori and First Nations circle practice and Ubuntu-rooted
reconciliation — and mediation appears across cultures in many forms; the four steps are one modern
expression held side by side, not above, those older ways (S-022). The technology & environment
lens: facilitation also works when the group is partly on a screen — the same steps (hear every
side, name needs, build agreement) apply, with extra care for the missing cues from Lesson 3. The
critical-thinking lens: a facilitator distinguishes a *position* ("I want the front seat") from the
*need* behind it ("I get carsick in back") — the solution usually lives in the needs, not the
positions.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7, what were the three repair steps after harm? And from Lesson
   3, why is checking better than assuming? Today you become the person who helps a *group* do both.
2. **Meet the four steps (10 min).** Look at the facilitation steps card. **Set the ground** — one
   voice at a time, no interrupting, the talking piece passes around. **Hear every side** — each
   person says what happened and how they see it. **Name needs** — behind each "I want ___" find the
   "I need ___." **Build agreement** — the group finds a solution everyone can accept.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a facilitator work an invented conflict: *A group has one shared
   device for a project, and two members argue over who uses it first.* Notice: the facilitator does
   not choose; they ask each person, "What do you need?" — one needs it early to leave on time, the
   other needs a turn before dinner. The group agrees on a schedule. The facilitator stayed neutral;
   the group found the answer.
4. **Practice facilitating (20 min).** In a group, choose an *invented* conflict. Pick one
   facilitator; everyone else is a party to the conflict. Pass the talking piece. Run all four
   steps. The goal is a solution all can accept — not a perfect ending.
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one thing facilitation made possible. Remember: a facilitator is not a
   judge — they are the person who makes sure everyone is heard.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only the "hear every side" step with the talking piece before attempting
  all four; give a neutral-phrase starter ("So what I hear you needing is ___").
- **Extension:** Identify a moment in a scenario where a facilitator took a side, and write what
  staying neutral would have changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the four steps and use them while staying
  neutral in a role-play?
- **Performance + peer:** The facilitated conversation, observed for hearing every side, naming
  needs, and reaching a solution all can accept; peers give one kind, specific piece of feedback.

## Home connection

At home, when a disagreement comes up, try being the one who asks each person, "What do you need?"
instead of arguing a side. Notice what changes.
