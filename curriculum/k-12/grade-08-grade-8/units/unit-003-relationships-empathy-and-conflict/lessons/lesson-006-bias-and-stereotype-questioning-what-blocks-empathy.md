# Lesson 6 — Bias and Stereotype: Questioning What Blocks Empathy

## Summary

Learners recognize that **bias and stereotypes** can block empathy by replacing a real person with
a narrow label, and learn to question them with a four-step log: **name the snap judgment, name the
stereotype behind it, check the evidence, and find a counterexample**. They practice on invented
situations and learn that bias is often **automatic** — and therefore something to catch, not to
pretend away.

## Objectives

- Recognize bias and stereotypes that can block empathy, and question them. _(D02.S2.08.02)_

## Connection

Before you know a person, a story about "people like them" may already be in your head — too quiet
to notice, too quick to argue with. A stereotype is a box: it turns one feature (where someone is
from, how they look, how they speak, what they use to get around) into a claim about the whole
person. That box blocks empathy, because you can't feel with someone you have already reduced to a
label. The good news: the box is a habit, and habits can be caught and questioned. Today you learn
how.

## Materials

- Bias-and-stereotype log
- Stereotype scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the bias-and-stereotype log and scenario cards.
- Retrieval: recall Lesson 5's "strongest case" skill, and Grade 7's work on stereotypes and labels
  (how a label can narrow a person). Today you build the tool that questions the label.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a stereotype is an
oversimplified belief that turns one feature into a claim about a whole group, and bias is the
leaning it creates — often automatic ("implicit") rather than deliberate (Banaji & Greenwald 2013,
S-344); (2) stereotypes block empathy because they replace the person with the label; (3) the skill
is a four-step question: what is my snap judgment? what stereotype is behind it? what is the
evidence? what counterexample breaks it?** Group-formation research (Tajfel 1971, S-232) shows
in-group/out-group habits can form from almost nothing, and stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson
1995, S-284) shows labels can weigh on a person's performance — both are about *systems and habits*,
not about any person being born prejudiced. Model the log once as a worked example (S-011). **Hold
this with care and without shame: everyone inherits stereotypes from a culture; noticing one is the
courageous step, not a failing.** The ethics lens: reducing a person to a label is a small injustice
that can scale into big ones — questioning it is right. The egalitarian lens: stereotypes most often
fall on the less powerful, and they do real work in deciding who is trusted, believed, or left out.
The global & anthropological lens: every culture carries its own stereotypes, and the same person
may be labeled differently in different places — the label is not the truth. The technology &
environment lens: media and screens repeat narrow images until types feel like laws (Common Sense
Education, S-063); noticing the repetition is part of questioning it. The critical-thinking lens:
a stereotype is a *generalization* with weak evidence — test it the way you would test any claim.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In Grade 7 you learned how a label can narrow a person. Say to a partner:
   what was one way a label gets "inside" and weighs on someone? Today we build the tool that
   questions the label.
2. **Meet the four-step log (10 min).** Look at the log. When you catch a snap judgment about a
   person, run four questions: **(1) What is my snap judgment? (2) What stereotype is behind it?
   (3) What is the actual evidence? (4) What counterexample breaks the box?** A stereotype is a
   box; a counterexample is a real person who does not fit it.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a judgment be questioned: *"Someone new joins the group who uses a
   wheelchair; a voice in my head says they can't play our game."* Snap judgment: "they can't."
   Stereotype behind it: "people who use wheelchairs can't do what we do." Evidence: none — I
   haven't asked. Counterexample: people who use wheelchairs play basketball, race, and lead. The
   box opened; now I can actually meet the person.
4. **Practice (15 min).** Choose a scenario card — an *invented* situation. Fill all four columns
   of the log. The hard part is column two: name the stereotype honestly, without pretending it is
   not there. Share one counterexample with the group.
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one thing you caught and questioned. Remember: catching a stereotype is
   not shame — it is the moment empathy gets its chance back.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank of feelings and a sentence starter ("The stereotype behind my
  judgment is ___"), and work the first column only if needed.
- **Extension:** Trace where a stereotype came from — media, family, one experience — and write one
  sentence about how it got repeated.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the snap judgment, the stereotype behind it,
  and a counterexample for an invented situation?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed bias-and-stereotype log, kept in the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

At home this week, notice one moment you (or someone close to you) judged a stranger by a label.
Quietly run the four questions. You do not have to announce it — noticing it is the practice.
