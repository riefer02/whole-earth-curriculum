# Lesson 9 — Systems of Care and Mutual Aid: Fair and Unfair

## Summary

Learners analyze how **systems of care and mutual aid** operate and what makes them fair or unfair,
using a map of **who gives, who receives, and who decides**, and a fairness checklist: **access,
dignity, and who is included**. They learn that mutual aid — people meeting each other's needs
together — is a widespread, documented human practice, and that its fairness turns on whether
**power and dignity are shared**, not only on whether goods are.

## Objectives

- Analyze how systems of care and mutual aid operate and what makes them fair or unfair.
  _(D02.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

When someone is sick, hungry, or without shelter, people do not usually solve it alone — they turn
to a **system of care**: a family network, a clinic, a food co-op, a neighborhood that watches out
for its elders, a rotating savings group where each member contributes and each takes a turn. These
systems can be fair or unfair. A fair one gives everyone access, treats people with **dignity**
rather than shame, and lets the people it serves have a say in how it runs. Today you learn to tell
the difference.

## Materials

- Care-system map
- Mutual-aid examples card

## Preparation

- Copy the care-system map and examples card.
- Retrieval: recall Lesson 7's "who decides, who receives, who is left out" tool, and Grade 7's
  mutual-aid action. Today you run that same tool on a *system* of care.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a system of care is a
standing arrangement for meeting needs — a clinic, a co-op, a savings group, a neighborhood
network; mutual aid is people meeting one another's needs together, in reciprocity, rather than
through a top-down giver and a passive receiver; (2) to judge fairness, map who gives, who
receives, and who decides, then run a three-point checklist: access (can everyone who needs it
reach it?), dignity (are people treated as equals, not shamed?), and inclusion (do the people
served have a say?); (3) mutual aid is widespread and documented across cultures and times
(Kropotkin 1902, S-163; communal-work traditions such as harambee, gotong royong, mink'a/minga,
and barn-raising, S-164).** Model the map once (S-011). **Hold the value commitment separately:
"people meeting one another's needs is good" is a value; "mutual-aid traditions are widespread and
documented" is a fact — teach them as distinct.** The ethics lens: a care system can be generous
and still unjust if it shames or excludes. The egalitarian lens: the fairness test is not only
"were goods given" but "was power shared" — the people served must help decide. The global &
anthropological lens: the examples are many and specific — harambee (Kenya), gotong royong
(Indonesia), mink'a/minga (the Andes), barn-raising (North America) — presented as living
traditions, never ranked, with local details to verify with community members. The technology &
environment lens: tools change care systems — mobile money, a shared group chat, a map of who
needs help — and each tool also raises a fairness question of access. The critical-thinking lens:
separate a system's *intentions* from its *outcomes* — a system can mean well and still leave
people out.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, what four questions reveal who is left out? Say them to a
   partner. Today we run them on a whole system of care.
2. **Meet the care-system map (10 min).** Look at the map. For any system of care, trace **who
   gives, who receives, and who decides**. Then run the fairness checklist: **Access** — can
   everyone who needs it reach it? **Dignity** — are people treated as equals, not shamed?
   **Inclusion** — do the people served have a say?
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a system be analyzed: *A neighborhood food co-op pools money each
   week and buys in bulk; members take what they need.* Who gives? Every member. Who receives?
   Every member, including those short that week. Who decides? The members, together, at a weekly
   meeting. Checklist: access — yes, membership is open and contributions flex; dignity — yes, no
   one is asked to prove they are poor; inclusion — yes, the served are the deciders. This is
   mutual aid: reciprocity among equals, not a handout from above.
4. **Analyze in groups (20 min).** Choose a care system from the examples card or one you know.
   Map who gives, receives, and decides. Run the three-point checklist. Write one sentence naming
   what makes it fair and one naming what makes it unfair (or could).
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one finding. Remember: the fairest care shares not only the goods but
   the power — and the dignity.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the map with "who gives" and "who receives" pre-filled, and a sentence
  starter for each checklist point ("Access is fair/unfair because ___").
- **Extension:** Compare two systems (or one system before and after a change) and explain what
  made one fairer, naming the specific difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner map who gives, receives, and decides, and apply the
  three-point fairness checklist with a concrete reason for each?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed care-system map and the fair/unfair sentences, kept in the
  unit portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, think of one system of care your family is part of (a shared fund, a neighbor network, a
place of worship's help, a community kitchen). Ask quietly: who gives, who receives, who decides —
and is it fair? Notice, without needing to fix it all.
