# Lesson 2 — More Than Words: How Tone and Body Language Change Meaning

## Summary

Learners discover that meaning lives in **more than words**: the same sentence, spoken with a
different **tone** and **body**, can mean warm agreement, cold dismissal, or anger. They learn that
tone and body language are **patterned and learned** (and differ across cultures), then practice
reading and sending clear, honest nonverbal messages.

## Objectives

- Analyze how tone of voice and body language can change the meaning of the same words.
  _(D02.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

Think of the single word "fine." One person says it with a warm voice and open hands — it means
"I'm okay, and I'm with you." Another says it flat, arms crossed, looking away — it means "I am
not fine, and I am closing the door." The *words* are identical; the *message* is opposite. In a
family kitchen, a market stall, a field, or a classroom, meaning rides on more than words. Today
you learn to read and send that hidden layer.

## Materials

- Same-words card
- Meaning chart

## Preparation

- Copy the same-words card and meaning chart.
- Retrieval: recall Grade 7's work on reading a boundary and speaking "I" statements. Tone and body
  are how a boundary is *felt* before it is spoken.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) meaning is carried by more
than words — tone and body language change what the same sentence means; (2) tone and body language
are patterned and learned, and the patterns differ across families and cultures — so we ask and
check rather than assume (Ekman & Friesen 1969, S-160); (3) honesty and kindness ask us to make our
tone and body match our words, and to check when they might not.** Model the mismatch once as a
  worked example (S-011) before pairs practice. **Safety: no learner is asked to act out real anger
  or real hurt; use the invented sentence and neutral examples only.** **Accessibility:** meaning
  lives in more than words in *every* way of communicating, so no learner needs a speaking voice or
  working eyes and ears to join. In a signed language, tone lives in the face, in how large, fast,
  and sharp the signs are, and in body posture; in an AAC device or in writing, it lives in word
  choice, punctuation, and emphasis. Offer each learner a path that fits: learners who are deaf or
  hard of hearing read and send tone through facial expression, sign intensity, or written tone
  markers (punctuation, a drawn face, a label like "warm" or "flat"); learners who are blind or low
  vision name the tone aloud and learn posture by describing it in words or copying the shape with
  their own body; learners who use AAC or do not speak convey tone by choosing words and emphasis on
  their device or by pointing to a feelings word. Nobody is asked to produce a voice they do not use
  or to read a face they cannot see. The ethics lens: a message can
hurt or heal depending on *how* it is said, not only what is said. The egalitarian lens: the same
words from someone with more power can land harder than from someone with less — noticing power is
part of reading meaning. The global & anthropological lens: display rules for tone and body differ
across cultures and even families (Ekman & Friesen, S-160) — eye contact, gesture, and silence mean
different things in different places, so "read the room" means "check, don't assume." The
technology & environment lens: this lesson sets up the next one — on a screen, tone and body are
often missing, and meaning gets even more fragile. The critical-thinking lens: distinguish what a
person *said* from what the message *meant*, and be willing to check your guess.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In Grade 7 you learned to read a boundary and speak an "I" statement. Say to
   a partner: what makes an "I" statement different from blame?
2. **Meet the same-words card (10 min).** Look at the sentence: **"Sure, fine."** Say it three ways
   — warm and open; flat with arms crossed; loud with a pointed finger. Each time, the words are the
   same, but the meaning changes: agreement, dismissal, anger. **Tone** is how the voice sounds;
   **body language** is posture, gesture, face, and distance.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch the mismatch be worked: the facilitator says "I'm not angry" with a
   tight jaw, crossed arms, and a hard voice. Ask: which do you believe — the words or the tone and
   body? When they clash, people believe the tone and body, and trust cracks.
4. **Practice in pairs (15 min).** One partner says the sentence with a chosen tone and body; the
   other records on the meaning chart: *the words, the tone and body, the meaning*. Then swap. Try
   at least three different meanings. Check your guess with your partner — did they mean what you
   read?
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one finding. Remember: you are always sending two messages at once —
   your words, and your tone and body. Make them match.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank of tones (warm, flat, sharp, worried, playful) to choose from
  before speaking.
- **Alternative modes:** Let a learner send tone however they can — sign, a drawn face, a written
  word with punctuation, or a described posture — and read tone back the same way; the skill is
  noticing the layer beyond the words, not producing a specific voice or face.
- **Extension:** Find a real example — a line from a song, play, or conversation — where tone
  changed the meaning, and write one sentence analyzing how.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name tone and body language as carriers of meaning
  and give one example of the same words meaning two different things?
- **Performance:** The pair practice, observed for whether the learner checks their reading with
  their partner rather than assuming.

## Home connection

At home this week, listen for one sentence said two different ways (for example, "fine," "okay,"
"later"). Ask yourself: what did the tone and body say that the words did not?
