# Lesson 7 — Who Is Left Out? Power and Resources in a Community

## Summary

Learners analyze how **power and resources** are shared in a community and **who is left out**,
using a four-question tool: **who decides, who receives, who is not at the table, and why**. They
learn to distinguish individual acts from **structural causes** — where someone is born, how work
and pay and access are arranged — without ever measuring a person's worth by their resources.

## Objectives

- Analyze how power and resources are shared within a community and who is left out. _(D02.S3.08.01)_

## Connection

In every community, some things are shared — a classroom's supplies, a village's water, a city's
spaces, a family's time and money — and some people decide how they are shared while others do not.
It is not random: the ones who decide are usually the ones with more **power**, and the ones left
out are often the ones already carrying less. Seeing this clearly — who is at the table, who is
missing, and why — is the first step toward sharing more fairly. Today you learn to see it.

## Materials

- Resource-sharing map
- "Who is left out" questions

## Preparation

- Copy the resource-sharing map and questions.
- Retrieval: recall Grade 7's work on communities changing, and Grade 6's "why some have more than
  they need while others lack." Today you turn that into a structured analysis of one community.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) power is the ability to
influence who gets what, and resources are the things a community shares — space, time, information,
money, and voice; (2) to see who is left out, ask four questions: who decides, who receives, who is
not at the table, and why; (3) the "why" is usually structural — where someone is born, how work
and pay and access are arranged — not a measure of anyone's worth.** Widely documented data show
poverty and inequality are driven largely by structural, place-based factors rather than individual
merit (World Bank, S-234; UNDP, S-283); hold the value commitment separately — "no person's worth
is measured by their resources" is a value, not a statistic. Model the map once (S-011). **Keep it
concrete and non-shaming: analyze systems, not classmates; use invented or public examples, never
call out an individual learner's family.** The ethics lens: fairness asks whether the sharing is
just, not just whether it is orderly. The egalitarian lens: the whole point is to notice the people
the arrangement forgets — the quieter, the poorer, the newcomer, the one whose needs are invisible.
The global & anthropological lens: different communities share differently — a commons, a council,
a market, a state — and each has its own pattern of who is left out; compare without ranking. The
technology & environment lens: technology can widen or narrow the gap (who can pay for data, who can
reach the well or the clinic) — ask how tools change access here. The critical-thinking lens:
separate *individual* actions from *structural* causes — one person's choice is not the same as a
system that sorts who gets what.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In Grade 6 you asked why some people have more than they need while others
   lack. Say to a partner: what did you learn was the bigger cause — a person's choices, or the
   structures around them? Today we analyze one community in detail.
2. **Meet the four questions (10 min).** Look at the resource-sharing map and the question set.
   Pick one shared resource in a community. Ask four questions: **Who decides? Who receives? Who is
   not at the table? Why?** The fourth question is the important one — it usually points to a
   structure, not a person.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a community be analyzed: *A school has one after-hours study room,
   and only the sports teams get keys.* Who decides? The staff who run the teams. Who receives? The
   team members. Who is not at the table? Students who work after school, or who care for siblings,
   or who were never invited to join a team. Why? The room's schedule was built around one group's
   needs without asking the rest. Notice: nobody here is evil — the *arrangement* is what leaves
   people out.
4. **Analyze in groups (20 min).** Choose a community you know — your school, neighborhood, or an
   invented one. Pick one resource (a space, a schedule, a budget, information, a voice in
   decisions). Answer all four questions. Then write one sentence naming *who is left out and why*.
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one finding. Remember: seeing who is left out is not blaming — it is
   the first move toward a fairer arrangement.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the map with "who decides" and "who receives" pre-filled for one resource,
  and a sentence starter for the "why" ("They are left out because the arrangement ___").
- **Extension:** Add the "missing voice" column — name who is not at the table and write one thing
  that would change if they were invited in.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner answer all four questions for one resource and name
  who is left out and why?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed resource-sharing map and the "who is left out and why"
  sentence, kept in the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, notice one shared resource (time, money, space, the last word in decisions). Ask quietly:
who decides, who receives, who is not at the table — and why? You do not have to announce the
answer; noticing is the practice.
