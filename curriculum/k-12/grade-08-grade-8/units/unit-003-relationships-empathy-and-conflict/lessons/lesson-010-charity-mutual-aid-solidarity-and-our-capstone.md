# Lesson 10 — Charity, Mutual Aid, Solidarity — and Our Capstone

## Summary

The unit capstone. Learners distinguish **charity** — a one-way gift in which the giver keeps the
power and the receiver stays passive — from **mutual aid and solidarity**, in which people meet one
another's needs as equals, sharing both the giving and the deciding. Then they retrieve the unit's
eight ideas, revisit the wonders they wrote in Lesson 1, and assemble a personal **commitment card**
to carry forward into how they live with others.

## Objectives

- Distinguish charity from mutual aid and solidarity, and explain the difference. _(D02.S4.08.02)_
- Synthesize the unit's eight goals into one view of how to communicate, understand, belong, and
  care — and turn that view into a personal commitment.
  _(D02.S1.08.01, D02.S1.08.02, D02.S2.08.01, D02.S2.08.02, D02.S3.08.01, D02.S3.08.02, D02.S4.08.01, D02.S4.08.02 — synthesis)_

## Connection

For ten days you have studied how we communicate, understand, belong, and care. One last question
pulls it all together: when someone needs help, there are two very different ways to give it.
**Charity** is a gift from above — the giver keeps the power and decides, the receiver is expected
to be grateful and stay quiet. **Mutual aid** is different: people who share a situation meet one
another's needs *together*, as equals, each giving what they can and deciding together — and
**solidarity** is the bond that makes them stand with one another, not just for one another. Today
you learn that difference, and you write down the kind of person you want to be with others.

## Materials

- Charity-vs-mutual-aid chart
- Capstone commitment card

## Preparation

- Copy the charity-vs-mutual-aid chart and the capstone commitment card.
- Return each learner's Lesson 1 unit map and wonder slips.
- Retrieval: this lesson is itself a **spaced, interleaved retrieval pass** across the whole unit
  (Dunlosky et al. 2013, S-039) — it works because learners must *recall*, not re-read.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) charity and mutual aid both
help, but they are not the same: charity is a one-way gift in which the giver keeps the power and
the receiver stays passive and often must prove need; mutual aid is reciprocity among equals, where
power and dignity are shared, and solidarity is the commitment to stand with others rather than
only for them (Dean Spade, S-345; Kropotkin, S-163); (2) the difference is not "charity is bad" —
it is that charity alone can leave inequality and the power imbalance untouched, while mutual aid
aims to share power too; (3) the unit's eight ideas are one thing — living well with others — and
the capstone turns them into a personal commitment.** Model the chart once (S-011). **Hold the
value gently and without shame: a genuine gift is not wrong; the question is whether our helping
also asks who decides and who is treated as an equal.** This is a **summative** moment, but mastery
is personal, not ranked — celebrate growth, never compare learners. The ethics lens: how we help
can honor or wound dignity. The egalitarian lens: the deepest test is whether the power is shared,
not just the goods. The global & anthropological lens: mutual aid and solidarity have long histories
in many places — from communal work (harambee, gotong royong, mink'a/minga, barn-raising) to
movements of people standing together — and giving itself has many distinct traditions, held side by
side, never as one correct way. Several traditions frame giving as a duty or a form of justice rather
than a condescension from above: zakat and sadaqah in Islamic practice, tzedakah in Jewish tradition,
and dāna and sevā in South Asian traditions. So "charity" is not one universal thing — the simple
one-way contrast here is a starting lens to think with, not a verdict on any tradition's way of
giving; invite learners to notice how the place they live names and practices giving. The technology & environment
lens: tools can serve charity or mutual aid — a donation app versus a shared network of neighbors —
and the same fairness questions from Lesson 9 apply. End on hope and agency, never despair: the
learner is leaving with something they can *do*.

## Procedure

1. **Recall the whole unit (10 min).** With a partner, recall the eight ideas: how tone and body
   change meaning; how digital messages lose cues; how to facilitate toward a solution all can
   accept; taking perspectives you don't share; questioning bias and stereotype; who is left out in
   power and resources; strengthening belonging; and systems of care and mutual aid. Name them
   without looking.
2. **Meet charity vs. mutual aid (15 min).** Look at the chart. **Charity**: one-way gift; the
   giver keeps the power and decides; the receiver is expected to be grateful and quiet, and may
   have to prove they are poor enough. **Mutual aid**: people in the same situation meet one
   another's needs together, as equals; each gives what they can; decisions are shared; dignity is
   kept. **Solidarity**: the bond of standing *with* others, not only *for* them. Both help — but
   only one also shares the power.
3. **Model it (5 min).** Watch the difference be shown: *A storm floods a street.* Charity: a
   distant donor sends boxes of what they think is needed. Mutual aid: the neighbors meet, share
   who has water, who has a dry room, who can cook, and decide together what to do — and they stand
   with the people downstream who were hit hardest. Notice: the second one shares the deciding, not
   just the goods.
4. **Revisit your wonders (5 min).** Open your Lesson 1 map and wonder slips. Which wonders can you
   now answer? Which have grown into new questions? Answer one you can, and mark one to keep
   wondering about.
5. **Build your commitment card (15 min).** Write six lines: **(1)** one communication habit you
   will keep (tone, body, or digital); **(2)** one facilitation move you will use; **(3)** one
   perspective you will seek out; **(4)** one bias you will keep questioning; **(5)** one belonging
   action you will do; **(6)** one act of care, in the spirit of mutual aid, not only charity.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one line you are proud of. Remember: **living well together is not a
   lesson you finish — it is a practice you carry.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each line ("One communication habit I will keep is
  ___ because ___").
- **Extension:** Add a "who benefits" line and a "what I will check back on in a month" line to
  your commitment.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio + self):** The completed charity-vs-mutual-aid chart and the commitment
  card are the unit's capstone artifacts, kept in the portfolio.
- **Self-assessment:** Learners mark one line they feel confident explaining and one line they want
  to keep learning about — "not yet" is information, never a verdict.

## Home connection

Share your commitment card with someone at home, and invite them to add one line of their own —
one way they will help, in the spirit of mutual aid rather than only charity.
