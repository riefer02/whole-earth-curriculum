# Lesson 3 — The Screen Between Us: How Digital Messages Change Meaning

## Summary

Learners discover that **digital messages remove most of the tone and body cues** that carry
meaning face to face, which makes short messages easy to misread in more than one way. They learn
to **notice the missing cues, check before assuming, and write clearer, kinder messages**.

## Objectives

- Analyze how digital messages can change meaning when tone and body language are missing.
  _(D02.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

A friend sends one word: "whatever." In the room with you, their shrug and smile would tell you it
is a joke; their crossed arms and hard voice would tell you it is anger. On a screen, the shrug and
the hard voice are gone — only the word remains, and it can mean several opposite things at once.
From a city apartment to a village where a shared phone is the family's, the screen sits between
people and hides the cues we rely on. Today you learn to read through that screen with care.

## Materials

- Digital-message card
- Cue chart

## Preparation

- Copy the digital-message card and cue chart.
- Retrieval: recall Lesson 2's finding — meaning rides on tone and body, not only words. Today the
  screen strips those away.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) digital messages remove
most nonverbal cues — tone, face, gesture, and immediate back-and-forth — so the same words can be
read several ways (reduced-cues communication; Suler 2004, S-343); (2) because the cues are gone,
misreading is common, not a personal failure — the skill is to notice and check, not to assume; (3)
the screen can also loosen self-restraint (Suler's "online disinhibition," S-343), so a pause before
sending is a form of care.** Model the decoding once as a worked example (S-011). **Safety: use only
invented, neutral messages; do not ask learners to share real, painful threads.** **Accessibility:**
this lesson's object is the written word, so learners who are deaf or hard of hearing meet it on
equal terms — the "missing cues" are named and written, never only heard. For learners who are
blind or low vision, read the messages aloud or offer large-print cards, and decode by speaking the
possible meanings rather than writing. For learners who cannot easily write by hand, decode aloud,
point to a cue on the chart, or answer with a device. The "said aloud versus written" comparison in
the outdoor variant should be offered visually or by touch (a written note passed hand to hand) for
learners who do not use a hearing voice. The ethics lens: a
message that is meant kindly but read harshly can still cause real hurt — so senders and readers
both have a responsibility. The egalitarian lens: digital access is not equal — some people share a
device, have expensive data, or cannot read and write the same language comfortably; the "text
thread" is not everyone's default. The global & anthropological lens: the same short message is read
through different languages, cultures, and norms, which multiplies the ways it can be misread. The
technology & environment lens: the screen is a technology that reshapes relationships — we use it,
so we must learn its risks as well as its gifts. The critical-thinking lens: for any ambiguous
message, list the possible readings *before* you choose one, then check.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what two things beside words carry meaning in a conversation?
   Say them to a partner. Today: what happens when a screen removes them?
2. **Build the cue chart (8 min).** Face to face, you get tone, face, gesture, and instant
   back-and-forth. On a screen, those cues are missing. Fill the cue chart: *what I have face to
   face* versus *what is missing on a screen*.
3. **Meet the digital-message card (10 min).** Read the message: **"whatever."** With no tone and
   no body, it could be agreement, anger, or indifference — three opposite meanings. The screen did
   not remove the words; it removed the cues that told you which meaning was meant.
4. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a decoding: *"you there?"* — Is it worry ("are you okay?"), need
   ("I need you now"), or annoyance ("why aren't you answering?")? Notice: the reader cannot know
   alone; the responsible move is to check — "what did you mean by that?" — not to assume.
5. **Practice in pairs (12 min).** Decode a second message. List at least two possible meanings.
   Then write one clearer version that a reader could not easily misread, and one question you
   could ask the sender to check. Swap and compare.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one rule for clearer, kinder messages. Remember: when the cues are
   gone, checking is stronger than guessing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a list of possible meanings to choose from before decoding, and a
  sentence starter for the check ("What did you mean when you wrote ___?").
- **Extension:** Take a real, neutral digital exchange and rewrite one message so its meaning is
  unambiguous without adding emoji.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the cues a screen removes and give at least two
  possible readings of an ambiguous message?
- **Performance:** The pair practice, observed for whether the learner checks rather than assumes.

## Home connection

At home, notice one message someone sent or received today. Ask (yourself or them): which cues were
missing, and what could it have meant besides what was assumed?
