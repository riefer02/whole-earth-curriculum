# Lesson 5 — One Conflict, Many Sides: Taking Perspectives I Don't Share

## Summary

Learners learn to analyze a conflict from **multiple perspectives, including ones they disagree
with**, by mapping each party's **view, needs, and strongest evidence**. They practice
reconstructing a view they do not share "charitably" — as its holder would state it — before
evaluating it, discovering that **understanding is not the same as agreeing**.

## Objectives

- Analyze a conflict from multiple perspectives, including ones they disagree with. _(D02.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

Two neighbors argue about a tree that shades one garden but drops leaves into the other. Each is
sure they are right — and each is partly right. A conflict almost always has more than one side,
and the sides you do *not* share are the ones you understand least. Seeing a disagreement from
every angle is not a way of giving in; it is how you come to understand it fully enough to judge it
fairly. Today you practice seeing through eyes that are not your own.

## Materials

- Conflict scenario
- Perspective map

## Preparation

- Copy the conflict scenario and perspective map.
- Retrieval: recall Grade 7's work on where a point of view comes from (background, culture,
  experience) and on empathy across difference. Today you turn that into a structured analysis of a
  conflict.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a conflict has parties, and
each party has a view (what they think), needs (what they want or fear losing), and evidence (the
strongest reasons for their view); (2) analyzing means reconstructing each party's case as its
holder would state it — "charitably" — before evaluating; (3) understanding a view is not agreeing
with it.** Perspective-taking is a well-studied route to prosocial behavior and reduced aggression
(Decety & Cowell 2014, S-231); model the map once as a worked example (S-011). **Safety: use
invented conflicts, and do not press learners to "defend" a real view that harms them or others;
the skill is analysis, not endorsement.** The ethics lens: fairness requires hearing the strongest
version of every side, not a straw-man. The egalitarian lens: in a conflict, some parties have more
power or louder voices — mapping every side gives the quieter parties their turn on the page. The
global & anthropological lens: the same conflict would be read differently in different places and
traditions — name whose perspective is still missing from the map. The technology & environment
lens: conflicts now also happen on screens and across distances, where the parties never meet — the
same mapping works, with extra care from Lesson 3. The critical-thinking lens: separate the task of
*understanding* a view from the task of *evaluating* it — you may understand fully and still
disagree.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In Grade 7 you learned that a point of view comes from background, culture,
   and experience. Say to a partner: what shapes your own view of a disagreement? Today we map
   everyone's.
2. **Meet the perspective map (10 min).** Look at the map. In the center is the **conflict**.
   Around it, for each **party**, write three things: their **view** (what they think), their
   **needs** (what they want or fear losing), and their **strongest evidence** (their best reason).
   Include the side you disagree with — that is the whole point.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a conflict be mapped: *A shared courtyard is wanted by one group
   for a football game and by another for a vegetable garden.* Party one: view "we need space to
   play"; need — exercise, belonging; evidence — many children, no other flat space. Party two: view
   "we need space to grow food"; need — food, family income; evidence — rising prices, sunny soil.
   Notice: each side's strongest case is real, even though they clash.
4. **Analyze in groups (20 min).** Take an invented conflict. Fill the map for every party. Then
   each learner writes one paragraph stating — as fairly as they can — the strongest case for the
   view they *least* agree with. Read it to the group. Then, and only then, evaluate: where is each
   side's evidence strong, and where is it weak?
5. **Close (5 min).** Share what changed when you stated the other side well. Remember: you can
   understand a view fully and still disagree — but now your disagreement is informed.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a two-party starter map with the views pre-filled, and a sentence frame
  ("The strongest thing this side can say is ___").
- **Extension:** Add a fourth "missing party" to the map — someone affected but not named — and
  reconstruct their case too.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name each party's view, needs, and evidence,
  including for the side they disagree with?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed perspective map plus the "strongest case for the view I
  disagree with" paragraph, kept in the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, take a disagreement (about chores, plans, or a shared thing) and state out loud the
strongest case for the *other* person's view before saying your own. Notice what happens.
