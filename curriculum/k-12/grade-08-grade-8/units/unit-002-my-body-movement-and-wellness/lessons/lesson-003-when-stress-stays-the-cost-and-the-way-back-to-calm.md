# Lesson 3 — When Stress Stays: The Cost and the Way Back to Calm

## Summary

Learners describe **how stress affects the body over time** — a short burst is a useful alarm that
rises and falls, while stress that never turns off wears on the body — and **name healthy ways to
help the body return to calm**, practicing one slow-breathing technique.

## Objectives

- Describe how stress affects the body over time and name healthy ways to help the body return to
  calm. _(D03.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

Some stress is a gift — it sharpens you before a performance, wakes you to a real danger, then
fades. But some stress does not fade: a worry that follows you to bed, a pressure that sits in your
shoulders for weeks. That kind of stress is like an alarm that never stops ringing, and a body
holding that alarm for too long pays a real price. Today you learn the difference — and how to help
your body come back to calm.

## Materials

- Two-panel stress sheet
- Calm-practice cards

## Preparation

- Copy the two-panel stress sheet and calm-practice cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the two branches — **fight-or-flight** (sympathetic) and
  **rest-and-digest** (parasympathetic). Today we ask what happens when the alarm branch stays on.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) acute stress — a short
burst — is normal and useful: it sharpens attention and readies the body, then passes; (2) chronic
stress — stress that does not pass — wears on the body over time: tense muscles, a racing heart,
upset digestion, broken sleep, and low mood (S-338); (3) the body can be helped back to calm with
healthy practices — slow breathing, movement, sleep, connection with others, time in nature, and
talking to someone — and these are skills, not weaknesses.** Model the slow-breathing technique
once (worked example, S-011), then practice together. The scientific account of chronic stress
(S-338) is held alongside cultural breathing practices from many peoples (pranayama, qigong, and
others) without ranking them (philosophy §11). The ethics lens: caring for a stressed body is a
form of responsibility toward oneself. The egalitarian lens: stress does not fall evenly — poverty,
violence, discrimination, and unsafe places add stress that is not anyone's fault, and no one
should be blamed for being stressed. The global lens: every culture has its own ways of settling
the body — breath, prayer, song, water, tea, rest — all worth honoring. The technology & environment
lens: constant alerts, screens before sleep, and a lack of green or safe space can keep the alarm
on; naming these is the first step to choosing differently. **Safeguarding:** some learners carry
heavy stress; do not require disclosure — invite optional sharing, and remind learners a trusted
adult is always a resource. Keep hope central: the lesson ends on agency, not on the weight of
stress.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what are the two branches of the automatic response? Today we
   ask what happens when the alarm branch stays on.
2. **Meet the two kinds of stress (10 min).** **Acute stress** is a short burst — it rises to meet a
   challenge and then fades. That is the alarm working as it should. **Chronic stress** is stress
   that does not turn off. Over time it wears on the body: muscles stay tight, the heart works
   harder, digestion and sleep are disturbed, and mood dips. The same alarm that saves you in a
   moment costs you when it never stops.
3. **Name the ways back to calm (15 min).** With a group, go through the calm-practice cards:
   **slow breathing**, **movement**, **sleep**, **connection** (a friend, a trusted adult),
   **nature**, and **talking to someone**. For each, say one reason it helps the body settle.
4. **Practice slow breathing (15 min).** Sit comfortably. Breathe in slowly for a count of four,
   and out slowly for a count of six — the long, slow exhale tells the body it is safe. Breathe
   through your nose or mouth, whichever is comfortable. Repeat four times. A leader may count with
   a slowly rising and falling hand so learners who are deaf or hard of hearing follow the pace
   visually, and learners may keep their eyes open and simply follow the hand. Notice your
   shoulders, your jaw, your hands.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: **stress that passes is an ally; stress that stays needs tending —
   and you know ways to tend it.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a single calm practice to try and a sentence starter ("When I feel stressed,
  one thing I can do is ___").
- **Extension:** Write a short "calm menu" for yourself, pairing a stress signal you notice (racing
  heart, tight jaw) with a practice that helps it settle.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner tell acute stress from chronic stress and name at
  least two healthy ways to return to calm?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed two-panel stress sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Try one slow-breathing round with someone at home before bed. Ask them what they do to settle when
life feels loud, and add it to your own list.
