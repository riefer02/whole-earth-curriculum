# Lesson 2 — Fight, Flight, or Rest: How My Nervous System Senses and Responds

## Summary

Learners explain the **basic role of the nervous system in sensing and responding**. They trace a
signal from the senses to the brain and spinal cord and out to the body, and name the two branches
of the automatic response — **sympathetic** (fight-or-flight) and **parasympathetic**
(rest-and-digest) — sorting body changes into the right branch.

## Objectives

- Explain the basic role of the nervous system in sensing and responding, such as the fight,
  flight, or rest response. _(D03.S1.08.01)_

## Connection

A door slams behind you and you jump before you even think. Your heart thuds, your breath
quickens, your muscles tighten — in under a second, your body has decided you might be in danger
and readied you to fight or run. A few minutes later, curled up safe, your breath slows and your
shoulders drop. That switch — from alarm to calm — is your nervous system doing its job. Today you
trace how it works.

## Materials

- Fight-flight-rest diagram
- Response cards

## Preparation

- Copy the fight-flight-rest diagram and response cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall the **internal body signals** you learned to notice (D03.S1.07.01
  — hunger, fatigue, stress). Today we add the machinery underneath those signals: the nervous
  system.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) the nervous system senses
(senses pick up information), sends it to the brain and spinal cord, and responds by changing the
body; (2) the automatic response has two branches — sympathetic (fight-or-flight: faster heart and
breath, tensed muscles, wide pupils, digestion paused) and parasympathetic (rest-and-digest: slower
heart and breath, relaxed muscles, digestion resumed); (3) both are healthy and necessary — danger
needs alarm, safety needs calm; neither is "bad."** Model the signal path once as a worked example
(S-011): sense → brain/spinal cord → response. The scientific account (S-339, S-338) describes the
sympathetic and parasympathetic branches; hold it as a well-supported scientific story, and note
that many cultures have long described the body's alarm and calm in their own words (philosophy
§11) — held side by side, not ranked. The ethics lens: knowing our own alarm is the first step to
meeting it kindly. The egalitarian lens: bodies sense and respond in varied ways — some nervous
systems alarm more easily, and that is a fact about a person, never a fault. The global lens: every
human body shares this wiring, whatever the place or culture. The technology & environment lens:
loud screens, alerts, and unsafe places can keep the alarm switched on — a preview of Lesson 3.
The critical-thinking lens: "the brain readies the body before conscious thought" is a checkable
  scientific fact (S-339), not a guess. **Safeguarding:** never deliberately startle a learner to
  demonstrate this; use recalled examples and the diagram instead. **Accessibility:** the startle
  example can be told in any mode — a sudden movement, a bright light, or a tap on the shoulder — so
  learners who are deaf or hard of hearing follow it as fully as those who hear a bang; and the
  diagram's meaning lives in its labels and in your spoken trace of the path, so a learner who
  cannot see the diagram can follow the same signal path in words, by sorting the response cards
  aloud, or with a tactile two-column chart.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: what internal body signals could you name? Today we look at
   the system underneath them.
2. **Meet the nervous system (8 min).** Your nervous system has a sensing side and a responding
   side. Senses — eyes, ears, skin, nose, tongue — pick up what is around you. Nerves carry that
   message to your **brain** and **spinal cord**, which decide, and send a response back out.
3. **Watch it modeled — a signal's path (12 min).** Trace one example: you hear a sudden loud
   bang. **(1) Sense:** your ears pick up the sound. **(2) Send:** nerves carry it to the brain.
   **(3) Decide:** the brain reads it as possible danger. **(4) Respond:** the **sympathetic**
   branch switches on — heart beats faster, breathing quickens, muscles tense, pupils widen, and
   digestion pauses. Your body is ready to **fight or flee**. Later, safe again, the
   **parasympathetic** branch takes over — heart and breath slow, muscles relax, digestion
   resumes. That is **rest-and-digest**.
4. **Sort the responses (15 min).** With a group, sort the response cards into two columns:
   fight-or-flight, or rest-and-digest. For each card, say what the body is doing and why it helps
   (alarm or calm).
5. **Close (10 min).** Feel your own pulse and breath — if finding your pulse is hard, simply
   notice your breath rising and falling. Sit still for one slow minute and feel them again.
   Remember: **your body has two gears — alarm and calm — and both keep you alive.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-labeled two-column chart and two cards; sort and match them to the
  branch.
- **Extension:** Explain why the same signal (a loud sound) can trigger fight-or-flight one moment
  and, once you recognize it as harmless, rest another.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner trace a signal's path (sense → brain/spinal cord →
  response) and sort body changes into fight-or-flight or rest?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed fight-flight-rest diagram, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

With someone at home, recall a moment you were startled and a moment you felt completely calm.
Which branch — alarm or calm — was switched on in each?
