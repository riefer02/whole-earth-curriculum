# Lesson 6 — Tobacco & Nicotine: What They Do to the Body and Brain

## Summary

Learners explain **how tobacco affects the body and brain** — nicotine reaches the brain quickly and is
highly addictive, and tobacco smoke harms the lungs, heart, and blood vessels — and practice making a
**safe, informed choice**, including clear refusal skills and the truth that most teens do not smoke.

## Objectives

- Explain how tobacco and nicotine affect the body and brain. _(D03.S4.08.01)_
- Practice making a safe, informed choice about tobacco, including how to refuse without shame.
  _(D03.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

Tobacco has been grown and used by humans for thousands of years — chewed, smoked, and offered in
ceremony — but its health effects are now among the best-understood facts in medicine. When tobacco
burns or is vaped, it delivers a chemical called **nicotine** to the brain within seconds. That speed is
why nicotine is so habit-forming. Today you learn what tobacco does to a body, and how to choose for
yourself with clear eyes.

## Materials

- Tobacco-effects sheet
- Choice-and-refusal cards

## Preparation

- Copy the effects sheet and refusal cards; preview any video before showing it.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall evaluating health claims and telling marketing from evidence; from
  Grade 6, recall assessing everyday risks.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It opens the unit's substance strand, and the tone is
everything: **factual, calm, and non-shaming — never moralizing, never fear, and never insulting people
who use tobacco.** The facts to land: **(1) nicotine reaches the brain in seconds and is highly
addictive (S-374); (2) tobacco smoke harms the lungs, heart, and blood vessels, and is a leading
preventable cause of death worldwide (S-374); (3) second-hand smoke harms the people around the smoker,
too (S-374); (4) the developing adolescent brain is especially sensitive to nicotine, so delaying use
is protective.** The critical-thinking lens is central: connect to Grade 7's marketing literacy — the
tobacco and vaping industries have a long history of designing products and ads to make use look
glamorous and universal; learners already know how to tell claims from evidence. The WHO tobacco
page documents this directly (tobacco advertising, promotion and sponsorship "increases and sustains
tobacco use by effectively recruiting new tobacco users" — S-374); S-312 is the Grade-7
food-marketing-literacy frame that taught the tell-claims-from-evidence skill itself. Deliver the **norms correction** that prevention science supports: *in most
places, the large majority of teenagers do not smoke or vape* — "everyone does it" is a myth, and
saying so is itself protective (S-374; frame as a general, hedged truth). The egalitarian lens: tobacco
companies have historically marketed hardest to particular groups and to lower-income countries; the
burden of tobacco falls unequally, which is a justice matter. The global lens: tobacco's harms are
global, and many countries now restrict tobacco advertising and raise the age of sale — and this lesson
honors Indigenous and ceremonial uses of tobacco as distinct from commercial smoking, without ranking
them. The technology & environment lens: tobacco is also a story of tools and land — new devices such as vapes are a technology engineered to deliver nicotine quickly, and growing tobacco and discarding its waste leave a footprint on the environment — so the choice is never only personal. The ethics lens: a safe, informed choice protects our own body and the people who breathe the air
around us. Teach **refusal as a skill** (worked examples: "No thanks, I don't smoke"; "I'm good — my
lungs are for running"; walk away; suggest something else), and always point to a trusted adult for
help. A learner who has already tried tobacco is never shamed — they are met with facts and support.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how do you tell a health claim from a marketing claim? Today we use
   that skill on tobacco.
2. **Meet nicotine (10 min).** Tobacco contains **nicotine**, a chemical that reaches the brain in
   seconds and is **highly addictive** — the body comes to want it more and more (S-374).
3. **Map the effects (10 min).** On the tobacco-effects sheet, label the body: **brain** (addiction),
   **lungs** (damage and disease), **heart and blood vessels** (strain). Note that **second-hand smoke**
   harms people nearby too (S-374).
4. **The real norm (5 min).** Here is a truth worth holding: in most places, the large majority of
   teenagers do **not** smoke or vape. "Everyone does it" is a myth — and knowing that makes it easier
   to choose for yourself (S-374).
5. **Practice refusal (15 min).** With your cards, role-play an offer and a refusal. Try: a clear "no
   thanks," a reason that is true for you, walking away, and suggesting something else to do. Switch
   roles.
6. **Close (5 min).** You have facts and words, not fear. Next: we meet alcohol.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each effect to a body part with a partner, and practice one simple refusal line.
- **Extension:** Explain why nicotine's effect on a still-developing adolescent brain makes *delaying*
  use protective, in your own words.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two body effects of tobacco and give one
  clear refusal response?

## Home connection

Ask a trusted adult at home what they wish they had known about tobacco when they were your age. Listen;
you do not need to share your own thoughts unless you want to.
