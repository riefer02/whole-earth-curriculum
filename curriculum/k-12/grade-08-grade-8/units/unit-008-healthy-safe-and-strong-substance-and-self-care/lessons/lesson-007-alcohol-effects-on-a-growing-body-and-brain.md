# Lesson 7 — Alcohol: Effects on a Growing Body and Brain

## Summary

Learners explain **how alcohol affects a growing body and brain** — it depresses the central nervous
system, slowing judgment and coordination, and the adolescent brain is especially vulnerable — and
weigh scenarios to make **safe, informed choices**, including the protective power of delaying drinking.

## Objectives

- Explain how alcohol affects the body and brain, especially a still-developing one. _(D03.S4.08.01)_
- Make a safe, informed choice about alcohol in a realistic scenario. _(D03.S4.08.01)_

## Connection

Alcohol has been part of human life for thousands of years — in food, medicine, and celebration — but
the science is equally old in one clear finding: it changes how the brain works, and it affects a young,
still-growing body differently than an adult's. Alcohol is not a food and not a toy; it is a **depressant**
— a substance that slows the brain down. Today you learn exactly how, and how to choose safely.

## Materials

- Alcohol-effects sheet
- Scenario-and-decision cards

## Preparation

- Copy the effects sheet and scenario cards; preview any video before showing it.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall how nicotine reaches the brain; from Grade 7, recall first aid and
  when to get adult help.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The tone must stay **factual, calm, and non-shaming —
never moralizing, never fear, and never judgment of families or cultures where alcohol is present.** The
facts to land: **(1) alcohol is a psychoactive substance that depresses the central nervous system — it
slows brain activity, which is why it affects judgment, coordination, reaction time, and memory (S-375);
(2) because a young person's body is smaller and their brain still developing, alcohol affects them
more, and early drinking is linked to greater later harm — so delay is protective (S-375); (3) alcohol
harms the liver and other organs with heavy, repeated use, and impairs the ability to judge danger
(S-375).** Do not present a single drink as inevitably harmful or a sip as a catastrophe — that would be
inaccurate and would cost the lesson its credibility with teens. Instead: the *developing* brain and the
*legal age* are the anchors. Nearly every country sets a minimum age for buying and drinking alcohol
(safety law, not a test of maturity), and the standard's own language is "safe, informed choices." The
critical-thinking lens: connect to Grade 7's marketing literacy — alcohol advertising associates
drinking with glamour and belonging, which is a claim to read, not to swallow. The egalitarian lens:
alcohol's harms fall unequally — on poorer communities and on those marketed to most — a justice matter.
The global lens: cultures hold alcohol very differently, from total avoidance to everyday moderation;
this lesson honors all of them and ranks none. The technology & environment lens: alcohol also travels through tools and landscapes — it is promoted through the same screens and media the learner reads critically, and producing it uses land and water — so the choice touches more than the drinker alone. The ethics lens: a safe choice protects our own body and
the people around us (drinking and driving or other risk puts others in danger). Teach **decision-making
as a skill** (worked example: notice the situation → name the risk → choose a safe response → know who
to call). Always include the **harm-reduction safety floor without endorsing use**: if someone has drunk
too much and becomes unresponsive, this is an emergency — call for adult or medical help immediately;
never leave an impaired person alone. That is knowledge that saves a life, and it is not the same as
approving. A learner who has already tried alcohol is met with facts and care, never shame.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: how did nicotine reach the brain? Today we meet a different
   substance that works another way.
2. **Meet alcohol (10 min).** **Alcohol** is a **depressant** — it slows the central nervous system.
   That is why it changes **judgment**, **coordination**, **reaction time**, and **memory** (S-375).
3. **Map the effects (10 min).** On the alcohol-effects sheet, label the body: **brain** (slower
   judgment, coordination, memory), **liver** (harm from heavy use), **a growing body** (smaller body,
   developing brain = greater effect). Mark why **delay** protects a young brain (S-375).
4. **The legal age (5 min).** Nearly every country sets a minimum age for alcohol. It is a safety law,
   not a test of maturity — and it exists because a young body is more vulnerable.
5. **Weigh a scenario (15 min).** With your cards, work one scene: alcohol is offered or present. Answer
   three questions — what could go wrong? What would a safe choice look like? Who could you ask or call?
   Practice saying your choice out loud.
6. **Close (5 min).** You now know the facts and the safety floor: if someone ever drinks too much and
   won't wake up, that is an emergency — get adult or medical help, and never leave them alone. Next: we
   meet medicines and other drugs.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each effect to a body part with a partner; write one sentence of a safe choice.
- **Extension:** Explain, in your own words, why delaying drinking protects a developing brain — and
  name the legal drinking age in your country.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two effects of alcohol and describe one
  safe, informed choice in a scenario?

## Home connection

Talk with a trusted adult at home about how your family or community views alcohol. You do not need to
agree; the goal is to understand the range of views — and to know one adult you could call if you ever
needed help.
