# Lesson 7 — Writing in Many Forms With My Own Voice

## Summary

Learners write in **three forms** — **narrative**, **argument**, and **informational** — with
coherent organization and their own voice. They work a one-topic-three-forms model as a worked
example, then draft one form on a topic of their own, attending to organization and voice.

## Objectives

- Write in multiple forms — narrative, argument, and informational text — with coherent
  organization and voice. _(D04.S3.08.01)_

## Connection

You already move between forms without thinking about it. You tell a friend *what happened*
(narrative); you persuade someone *to agree* (argument); you explain *how something works*
(informational). Writing does the same three jobs, with more care. Each form has its own shape
and its own voice — and the shape and the voice are *yours*, not copied. Today you practice all
three, so you can choose the right form for the job, in your own words, in your own language.

## Materials

- Three-forms card
- One-topic-three-forms model

## Preparation

- Copy the three-forms card and the one-topic-three-forms model.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6 and 7, recall writing arguments with claim and reasons (D04.S3.06.01)
  and developing ideas with precise language (D04.S3.07.01). Today we move *across* forms.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) three forms do three
jobs — narrative tells what happened (beginning, middle, end), argument makes a case (claim,
reasons, close), informational explains (topic, facts, organization); (2) organization is the
form's skeleton, and voice is the writer's own sound; (3) form is a choice, and the learner is
the chooser.** Model the one-topic-three-forms set once (worked example, S-011). The ethics
lens: choosing the right form for the audience is a matter of honesty — a report should not
pretend to be a story, an argument should not hide its claim. The egalitarian lens: every learner
has a voice worth hearing; writing in one's home language is welcome, and voice is not the same
as "correctness." The global lens: forms vary across cultures — a story, a case, and a report
look different in different traditions — and all are legitimate. The social-emotional lens: voice
is the self in the writing; encourage learners to sound like themselves. Keep the topic low-stakes
and real so voice can come through without fear.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the parts of an argument you built in Grade 6? Today we write
   in *three* forms — and choose the one that fits the job.
2. **Meet the three forms (8 min).** **Narrative** tells what happened, with a beginning, a
   middle, and an end. **Argument** makes a case, with a claim and reasons. **Informational**
   explains, with a topic and organized facts. Each has a shape; each carries your voice.
3. **Watch it modeled (15 min).** Here is one topic — a flooded river — three ways.
   **Narrative:** "That morning the river rose over the stones, and Mira watched her family's
   small boat lift with it." **Argument:** "The village should move the path to higher ground,
   because the river floods every rainy season and the low path is no longer safe." 
   **Informational:** "Each rainy season, the river rises and covers the low path; the high path
   stays dry because it sits above the flood line." Same topic, three shapes, three jobs.
4. **Write your own (22 min).** Choose a topic you know well — a place, a change, a choice.
   Pick one form. Draft a short piece: for narrative, a beginning, middle, and end; for argument,
   a claim and two reasons; for informational, a topic and three organized facts. Read it back
   and ask: does it sound like *me*?
5. **Close (5 min).** Share a first line. Remember: **the form is the shape; the voice is
   yours.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence starter for each form ("That day, ___", "We should ___
  because ___", "___ is ___ and it ___").
- **Extension:** Write the same topic in a second form and explain in one sentence how the
  organization and voice change.
- **Expression (UDL):** A learner may dictate, record, sign, or tell their piece to a scribe
  instead of writing it — the *organization* (beginning–middle–end, claim–reasons, topic–facts)
  can be shown by speaking, drawing, or gesture. Voice lives in spoken and signed language too,
  not only on the page.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner write a short piece in a chosen form with clear
  organization (beginning–middle–end, claim–reasons, or topic–facts) and a recognizable voice?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I choose the form on purpose, and does my writing sound
  like me, not like a copy?"

## Home connection

Choose one small thing from your day and tell it to someone at home three ways — what happened,
why something should change, and the plain facts — and notice which form they remember best.
