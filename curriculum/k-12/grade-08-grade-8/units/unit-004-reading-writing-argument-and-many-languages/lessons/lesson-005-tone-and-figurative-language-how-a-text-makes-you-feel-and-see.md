# Lesson 5 — Tone and Figurative Language: How a Text Makes You Feel and See

## Summary

Learners learn four common **figures of speech** — **metaphor**, **simile**, **personification**,
and **hyperbole** — and practice naming a text's **tone** and explaining how figurative language
shapes what a reader understands and feels. They work one pair of lines as a modeled example,
then analyze a second pair.

## Objectives

- Analyze how tone and figurative language shape a reader's understanding of a text.
  _(D04.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

People do not say "I am hopeful." They say "hope is a seed," or "hope is a candle in the dark."
Figurative language does in one picture what plain words do slowly — it makes you *see* and
*feel* an idea, not just understand it. And every text has a **tone** — a mood in the words,
like warmth or sharpness in a voice. Today you learn to name both, so you can say not only *what*
a text means but *how* it makes you feel that meaning.

## Materials

- Tone-and-figure card
- Poem or line pairs

## Preparation

- Copy the tone-and-figure card and line pairs.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how an author's word choice built meaning (D04.S3.07.01,
  D04.S2.07.01). Today we name the *figures* and the *tone* behind that meaning.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) figurative language says
one thing by way of another — metaphor (implicit comparison), simile (comparison with "like/as"),
personification (human traits to non-human things), hyperbole (deliberate exaggeration); (2) tone
is the attitude or mood the words carry; (3) both shape what a reader understands and feels.**
Model the first pair once (worked example, S-011). These are standard literary terms (S-347). The
ethics lens: figurative language can lift people up or cut them down — a metaphor can wound or
heal, so we choose figures with care. The egalitarian lens: figurative language is everywhere, in
every language and every social class — it is not "fancy" writing; it is human thinking. The
global lens: different languages make different pictures ("it's raining cats and dogs" is one
language's picture; others say "it's raining ropes" or "raining pitchers") — no language's
figures are more correct. The social-emotional lens: naming tone is naming feeling, in a text and
in ourselves. Invite learners to share figures from their own languages as gifts.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What did precise word choice do in your Grade 7 writing? Today we name
   the *pictures* words make and the *mood* they carry.
2. **Meet the figures and the tone (8 min).** A **metaphor** says one thing *is* another ("hope
   is a seed"). A **simile** says one thing is *like* another ("hope is like a seed"). 
   **Personification** gives a non-human thing human traits ("the wind whispered").
   **Hyperbole** exaggerates on purpose ("I've told you a thousand times"). **Tone** is the
   feeling the words carry — hopeful, bitter, playful, solemn.
3. **Watch it modeled (15 min).** Here is one idea two ways. "She was very tired and sad." And:
   "Grief sat on her shoulders like a coat of stones, and each step asked more than her legs
   could give." The second uses a **simile** ("like a coat of stones"), **personification**
   ("each step asked"), and its **tone** is heavy and tender. It does not just say she is sad —
   it makes you *feel* the weight.
4. **Analyze a second pair (15 min).** With a partner, take a line pair. Name the figure in
   each, name the tone, and say how each shapes what you understand and feel.
5. **Close (7 min).** Share one figure you found. Remember: **figures make the abstract
   seeable; tone makes it feelable.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a two-choice label ("metaphor or simile?", "hopeful tone or sad tone?")
  for each line.
- **Extension:** Write one line of your own using a metaphor or personification, then name the
  tone it carries and explain the effect.
- **Expression (UDL):** Figurative language can be *heard, felt, and acted*, not only seen — a
  metaphor is a word-picture that lives in sound, touch, and movement. Read the line pairs aloud
  and let learners render a metaphor through gesture, sound, or spoken image instead of sight.
  Answers can be spoken or dictated to a scribe.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the figure and tone in two lines and explain
  how each shapes understanding and feeling, with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed line-pair analysis, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a saying or proverb they grew up with. Name the picture it makes and the
tone it carries, and share one figure from your own language.
