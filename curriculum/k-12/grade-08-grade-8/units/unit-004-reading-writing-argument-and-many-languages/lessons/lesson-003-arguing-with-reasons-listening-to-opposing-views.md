# Lesson 3 — Arguing With Reasons, Listening to Opposing Views

## Summary

Learners practice **structured deliberation**: arguing a position with reasons and evidence while
**truly listening to the other side** — paraphrasing it before responding. They learn a four-step
flow, watch it modeled, and then hold a short, low-stakes deliberation in two sides.

## Objectives

- Engage in a structured debate or deliberation, arguing a position with reasons while listening
  to opposing views. _(D04.S1.08.02)_

## Connection

Disagreement is ordinary — in a family, a market, a neighborhood, a nation. What is rare, and
learnable, is disagreeing *well*: making your case with reasons and then *really* hearing the
other side before you answer. Arguing well is not about winning; it is about deciding together
with more of the truth in the room. Today you practice the skill that lets many voices make one
fairer decision.

## Materials

- Deliberation flow card
- A shared question

## Preparation

- Copy the deliberation flow card.
- Choose a low-stakes, fair, real question the group can genuinely disagree about.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6 and 7, recall your argument scaffold — claim, reasons, evidence, and
  response to a counterargument (D04.S3.06.01; D04.S3.07.01). Today you *speak* it and *listen*
  to the other side.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) deliberation is arguing a
position with reasons *and* listening to the opposing view — not just waiting to talk; (2) the
four-step flow — state position, give reasons, paraphrase the other side, respond — keeps it
fair; (3) "listening is not losing" — hearing the other side is how we decide together.** Model
the flow once with a learner or co-facilitator (worked example, S-011). Structured discussion of
contested issues is a core democratic skill with a real research base (Hess 2009, S-349); the
lesson holds "listening is not losing" as a value, distinct from that descriptive claim. The
ethics lens: how we disagree is a matter of right and wrong — we can argue without wounding. The
egalitarian lens: deliberation only works when every voice can speak; ask "whose view is not in
the room, and why?" The global lens: cultures deliberate differently — some speak in turn with a
talking object, some seek consensus, some vote; this is one fair structure among many. The
community lens: this skill builds the shared decisions that hold a community together. Choose a
question where learners can genuinely disagree without fear (a park vs. a clinic, not a question
that divides the group along identity lines). Watch that quieter learners get the floor.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the parts of an argument you built in Grade 6 — claim, reasons,
   evidence, counterargument? Today you speak it out loud, with a partner who disagrees.
2. **Meet the four-step flow (8 min).** Deliberation is four moves: **(1) State your position.
   (2) Give your reasons and evidence. (3) Listen and paraphrase the other side — say it back in
   your own words until they agree you heard them. (4) Respond — agree, partly agree, or
   respectfully differ, with reasons.**
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Watch a short example. One voice: "I think we should build the
   park first, because children need a safe place to play, and the clinic is farther from most
   homes." The other voice: "So you are saying play space matters more to you right now — did I
   hear you?" Then: "I hear that. I think the clinic should come first, because people who are
   sick must travel far for care." Notice: no one wins; both are now clearer.
4. **Hold your deliberation (25 min).** In two sides, take the shared question. Use the flow.
   Paraphrase before you respond. Aim to understand the other side well enough that they say,
   "Yes, that is what I mean."
5. **Close (7 min).** Share one thing the other side said that you had not thought of. Remember:
   **listening is not losing.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("I hear you saying ___, is that right?", "One reason I
  think ___ is ___").
- **Extension:** Add evidence — find one fact that supports your side and say where it came from,
  then listen to the other side's evidence fairly.
- **Expression (UDL):** A learner may state a position and paraphrase the other side by signing,
  writing, pointing to a card, or using a communication device or partner — a silent or
  non-speaking voice is a full voice in the circle. "Listening" includes watching lips, hands,
  and written turns; anyone can pass rather than speak.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/performance):** Can the learner state a position with at least one
  reason, and paraphrase the opposing view accurately before responding?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I say back the other side's view until they agreed I had
  heard them — before I gave my own answer?"

## Home connection

Next time you and someone at home disagree about something small, try the flow: say your reason,
then say theirs back to them, then respond. Notice what changes.
