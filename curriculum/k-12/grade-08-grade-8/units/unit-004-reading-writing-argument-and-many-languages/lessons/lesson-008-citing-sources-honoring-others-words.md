# Lesson 8 — Citing Sources, Honoring Others' Words

## Summary

Learners learn to **cite sources accurately** and to **distinguish their own words and ideas from
those of others** — by quoting with quotation marks, paraphrasing in their own words, and
labeling which is which. They work a three-way model as a worked example, then practice on one
short passage.

## Objectives

- Cite sources accurately in writing and distinguish their own words and ideas from those of
  others. _(D04.S3.08.02)_

## Connection

Every idea you have stands on ideas others had first — a fact from a book, a number from a
lesson, a story from a grandparent. Using those ideas is good and normal; the honesty is in
*saying whose they are*. When you copy words without credit, you take something that is not
yours; when you cite, you honor the person who gave you the idea and let your reader check it.
Today you learn the small marks and habits that keep your writing honest.

## Materials

- Quote-paraphrase-own card
- A short source passage

## Preparation

- Copy the quote-paraphrase-own card and the source passage.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall gathering information from several sources and writing in your
  own words (D04.S3.07.01). Today we make the attribution *explicit*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) citing means saying where
words and ideas came from; (2) three moves — quote (exact words in quotation marks, with
attribution), paraphrase (the idea in your own words, still attributed), and your own idea (no
attribution needed) — and the learner must label which is which; (3) copying words or ideas
without credit is dishonest, whatever the language.** Model all three once (worked example,
S-011). The ethics lens is this lesson's heart: attribution is honesty toward the people whose
thinking we build on. The egalitarian lens: the right to be credited belongs to everyone — a
scholar in a library and a grandparent in a village are both sources worthy of credit. The global
lens: every culture has its own customs of giving credit and passing knowledge (oral traditions
name the teller, as griots do); citing is one custom among many, and its purpose — honesty — is
universal. The critical-thinking lens: a citation lets a reader check the claim — which is how
knowledge stays honest. Use a low-stakes, real passage so the skill is practiced without fear.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** How did you gather information from sources in Grade 7? Today we make one
   thing explicit: *whose* words and ideas are these?
2. **Meet the three moves (8 min).** **Quote:** copy the exact words in quotation marks and name
   the source ("As Mira wrote, 'the river rose at dawn.'"). **Paraphrase:** put the idea in your
   own words and still name the source ("Mira explains that the river flooded in the morning.").
   **Your own idea:** your own thought, no quotation marks, no source ("The flood changed how the
   village planned its paths."). Label each — that is the honesty.
3. **Watch it modeled (12 min).** Here is a source passage: "Each rainy season, the river rises
   and covers the low path." Watch three moves. **Quote:** The report says, "the river rises and
   covers the low path." **Paraphrase:** According to the report, the low path floods every rainy
   season. **My own idea:** Moving the path higher would keep people safer. Each line is clearly
   marked.
4. **Practice (20 min).** With a partner, take a short source passage. Write one **quote** (with
   quotation marks and attribution), one **paraphrase** (your own words, still attributed), and
   one **your own idea** (labeled as yours). Then check each other's work: did each line say
   whose words they are?
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: **credit is honesty, not weakness.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three moves with blanks to fill ("___ wrote, '___'", "According to
  ___, ___", "In my view, ___").
- **Extension:** Build a two-entry reference list — author, title, where it came from, date — and
  check that each entry has all its parts.
- **Expression (UDL):** Quoting, paraphrasing, and labeling can be done aloud — say the quote, say
  the paraphrase, and name whose words they are — with a scribe or recording capturing it. The
  honesty of *saying whose words they are* is the skill; quotation marks are one way to show it,
  not the only one.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner quote a passage with quotation marks and
  attribution, paraphrase it in their own words with attribution, and label which line is their
  own idea?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could a reader tell, for every line I wrote, whose words and
  ideas they are?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one thing you learned today and say where you learned it — naming the
source aloud. Notice how natural giving credit feels once you try it.
