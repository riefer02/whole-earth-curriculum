# Lesson 6 — Texts From Many Cultures and Times

## Summary

Learners read three short texts from different cultures and time periods and **compare how each
reflects its context** — its form, its subject, and the culture and time that made it. They work
the three texts as modeled examples, then bring a text from their own culture to the same lens.

## Objectives

- Read texts from multiple cultures and time periods, comparing how each reflects its context.
  _(D04.S2.08.02)_

## Connection

A poem is not written in the air; it is written by a person in a place and a time, and it carries
the shape of that place and time. A short verse that watches a frog jump into an old pond comes
from a culture that treasures a single, still instant. A line about longing comes from a
tradition of mystic poetry. A story kept by word, passed from grandparent to grandchild, comes
from a people who hold their history in memory. Reading across cultures and times, you learn to
see the *person and the world* behind every text — and to see your own texts the same way.

## Materials

- Three-text card set
- Context comparison page

## Preparation

- Copy the three-text card set and context comparison page.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6 and 7, recall reading across genres (D04.S2.06.02) and how an author's
  position shapes a text (D04.S2.07.01). Today we read *across cultures and times*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) every text reflects the
culture and time that made it — in its form (haiku, mystic verse, oral epic), its subject, and
its purpose; (2) comparing texts across cultures and times is how we hear many human voices, not
one; (3) no culture's texts are "higher" — each is a window into its world.** The three texts are
real and well documented: Bashō's frog haiku (Japan, 1680s, S-351); Rumi, the 13th-century
Persian poet (S-352); and the Sundiata epic, kept by West African griots (S-269). The global
lens is this lesson's heart — read widely, across the whole human family. The ethics lens: read
each text with the respect it asks; do not flatten it into a stereotype. The egalitarian lens:
texts survive because people keep them — oral traditions kept by memory are as much literature
as printed books, and the lesson says so plainly. The social-emotional lens: these are texts of
feeling — a pond, a longing, a people's memory — and feeling travels across time. Home language
first: invite learners to share a text from their own culture in their own language, and treat
each speaker as the expert on their own text. If you do not know a language or text, be humble;
let those who know it teach.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What genres did you read in Grade 6 and 7 — fiction, nonfiction, poetry,
   drama? Today we read three short texts from very different cultures and times, and ask what
   each reflects about its world.
2. **Meet the three texts (15 min).** Read each aloud, slowly.
   - **Bashō (Japan, 1680s):** "Old pond — / a frog jumps in — / the sound of water." A *haiku*:
     three brief parts, one instant, one small creature. It reflects a culture that finds the
     whole world in a single, quiet moment.
   - **Rumi (Persia, 1200s):** "Listen to the reed flute, how it tells a tale, complaining of
     separation." A *mystic's verse*: longing, music, the soul's journey. It reflects a tradition
     of Sufi poetry in which poems are also prayers.
   - **The griot (West Africa, oral tradition):** "I am a griot; it is I, Djeli Mamoudou Kouyaté,
     who speak. I teach kings the history of their ancestors, so the lives of the ancients may
     serve them as an example." A *keeper of memory*: history held by word and passed down. It
     reflects a culture where the spoken word carries a people's past.
3. **Compare for context (15 min).** For each text, ask three questions: **What is its form?**
   (haiku, verse, oral epic.) **What is its subject?** (an instant, a longing, a people's
   memory.) **What does that form and subject reflect about the culture and time that made it?**
   Record on your page.
4. **Bring your own (10 min).** Share a short text from your own culture — a proverb, a line, a
   song — in your own language if you wish. Name its form, its subject, and one thing it reflects
   about where and when it came from.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: **every text is a window into the world that made it.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three questions with two choices each ("short form / long form,"
  "about nature / about longing / about memory") and ask learners to match.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth text from a culture or time of your choice and write one sentence on
  how its form and subject reflect its context.
- **Expression (UDL):** The three texts are already read aloud, so learners who read print slowly
  can simply listen; those who write with difficulty can speak their comparison to a scribe or
  into a recording. The three-panel image is described in its alt text and in the lesson body.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name, for each text, its form and subject and one
  way it reflects its culture and time, with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed context comparison page, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a text they grew up with — a proverb, a song, a prayer, a story — and
find one thing its form or subject reflects about the time and place it came from.
