# Lesson 4 — Point of View: Whose Eyes Am I Seeing Through?

## Summary

Learners learn three common **points of view** — **first person**, **third person limited**, and
**third person omniscient** — and analyze how the choice of narrator shapes what a reader
understands. They work one two-view story pair as a modeled example, then compare points of view
in a second pair.

## Objectives

- Analyze how point of view shapes a reader's understanding of a text. _(D04.S2.08.01)_

## Connection

The same event looks different depending on whose eyes you see it through. A rainy day is a
disaster to the child planning a picnic and a gift to the farmer whose fields are dry. Every text
chooses a point of view — and that choice quietly decides what you are allowed to know, and what
is kept from you. Today you learn to notice whose eyes you are seeing through, and what the view
shows and hides.

## Materials

- Point-of-view card
- Two-view story pair

## Preparation

- Copy the point-of-view card and two-view story pairs.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how an author develops characters and plot (D04.S2.07.01).
  Today we zoom in on the *lens* — point of view.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) point of view is whose
eyes and mind the reader sees through — first person ("I"), third person limited (inside one
character's thoughts), third person omniscient (inside all characters' thoughts); (2) the choice
of point of view shapes what the reader knows, suspects, and feels; (3) no point of view is
"better" — each reveals and hides.** Model the first pair once (worked example, S-011). These are
standard literary terms (S-348). The ethics lens: a story can be told to hide a voice — ask
"whose point of view is missing, and what would they say?" The egalitarian lens: whose eyes get
to tell the story is a question of power; many great stories center voices that were once left
out. The global lens: storytelling traditions around the world choose different points of view —
some tell through a collective "we," some through a single "I," some through an all-knowing
teller. The social-emotional lens: stepping into another's point of view is the same move as
empathy — and it is a muscle, not a gift.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** How did you see characters and plot develop in a Grade 7 text? Today we
   ask a sharper question: *whose eyes* are you seeing through?
2. **Meet the points of view (8 min).** **First person** uses "I" — you are inside one teller.
   **Third person limited** uses "she/he/they" but shows only one character's thoughts. **Third
   person omniscient** shows all characters' thoughts. Each is a lens: each reveals and hides.
3. **Watch it modeled (15 min).** Here is one event, two ways. First person: "I watched the
   ball sail over the fence and felt my stomach drop — I was the one who kicked it." Third
   person limited: "Amara watched the ball sail over the fence; her stomach dropped. Across the
   yard, the old gardener looked up, but what he thought, no one could say." The first puts you
   inside the kicker's fear; the second keeps the gardener a mystery. Same event, different
   knowing.
4. **Compare a second pair (15 min).** With a partner, take a two-view story pair. Name the
   point of view in each, then say what each version lets you know — and what it hides.
5. **Close (7 min).** Share one thing the second point of view let you know that the first did
   not. Remember: **the lens chooses the view.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two labels to choose from ("the teller says 'I'" / "the teller says
  'she/he/they'") and ask which each version uses.
- **Extension:** Rewrite one paragraph from a *third* point of view — an omniscient narrator, or
  a different character's "I" — and write one sentence on what changes.
- **Expression (UDL):** The two-view pair can be read aloud by a partner, a recording, or
  text-to-speech and analyzed by listening — "point of view" is simply *whose mind you are
  inside*, with no eye or seeing metaphor required. Anyone who cannot see the diagram can have it
  described; the lesson text already names the three points of view in words.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify the point of view in two versions and say
  what each reveals and hides, with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed two-view comparison, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Think of one small thing that happened today. Tell it first from your own eyes, then from the
eyes of someone else who was there. Notice what changes.
