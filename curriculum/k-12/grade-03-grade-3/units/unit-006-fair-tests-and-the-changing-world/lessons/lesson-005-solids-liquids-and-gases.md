# Lesson 5 — Solids, Liquids, and Gases

## Summary

Everything around us that takes up space is **matter**, and matter comes in three
**states**. A **solid** keeps its shape; a **liquid** takes the shape of its container
and **flows**; a **gas** spreads out to fill the space around it. Learners observe a
stone, water, and air, sort examples, and describe each state in their own words —
seeing that the same world can be firm, flowing, or invisible.

## Objectives

- Describe the three states of matter (solid, liquid, and gas) and how heating and
  cooling change them. _(D06.S3.03.01)_

## Connection

Look around you right now. The floor and your chair are **solid** — they keep their
shape. The water you drink is a **liquid** — it pours and takes the shape of your cup.
And the air you are breathing is a **gas** — you cannot see it, but it is there, filling
the whole room. Three states of the same stuff, and you are touching all three at once.
Wherever you live — by a river, in a desert, on a mountain — you meet solids, liquids,
and gases every day.

## Materials

- A solid, a liquid, and a gas to observe (a stone or ice cube, water in a cup, air in a
  clear bag)
- Two clear containers of different shapes
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather the three examples and the two differently-shaped containers.
- Fill a clear bag with air and seal it so learners can see and feel that a gas takes up
  space.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Introduce the three states with a **worked
example** — hold up the stone, the water, and the air bag and say each state's rule once
— then let learners sort with a partner (guided practice). A common misconception is that
gas is "nothing"; emphasize gently that air is matter — it takes up space, and a sealed
bag of air is the proof a child can hold. Keep examples global: ice and snow (solid) on a
mountain, river water (liquid), the wind and breath (gas) — the same three states appear
everywhere. Note honestly that some "in-between" things (sand, jelly, clay) can be
tricky to sort, and that is fine — classification is a tool, not a test. This lesson
pairs with Lesson 6, where heating and cooling move matter between states.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** What is everything around you made of? Scientists
   call it **matter**. Matter comes in three **states**. Can you name one?
2. **Meet the three states (15 min).** Feel a **solid** — it keeps its shape. Pour a
   **liquid** — it takes the shape of its cup and flows. Feel the sealed bag of **gas**
   — you cannot see the air, but it is there, filling the space. Say the rule for each:
   solid keeps its shape; liquid flows and takes the container's shape; gas spreads out
   to fill the space.
3. **Sort and describe (15 min).** Look around you and sort things into **solid**,
   **liquid**, and **gas**. Name one of each and say why it belongs there.
4. **Draw the three states (10 min).** Draw one solid, one liquid, and one gas, and label
   each with its rule.
5. **Close (5 min).** One world, three states: firm, flowing, and filling the air. You
   are breathing matter right now.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort three clear examples (stone, water, air) with a partner and say each
  name.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the solid, the liquid, or the gas when named; a
  partner says the rule.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the stone (solid), the water (liquid), and the
  moving air of a fan or breath (gas), and describe each by touch.
- **Support (motor):** Direct a partner to pour the water and hold the air bag while you
  name each state and its rule.
- **Extension:** Find one "tricky" thing (sand, jelly, honey) and explain which state it
  is and why it is tricky.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three states and say one rule
  for each?
- **Self:** The learner checks their drawing: "Did I show all three states?"

## Home connection

At home, find one solid, one liquid, and one gas and tell a grown-up the rule for each.
Your breath is the gas — prove it by fogging a spoon or a mirror.
