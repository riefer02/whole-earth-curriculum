# Lesson 7 — Push and Pull: Forces and Motion

## Summary

A **force** is a **push** or a **pull**, and a force changes **motion**. Learners push
and pull a toy car, a found object, and each other's hands, and name the five ways a
force changes motion: it can **start**, **stop**, **speed up**, **slow down**, or
**turn** (change direction). They feel the invisible hand — force — that moves their
world.

## Objectives

- Explain that a push or pull (force) changes an object's motion, and describe
  friction. _(D06.S3.03.02)_

## Connection

Kick a ball and it rolls — that is a **push**. Open a drawer and it slides toward you —
that is a **pull**. A **force** is any push or pull, and a force always does one of five
things: it makes something **start**, **stop**, **speed up**, **slow down**, or **turn**.
You use forces all day, everywhere — carrying water, rolling a tire, throwing a seed,
pulling a fish from a line. The same force moves a bicycle in any city and a cart in any
village.

## Materials

- A toy car or ball
- A soft rope or cloth
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Have a toy car or ball and a soft rope ready per pair.
- Clear a safe space for pushing, pulling, and a gentle tug-of-war.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea is simple and physical, so let
learners *do* it — but teach the five motion changes explicitly first with a **worked
example** (push the car: "it started and sped up; now I stop it"), then let pairs
explore (guided practice). Frame force as neutral and useful — forces are not violent;
the same push that throws a ball also holds a door open gently. Keep it safe and
cooperative: the tug-of-war is gentle and even, and learners feel both the push and the
pull rather than competing. This lesson sets up Lesson 8 on friction — for now, hold the
question "why does the car eventually stop on its own?" open, and let it be answered
tomorrow. Include every body: forces can be felt and described by any learner, seated or
standing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** What makes things move? Try to move a toy car without
   touching it. It will not move. A **force** — a **push** or a **pull** — is what
   changes its **motion**.
2. **Meet the five changes (10 min).** Watch the worked example: push the car — it
   **starts** and **speeds up**; catch it — it **stops**; pull it gently back — it
   **slows down**; nudge it from the side — it **turns**. Say the five: start, stop,
   speed up, slow down, turn.
3. **Push and pull (15 min).** With a partner, push the car, pull it, turn it, stop it.
   Push your partner's hands, pull them gently with the rope. Each time, say what the
   force did: "My push started it." "My pull stopped it."
4. **Draw a force (10 min).** Draw one push and one pull from your day, and write or
   draw what each force changed.
5. **Close (5 min).** A force is a push or a pull, and it changes motion. You are a force
   user — you push and pull your world into motion all day long.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Push and pull a toy with a partner and say just "started" or "stopped."
- **Support (non-speaking):** Act out the push or pull; a partner says the motion
  change.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a partner's push and pull on your hands and name
  what each one did to your own motion.
- **Support (motor):** Direct a partner to push and pull the toy while you name each
  motion change — start, stop, speed up, slow down, or turn.
- **Extension:** Find one force that does three of the five changes at once (a rolling
  ball that starts, turns, and speeds up) and describe it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner push and pull an object and say what each
  force changed (start, stop, speed up, slow down, or turn)?
- **Self:** The learner checks their drawing: "Did I show what my force changed?"

## Home connection

At home, find one push and one pull you do every day — closing a door, drawing a pail of
water, pushing a broom. Tell a grown-up what each force changes.
