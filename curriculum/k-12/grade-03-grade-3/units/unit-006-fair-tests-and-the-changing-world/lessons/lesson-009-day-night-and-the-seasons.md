# Lesson 9 — Day, Night, and the Seasons

## Summary

Why is it light when you wake and dark when you sleep? Earth **spins** — **rotates** —
once each day, so the side facing the Sun has **day** and the side turned away has
**night**. And Earth **travels** — **orbits** — the Sun once each year, tilted a little
as it goes, and that tilt makes the **seasons**. Learners model it with a ball and a
light, and discover that when it is day for you, it is night for someone on the other
side of the world.

## Objectives

- Explain how Earth's rotation causes day and night and how its orbit causes the
  seasons. _(D06.S4.03.01)_

## Connection

Every morning the Sun seems to rise, and every evening it seems to set. It is not the
Sun moving — it is **you**, riding a spinning Earth. One whole **turn** is a **day**:
the side facing the Sun is in daylight, and the side turned away is in night. And as
Earth slowly **orbits** the Sun over a whole year, tipped a little to one side, the
Sunlight falls higher or lower, and the world warms into summer and cools into winter.
Right now, on the other side of the planet, someone is sleeping in the night while you
are awake in the day.

## Materials

- A ball to be the Earth and a lamp or bright spot to be the Sun
- A marked stick or pencil through the ball
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Set up the lamp (or use a sunny window) as the "Sun" and have the ball ready.
- Mark a dot on the ball as "you," so learners can watch it move into light and dark.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Model day and night concretely first —
turn the ball and point to the lit and dark halves — then name **rotate** and **orbit**
(concrete before abstract). Be careful with a common misconception: learners may think
the Sun moves around us; gently correct with the spinning-ball model, saying "the Sun
seems to move because we turn." For seasons, the **tilt** is the key idea at this level
— keep it simple and honest, not exhaustive. Hold the big story with care (§11): the
spinning Earth and orbiting seasons are what **scientists tell from evidence** gathered
over centuries; other cultures have told the story of the Sun and Earth in their own
beautiful ways, and both can be honored — science is one way of knowing, not the only
one. Keep the global lens: day length and seasons differ with latitude — near the
equator the seasons barely change, and far north or south the days grow very long or
short. No fear — this is the steady, reliable rhythm of our home.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Where does the Sun go at night? It does not go
   anywhere — the **Earth** is doing the moving. Today you will see how.
2. **Day and night (15 min).** Turn the ball slowly beside the light. The side facing
   the Sun is in **day**; the side turned away is in **night**. Earth makes one full
   **rotation** — one **turn** — every day. Find the dot that is "you" and watch it move
   from light into dark and back. Say it: Earth rotates; that is day and night.
3. **The seasons (15 min).** Now walk the ball in a big circle around the Sun — that is
   Earth's **orbit**, one full trip each year. Earth is **tilted** a little as it goes.
   When your part of Earth is tipped toward the Sun, the Sun sits higher and you get
   summer; tipped away, the Sun sits lower and you get winter. That tilt, not how close
   Earth is, makes the **seasons**.
4. **Draw it (10 min).** Draw the spinning Earth with day on one side and night on the
   other, and draw the tilted orbit with the four seasons.
5. **Close (5 min).** Earth spins and orbits, tilted as it goes — and that is why you
   have morning and night, summer and winter. A steady, spinning home, carrying you
   through light and dark. Scientists tell this story from evidence they gathered over
   many years. People all over the world have also told the story of the Sun and Earth
   in their own beautiful ways, and both can be honored side by side.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Turn the ball with a partner and point to the lit side (day) and dark
  side (night).
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the day side and night side of the ball; a
  partner says the words.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the warm lamp (Sun) on your face, then turn away
  to feel the "night side" of your own body, and describe the difference.
- **Support (motor):** Direct a partner to turn the ball and walk the orbit while you
  point out day, night, and the tilt.
- **Extension:** Explain why it is summer in one hemisphere while it is winter in the
  other, using the tilt.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain (or model) that Earth's turning
  makes day and night and that its tilted orbit makes the seasons?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The labeled day-night-and-seasons drawing.

## Home connection

At home, notice when the Sun rises and sets over the week, or watch the daylight get
longer or shorter through the season. Tell a grown-up what Earth is doing while you
watch.
