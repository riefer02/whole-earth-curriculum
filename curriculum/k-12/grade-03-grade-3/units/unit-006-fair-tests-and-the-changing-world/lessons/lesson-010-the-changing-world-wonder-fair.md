# Lesson 10 — The Changing World Wonder Fair

## Summary

The unit closes where the changing world begins. Learners discover that **wind**,
**water**, and **ice** shape the land slowly over time — rivers carve valleys, waves
wear cliffs, wind piles sand into dunes, and ice scrapes and cracks rock. Then they
gather everything they predicted, tested, recorded, and found across the unit into a
**wonder fair**, share it with one another, and name the wonders they will carry
forward — celebrating how much they have grown as scientists, together, with no
ranking.

## Objectives

- Describe how wind, water, and ice shape the land over time. _(D06.S4.03.02)_
- Review and show learning across all four strands of the unit. _(D06.S1.03.01,
  D06.S1.03.02, D06.S2.03.01, D06.S2.03.02, D06.S3.03.01, D06.S3.03.02, D06.S4.03.01)_

## Connection

Look at a river, a hill, a sandy shore, or a cracked rock. The land looks still, but it
is always, slowly, changing. **Water** carries a river's curve a little deeper each
year; **wind** piles sand into dunes; **ice** freezes in a crack and pries the rock
apart. These forces have been shaping the land for longer than anyone can count. And
that is the whole unit in one idea: the world changes — and you now know how to watch
it, test it, and find out.

## Materials

- Learners' own work from the unit
- A dish of sand or soil and a cup of water
- A display space (table, mat, or wall)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Help learners gather their work from the unit.
- Set up the sand-and-water dish for the land-shaping demonstration.
- Set up a display space and decide how sharing will flow (gallery walk, circle share, or
  wonder walk).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Open with the concrete — pour water over
a mound of sand and watch it carve a little valley (concrete before abstract) — then
name the three shapers: wind, water, ice. Hold the changing land with **hope, not
fear**: erosion is a natural, slow shaping that has always happened; where people worry
about soil washing away, there are real, doable responses (planting roots to hold soil,
terracing, caring for the land), and that is agency a child can hold. The wonder fair
is a **celebration of mastery, not a competition** — no winners, no rankings, no "best"
work; every share is welcomed exactly as it is. Keep praise about effort and wonder,
not comparison. Retrieval is built in: recalling and explaining to a peer is the
deepest kind of remembering, spaced and interleaved across the whole unit. Honor every
body: sharing may be by speaking, pointing, signing, acting, or displaying work. End by
gathering "what do you still wonder?" — and honor that some questions we cannot answer
yet, and that is wonderful.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** The world is always changing. Pour water over the mound of sand
   and watch it carve a little valley. **Water** shapes the land — and so do **wind**
   and **ice**. This has been happening slowly, for a very, very long time.
2. **Meet the three shapers (10 min).** **Water** — rivers carve valleys, waves wear
   cliffs, rain softens the soil. **Wind** — it carries sand into dunes and wears rocks
   smooth. **Ice** — freezing water in a crack pries rock apart, and great sheets of ice
   scrape the land as they move. Name each one and point to a picture or a place you
   know.
3. **Set up your share (10 min).** Gather your work from the unit — your fair test, your
   data table, your food chain, your plant, your states of matter, your forces, your day
   and night. Choose the piece you are most proud of, or the one holding your favorite
   wonder, and put it in the display space.
4. **The Wonder Fair (20 min).** Visit each other's work. When it is your turn, tell (or
   point to or show) two things: one thing you *tested or found out*, and one thing you
   *still wonder about*. Listen with kind ears and ask each other questions.
5. **One more wonder (10 min).** On fresh paper, draw or write your biggest question to
   carry forward: "I still wonder…" Add it to the wonder wall or wonder book.
6. **Close (5 min).** Look at all you did: you changed one thing, read the data, traced
   the food chain, named the plant's parts, watched matter change, felt forces, and
   followed Earth's turning. The world changes — and you know how to wonder, test, and
   find out. Keep wondering.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Choose one piece and share one sentence ("I tested…" or "I found out…")
  with a partner first.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Display work and point to it; a peer or adult reads the
  label aloud.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the sand-and-water valley with your hands, and
  share by telling and listening; a peer describes each displayed piece aloud.
- **Extension:** Write or draw three "I still wonder" questions and star the one you
  most want to explore next.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one land-shaper (wind, water, or
  ice) and one thing they learned and one thing they still wonder across the unit?
- **Self / peer:** Learners tell one another one thing they liked about each other's
  sharing.
- **Portfolio:** The gathered unit work plus the final "I still wonder…" page, as a
  record of growth.

## Home connection

Bring your "I still wonder…" question home and ask a grown-up what *they* wonder about.
Wonder together — and keep watching the changing world around you.
