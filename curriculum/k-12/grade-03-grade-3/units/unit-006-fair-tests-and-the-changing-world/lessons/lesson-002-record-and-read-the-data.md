# Lesson 2 — Record and Read the Data

## Summary

The fair test planned in Lesson 1 is now carried out. Learners observe carefully,
**record** what happens using a simple **table** of marks, drawings, or words, and then
**read the table back** to describe what the data show. They meet the scientist's
honesty: **data are evidence** — what really happened — and describing the data is
telling the truth about it, whether or not it matched the prediction.

## Objectives

- Record observations using drawings, words, or simple tables, and describe what the
  data show. _(D06.S1.03.02)_
- Plan and carry out a simple fair test by changing one thing and keeping the rest the
  same. _(D06.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

You rolled the car three times on the smooth floor and three times on the rough mat.
If you do not write it down, you will only half-remember it. A scientist **records** —
she makes a little table with two columns and puts a **tick mark** each time. At the
end she looks at the marks and says what the **data** show: "The car rolled farther on
the smooth floor every time." That is how a guess becomes something you can trust: you
wrote down what really happened.

## Materials

- The fair test planned in Lesson 1 (or a fresh one everyone can run)
- A simple data table per learner
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Set up the test so everyone can see it and, where possible, run a trial themselves.
- Prepare the data table with two columns ("Smooth" / "Rough") and room for three marks
  each.
- Have a class results chart ready if you will gather results together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The heart of it is the link between
**observing**, **recording**, and **describing**: recording turns a blurry memory into
**data**, and describing the data is where the learning lives. Model the table once
(worked example) — put a tick in the right column, then read it back in a sentence —
then guide pairs, then let learners record alone. Build in retrieval: begin by asking
learners to recall their plan and prediction from Lesson 1. Keep the scientist's honesty
front and center: if the result did not match the prediction, the data still win, and
that is a discovery, not a defeat. A table need not be fancy — marks in dirt, tally
lines, drawings, or words all count as recording. Repeating the trial three times is a
first, gentle step toward "why we test more than once" (spaced for Grade 4).

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Yesterday you planned a fair test. Say it again:
   what is the one thing you change, and what do you keep the same? What did you
   predict?
2. **Carry out the test (15 min).** Do the test. Roll the car three times on the smooth
   floor and three times on the rough mat. **Observe** closely: what happens each time?
3. **Record the data (15 min).** On your table, put a **tick mark** (or a drawing) each
   time. Where did it roll farther? Mark it. Your marks are your **data** — what really
   happened.
4. **Read the data (10 min).** Look at your table. What do the data **show**? Say it in
   your own words: "It rolled farther on smooth every time." "The smooth floor let it
   roll farther." Write or draw one sentence.
5. **Share and close (5 min).** Tell a partner what your data show. Did it match your
   prediction? Whether it did or not, you now know something true — because you recorded
   it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Record together on the class chart; the learner points to the column with
  more marks and says one thing the data show.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Make the marks or point to a drawn picture of the result;
  a partner says the sentence aloud.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a test you can hear or feel (a bell, a sliding
  block) and tally with objects or raised marks, then describe by telling.
- **Support (motor):** Direct a partner to run each trial while you call out where each
  mark goes, then read the table back aloud.
- **Extension:** Record the distances in numbers with a meter stick, and say which
  number is bigger and what that means.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner record at least one mark per trial and
  describe in their own words what the data show?
- **Self:** The learner checks their table: "Did I mark every try?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed data table with the learner's one-sentence
  description.

## Home connection

At home, make a tiny table of something you do three times — how many times you can hop,
or which of two spoons sinks. Record the marks and tell a grown-up what your data show.
