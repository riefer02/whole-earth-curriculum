# Lesson 3 — Who Eats What? A Food Chain

## Summary

Energy for life begins with the **Sun**. Learners follow it through a **food chain**:
plants make their own food from sunlight and are **producers**; animals eat plants or
other animals and are **consumers**; worms, fungi, and tiny living things break down
dead plants and animals and are **decomposers**, returning **nutrients** to the soil.
Learners draw a chain with arrows and label each link, and discover that every link
matters — a decomposer is just as vital as a lion.

## Objectives

- Explain how a food chain links producers, consumers, and decomposers and trace energy
  through it. _(D06.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

Have you seen a bird peck at a seed, or a snail chew a leaf? That is a link in a chain.
The **Sun** gives light, the plant catches that light and makes its own food, the snail
eats the plant, the bird eats the snail, and when any of them dies, worms and tiny
living things break it down and feed the soil — which grows new plants. Energy travels
along that chain, from the Sun to the plant to the eater, and around again through the
soil. You are part of a chain too: whatever you ate today began as a plant or an animal
that ate a plant.

## Materials

- Food-chain picture cards (Sun, grass or rice, an insect or snail, a bird or frog, a
  worm or fungus)
- Paper and crayons
- A length of string or chalk

## Preparation

- Prepare the picture cards and shuffle them.
- Clear a floor or table space, or draw a line outside with chalk, to lay the chain in
  order.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Teach the chain with a **worked example**
first — model Sun → grass → grasshopper → bird → worm, pointing at each card — then let
learners order their own shuffled set (guided practice) before drawing alone. Guard
against ranking: a food chain is a *circle of dependence*, not a ladder of importance;
the worm and the lion are equal partners in keeping the whole alive (the egalitarian and
ethics lenses). Use global examples so no one home is the default — rice and a person,
seaweed and a fish, acacia leaves and a giraffe, maize and a hen. Note gently that
scientists describe this chain from evidence gathered over many years; other traditions
may tell the story of life's interdependence in their own words, and both can be honored
(philosophy §11). Keep it factual and warm, with no fear.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** What did you eat today? Trace it back: did it come
   from a plant, or from an animal that ate a plant? Energy for every living thing
   begins with the **Sun**.
2. **Meet the links (10 min).** A **food chain** has three kinds of links. **Producers**
   are plants — they make their own food from sunlight. **Consumers** are animals — they
   eat plants or other animals. **Decomposers** are worms, fungi, and tiny living things
   that break down dead plants and animals and return **nutrients** to the soil. Say each
   name and point to its card.
3. **Build the chain (15 min).** Lay the cards in order: **Sun → grass → grasshopper →
   bird → worm → soil**. Draw arrows to show the energy moving from one link to the
   next. Check with a partner: where does the energy go at each step?
4. **Draw your own chain (15 min).** On your paper, draw a food chain from your own
   place or from anywhere in the world — a pond, an ocean, a field, a forest. Label each
   link: **producer**, **consumer**, **decomposer**. Show the arrows.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every link matters. Without the plant, the eater has no food;
   without the decomposer, the soil has no food for new plants. It is a circle, and you
   are part of it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Order just three links (Sun, plant, eater) and say them with a partner.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Move the cards into order and point; a partner says each
  name.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a real leaf, a seed, and soil, and order the
  links by touch and description with a partner.
- **Extension:** Build a second chain from a different habitat and tell how the two
  chains are alike and different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order a chain and name (or point to) each
  link as producer, consumer, or decomposer?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The labeled food-chain drawing with arrows.

## Home connection

At home, pick one food you eat and trace its chain back to the Sun with a grown-up.
"Rice ← the plant grew in a field ← the Sun gave it light."
