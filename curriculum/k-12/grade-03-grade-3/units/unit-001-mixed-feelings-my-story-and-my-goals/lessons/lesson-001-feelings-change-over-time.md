# Lesson 1 — Feelings Change Over Time

## Summary

Learners discover that a feeling is not stuck in place. A feeling can **grow** bigger,
**shrink** smaller, or **change** into a different feeling over time. They trace one
real feeling change on a "feeling path" — a winding line with three stops — and learn
that noticing the change helps them remember that even a very big feeling does not last
forever.

## Objectives

- Describe how a feeling can change over time. _(D01.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

Think of the first day of school. In the morning your stomach was fluttery and your
hands were cold — you felt **nervous**. By lunch you were laughing with a friend —
the nervousness had **shrunk**, and you felt **excited**. By the time you walked home
you felt **proud** of yourself for doing it. One day, three feelings, one after the
other. In a fishing village, a child waits out a storm afraid, then feels **relieved**
when the boat comes home, then **glad** at the meal together. Everywhere in the world,
feelings move like weather — they arrive, they shift, and they pass.

## Materials

- Feeling path chart
- Blank feeling path page
- Crayons and pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the feeling path chart (three stops: nervous → excited → proud).
- Copy a blank feeling path page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and a quiet space for drawing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The heart of it is a simple, true idea:
feelings are temporary. Teach the worked example (nervous → excited → proud) aloud and
with your body first, then let each learner trace their own change. Some learners will
choose a change that is not "neat" (mad → madder → calmer, or a feeling that stayed a
long time); honor every shape a feeling takes. Never tell a learner a feeling "should"
have changed by now — the point is *noticing*, not rushing. If a learner names a very
heavy change (grief, fear), stay calm, name the feeling with them, and move gently to
the next step; see `docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned to name look-alike feelings and to notice
   where a feeling lives in your body. Today you learn something new about feelings:
   they **change over time**. They are not stuck.
2. **Meet a worked example (10 min).** Look at the feeling path. It starts at
   **nervous** — wavy eyebrows, fluttery stomach. Then the line climbs to **excited** —
   a big smile, a bouncy body. Then it lands on **proud** — a soft smile, a warm chest.
   One feeling changed into another, bit by bit. A feeling can also **grow** (a little
   worry becomes a big worry) or **shrink** (a big mad becomes a small mad) or **fade**
   (excitement settles into calm).
3. **Trace your own path (15 min).** Think of one real moment from your life where a
   feeling changed. It might be before and after a game, a test, a visit, or a hard
   goodbye. Draw or write your three stops on your path: how you felt **first**, how it
   changed **in the middle**, how you felt **last**. Use the feeling words you know —
   nervous, excited, proud, mad, sad, calm, and more.
4. **Share one change (10 min).** With a partner, tell your path: "First I felt ___,
   then it changed to ___, and last I felt ___." Notice how every path is different —
   that is normal and good.
5. **Close (5 min).** Feelings are like weather: they come, they shift, they pass. When
   a big feeling comes, you can remember: *this will change.* You are not your feeling;
   you are the one watching it move.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a ready-made set of three feeling faces to place on the path
  instead of writing words; scribe the feeling names the learner says.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth stop and name what caused each change ("it changed
  because my friend showed up").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe one feeling that changed over
  time, naming at least two stops in order (first… then… last)?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time their feeling changed — nervous before something, then
different after — and tell them one of yours.
