# Lesson 3 — Advertisement or Information?

## Summary

Learners tell an **advertisement** from **information** and give **one clue** that something
is an ad. An ad asks you to *buy*, shows a *price*, says *"best ever!"* or *"buy now!"*, or
uses exciting words and pictures to **persuade** you to want something. Information tells a
fact and asks for nothing. They learn that advertising is not bad — but *noticing* when a
message wants our money is a power everyone can have.

## Objectives

- Tell the difference between an advertisement and information, and give one clue that
  something is an ad. _(D10.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

Think of a message you saw today — a sign in a shop, a label on a packet, a picture on a
screen. Some messages tell you a true fact: "Brushing your teeth twice a day helps keep
them clean." Other messages want you to buy: "BUY Sparkle Paste — the BEST ever! Only five
coins!" One is information; the other is an **advertisement**. We can tell them apart by
their clues.

## Materials

- Message cards (pairs of an advertisement and an information message)
- A "clues that it's an ad" chart
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare or write message pairs: an information message (a fact, no price) and an
  advertisement (asks to buy, shows a price, says "best ever").
- Prepare the clue chart: *asks you to buy · shows a price · "best ever!" · "buy now!" ·
  exciting words and pictures that make you want it*.
- Set out paper and crayons.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Keep it fair and warm: **advertising is not
bad, and noticing it is not suspicion.** People buy food, soap, and shoes, and sellers help
them find these things. The skill is simply to *notice* when a message wants our money
rather than only telling us a fact. That skill matters everywhere, and it protects families
with little to spend just as much as anyone else — knowing the clue is a kind of fairness.
This is the Grade 3 upgrade of Grade 2's "true information or selling": now learners use the
word **advertisement** and name a **specific clue**. Hold the curriculum's stance: media is
met with judgment, not fear. Use simple, everyday examples with no wealth assumption — a
market stall, a street sign, a label on rice or soap — so every learner recognizes an ad
from their own life. Distinguish evidence from values: "this message shows a price" is a
fact; "ads are bad" is a value judgment we do not make. Honor every body: read each message
aloud for a learner who is blind or has low vision, and let them name the clue by voice; a
non-speaking learner points to "buy," "price," or "fact" on the clue chart; a learner who is
Deaf or hard of hearing can read the printed messages and sign "buy" or "fact" to answer.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Some messages tell you a fact; some want you to buy. How can you
   tell them apart?
2. **Meet the clues (10 min).** An **advertisement** asks you to *buy*, shows a *price*,
   and may say *"best ever!"* or *"buy now!"* or use exciting words and pictures to
   persuade. An **information** message tells a fact and asks for nothing.
3. **Sort the messages (10 min).** For each message card, say which it is and give one
   clue: "This is an ad because it shows a price and says buy now." Or: "This is
   information because it tells a fact and asks for nothing."
4. **Make one of each (12 min).** Write or draw one information message (a fact you know is
   true) and one advertisement (about something you would like to sell), with a price and a
   "buy" word. Label each: *advertisement* or *information*.
5. **Close (3 min).** An advertisement is not bad — but noticing it is a power. A careful
   reader sees the price and the "buy now!" and says: "This is an ad. Do I need this, or am
   I just being asked to buy?"

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort one pair and name one clue together aloud, using the clue chart.
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear each message read aloud and name the clue by voice (price,
  buy, or fact).
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Read the printed messages and point to "buy,"
  "price," or "fact" on the clue chart to answer.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to write and draw while the learner says
  or points to which message is an advertisement and which is information.
- **Extension:** Tell why a family might buy something even when the message is an ad (they
  truly need it), and name one clue that helped you see it was an ad.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner tell whether a simple message is an
  advertisement or information, and give one clue?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "advertisement" and "information" message pair, labeled.

## Home connection

At home, find one message on a packet, a sign, or a screen. Tell a grown-up whether it is an
advertisement or information, and give one clue. Ask the grown-up how they decide when
something is worth buying.
