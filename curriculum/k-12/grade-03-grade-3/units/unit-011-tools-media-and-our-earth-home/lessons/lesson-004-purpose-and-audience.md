# Lesson 4 — Every Message Has a Purpose and an Audience

## Summary

Learners discover that every message has a **purpose** and an **audience**, and they name
both for simple messages. A **purpose** is what the message is for — to *inform*, *persuade*,
*entertain*, or *warn*. An **audience** is who it is for — students, walkers, children,
families. They sort and label messages, then make one of their own with a clear purpose and
audience.

## Objectives

- Explain that a message has a purpose and an audience, and name both for a simple
  message. _(D10.S2.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of a message you saw today — a lunch menu, a sign, a book cover, a poster. Every one
of them was made by a person with two questions in mind: *what is this message for?* and
*who is it for?* The menu informs hungry students; the "cross carefully" sign warns walkers
near the road; the storybook entertains children at bedtime. That is a message's **purpose**
(what it is for) and its **audience** (who it is for). When you can name both, you can read
any message with clear eyes.

## Materials

- Message cards with a purpose and an audience (a menu, a toy ad, a "cross carefully" sign, a storybook)
- A "purpose words" and "audience words" chart
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the message cards: a lunch menu (inform, students), a toy ad (persuade, children),
  a "cross carefully" sign (warn, walkers), a storybook cover (entertain, children).
- Prepare the chart: purpose words (*inform, persuade, entertain, warn*) and audience words
  (*who it is for*).
- Set out paper and crayons.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is the Grade 3 upgrade of Grade 2's
"who made it and who is it for": now learners name the **purpose** (inform, persuade,
entertain, warn) and the **audience** of a message. Model the two questions — *what is it
for? who is it for?* — on one message, then guide practice on a second before learners work
independently (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the media stance warm: reading a
message's purpose and audience is not suspicion; it is understanding — the maker of a menu
wants to help you eat, and the maker of a toy ad wants you to want the toy. Both are
honest once you can see them. Use messages from many places and lives — a market notice, a
road sign, a menu, a story told aloud — so no one home is the default; a spoken story or
song is a real message with a purpose and audience too. Honor every body: read each message
aloud for a learner who is blind or has low vision and let them name purpose and audience by
voice; a non-speaking learner points to "inform," "persuade," "entertain," or "warn" and to
the audience picture; acting out each message works for learners who learn best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Every message was made for a reason and for someone. Today you learn
   to name both.
2. **Meet purpose and audience (10 min).** A **purpose** is what a message is for: to
   *inform*, *persuade*, *entertain*, or *warn*. An **audience** is who it is for. Look at
   the lunch menu: its purpose is to inform, and its audience is students.
3. **Name them together (10 min).** For each card, ask the two questions — *what is it for?
   who is it for?* — and say both: "The toy ad's purpose is to persuade, and its audience is
   children." "The sign's purpose is to warn, and its audience is walkers."
4. **Make your own message (12 min).** Make a simple message of your own — a poster, a
   notice, a story title, a sign. Label its **purpose** (inform, persuade, entertain, or
   warn) and its **audience** (who it is for).
5. **Share and guess (5 min).** Show your message to a partner. Can they guess its purpose
   and audience? Tell them if they are right.
6. **Close (3 min).** When you can name a message's purpose and audience, you can read it
   with clear eyes — no message tricks you, and no message is wasted on the wrong person.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name the purpose and audience of one message together aloud, using the chart
  and the frame "Its purpose is to ___, and its audience is ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear each message described and name its purpose and audience by
  voice; a partner scribes the learner's own message.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Read or see each message and point to the purpose
  word and the audience picture to answer; the learner's own message may be drawn and
  labeled with pictures.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to draw and label while the learner says
  or points to the purpose and audience.
- **Extension:** Make a second message with a different purpose for the same audience, or
  explain how changing the audience would change the message (a "cross carefully" sign for
  drivers instead of walkers).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name both the purpose and the audience of a
  simple message?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's own message, labeled with its purpose and audience.

## Home connection

At home, find one message — a sign, a label, a notice, a song. Tell a grown-up its purpose
(inform, persuade, entertain, or warn) and its audience (who it is for). Ask them who they
think made it and why.
