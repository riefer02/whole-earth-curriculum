# Lesson 5 — Rules and Routines That Work for Everyone

## Summary

Learners see the same shared moment two ways: without a routine, people crowd and
someone is left out; with a routine, everyone takes a turn and no one is left out. They
explain that **rules and routines are not bossy orders — they are how a community makes
things fair for everyone.** Then they test the class's own routines and name how each
one serves the whole. This gathers the year's community work at its grade-3 depth.

## Objectives

- Explain how rules and routines help a community work fairly for everyone.
  _(D02.S3.03.02)_

## Connection

In every community, people need shared things — water, food, a turn to speak, a place
in line, a clean shared space. When there is no routine, the quick and the loud get
there first and someone is left out. When there is a **routine** — take turns, share,
listen, tidy up — everyone gets a turn, and the community works **fairly** for
everyone. All over the world, markets, buses, water pumps, and kitchens run on such
small routines so that everyone is treated fairly. Today you look at how your own class
does this.

## Materials

- A "before and after the routine" chart
- Chart paper and markers
- One shared object (a cup or a ball) to pass in turns

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw the "before and after" chart: one scene of a crowded, unfair moment
  and the same moment with a fair routine.
- Set out chart paper for the class list "what our routines make fair."
- Arrange space to act out a moment with and without a routine.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It spirals up from Grade 2's "welcoming
circle," now into **the *why* of fairness**: a routine is fair when it serves everyone,
not just the fast or the loud. The egalitarian commitment is firm: **a fair routine
includes the ones who are easy to miss** — a learner who moves slowly, who uses a
wheelchair, who is shy, who does not speak the room's main language. Test each routine
against that question: *does this work for everyone, or only for some?* Be tender: some
learners have seen routines used unfairly against them (a rule that favors some over
others). Honor that honestly and turn it into agency — "what would make it fair for
everyone?" A rule is not sacred; a community can change a rule that is not fair. For a
learner who is blind or has low vision, act the scenes out so the difference is felt —
crowding bodies versus a patient line — and describe it aloud. For a learner who does
not use a speaking voice, accept pointing, signing, drawing, or acting out a turn. The
line between "order" and "fairness" is the lesson's heart.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Here is one cup of water and six thirsty people. What happens
   with no routine? Some push, some get there first, and one person is left out.
2. **See the difference (10 min).** Look at the "before and after" chart. Without a
   routine: a crowd, a push, a left-out person. With a routine: a line, a turn for
   each, and everyone drinks. Say what changed. The routine made it **fair**.
3. **Act it out (10 min).** With one shared object, first try to share it with no
   routine — feel the crowding. Then form a circle and pass it, taking turns, so
   everyone gets it. Which felt fair? Why?
4. **Name the "why" (10 min).** Together, finish this sentence: *a rule or routine
   helps a community because it…* (makes sure no one is left out; gives everyone a
   turn; keeps a shared space clean for all; lets everyone be heard).
5. **Test your own routines (10 min).** Look at your class's real routines — lining up,
   sharing materials, taking turns to speak. For each, ask: *does this work fairly for
   everyone?* If one does not, say how you could fix it so it does.
6. **Close (5 min).** A fair community is not an accident. It is built of small
   routines that include everyone — and your class knows how to build them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Act out one routine at a time; offer a sentence starter ("a routine
  helps because…").
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to or act out a fair and an unfair moment; a
  partner voices the "why."
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the difference by acting the scenes out; describe
  the chart aloud.
- **Extension:** Name a rule that is fair for most but not all, and say how to change it
  so everyone is included.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance + observation):** Can the learner explain, in their own
  words or by acting it out, how a rule or routine makes a shared moment fair for
  everyone — and name one way the class's own routine includes people?

## Home connection

Find one routine at home (meals, water, chores) and ask: does it work fairly for
everyone? Tell someone what you noticed, and one small change that would make it fairer.
