# Lesson 2 — Mood in a Picture

## Summary

Learners name the **mood** of an artwork and **point to what in the picture creates
it** — the color, the line, the shape. Then they make a small picture with a chosen
mood. They discover that pictures speak a language of feeling, and that the same
feeling can be shown in pictures made far away and long ago.

## Objectives

- Describe the mood of an artwork and point to what in the picture creates it.
  _(D07.S1.03.02)_

## Connection

A picture can make you feel something before you say a single word — a soft blue
sunset feels calm, a jagged dark storm feels wild, a bright crowded market feels
excited. The feeling a picture gives you is its **mood**, and it does not come from
nowhere: the **color**, **line**, and **shape** in the picture make it. Today you will
look at pictures, name their mood, and point to what makes them feel that way.

## Materials

- A few artworks to look at (a calm scene, an exciting scene, a stormy scene)
- Paper and drawing tools

## Preparation

- Choose two or three pictures with clearly different moods to look at together.
- Have paper and drawing tools ready for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Naming mood and pointing to evidence is a **skill**, so model it
once — "this picture feels calm; the smooth line and light color make it calm" — before
learners try on their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). There is no single
"right" mood; two learners may feel two things about one picture, and both can be true
— ask "what in the picture makes you feel that?" rather than "is that the correct
mood?" A learner who is blind or has low vision reads mood through a **spoken
description** of the color, line, and shape, and through touch (raised-line or textured
versions); they name the mood and point to the described clue. A learner who does not
speak points to a mood word card or to the part of the picture. Mood is a feeling, and
everyone's feeling is welcome.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at the first picture. What feeling rises up in you — calm,
   wild, joyful, lonely, excited? That feeling is the picture's **mood**.
2. **Name the mood (10 min).** Say the mood word, or point to a mood word card. There
   is no wrong answer: if the picture feels calm to you, that is real.
3. **Point to the clue (15 min).** Now look again. What in the picture makes you feel
   that way? Point to it and name it — the **color** (dark and heavy, light and soft),
   the **line** (smooth and curved, sharp and jagged), or the **shape** (round and
   gentle, spiky and sudden). That is your **evidence**.
4. **Make a mood (10 min).** Draw a small picture with a mood you choose — calm, wild,
   excited, quiet. Use color, line, and shape to make it feel that way.
5. **Share and close (5 min).** Show your picture. Tell (or point to) its mood, and
   point to what in it creates the feeling. Remember: pictures speak a language of
   feeling, and the clues are in the color, line, and shape.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer two clear choices ("does this feel calm or wild?") and one clue to
  point to ("point to the dark color").
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Name the mood from a spoken or tactile description
  and point to the described clue; a partner can describe the color and line.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to a mood word card and to the part of the picture
  that gives the feeling.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to name a mood and two or three clues, and to compare
  two pictures with different moods ("this one is calm because the line is smooth; this
  one is wild because the line is sharp").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner name a mood and point to a color, line,
  or shape that creates it?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's mood picture, kept in the portfolio (or a
  photo of it).

## Resources

- No external resources required; the claims in this lesson are about the learner's own
  looking and feeling, which they practice directly.

## Home connection

With a grown-up, look at a picture in a book or on a wall and name its mood. Then each
of you points to the part of the picture that makes you feel that way, and see if you
agree.
