# Lesson 1 — Patterns in Art

## Summary

Learners find **patterns** all around them — in cloth, leaves, shells, and tiles — and
then make an artwork that uses a repeating **shape**, **line**, or **color**. They
discover that a pattern is something that comes back the same way, and that repeating
shapes and lines are a language people everywhere have used to decorate cloth, homes,
and baskets.

## Objectives

- Use a simple pattern or repetition of shape, line, or color in an artwork.
  _(D07.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

Look around you — the weave of a cloth, the ribs of a leaf, the tiles on a floor, the
border of a blanket or a basket. A **pattern** is the same shape, line, or color
coming back again and again. People everywhere have repeated shapes and lines to make
things beautiful: woven cloth in one part of the world, painted borders in another,
carved shells and beads in a third. Today you will make a pattern of your own.

## Materials

- Paper and drawing tools (crayons, colored pencils, or markers)
- A few pattern examples to notice (a cloth, a leaf, a shell, a tile, a printed border)

## Preparation

- Gather a few real objects that show repeating patterns for learners to look at and
  touch.
- Have paper and drawing tools ready for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Making a repeating pattern is a **skill**, so model one aloud
and on the board first — "triangle, triangle, circle; triangle, triangle, circle" —
before learners try their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). A pattern is a
repeat, not a perfect drawing; if the repeat stays the same, it is a pattern. A
learner who is blind or has low vision makes a pattern by **touch and placement**: they
arrange repeated objects (two stones, one leaf; two stones, one leaf) in a row, or
press a repeated shape into clay, and a partner names the sequence. A learner with
limited fine motor repeats with a big sponge stamp, a cut-out stencil, or by directing
a partner who places the shapes. A learner who does not speak points to the elements
and shows the repeat. Every pattern belongs.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at the objects in front of you. Do you see the same shape,
   line, or color coming back again and again? That is a **pattern**. A pattern is a
   **repeat**.
2. **Find patterns (10 min).** Look around the room and out the window. Where do you
   see a repeat — a row of bricks, a stripe in a cloth, the ribs of a leaf? Touch the
   examples and trace the repeat with a finger.
3. **Choose your repeat (10 min).** Pick one thing to repeat in your artwork: a
   **shape** (a circle, a triangle), a **line** (a zigzag, a wave), or a **color**
   (blue, blue, green). Say or point to what you will repeat.
4. **Make your pattern (15 min).** Draw, stamp, or press your repeat across the page —
   the same shape, line, or color coming back the same way. You may alternate two
   things (triangle, circle, triangle, circle) or keep one. Your pattern is yours; no
   one has made exactly this repeat before.
5. **Share and close (5 min).** Show a friend or grown-up your pattern. Point to the
   part that repeats. Remember: a pattern is something that comes back the same — and
   repeating shapes and lines are how people everywhere have made things beautiful.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer one clear repeat ("draw a circle, then another circle, then
  another") and let the learner keep that one pattern going.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Make a pattern by touch — repeated objects in a row
  or a pressed shape in clay — with a partner naming the sequence.
- **Support (fine motor):** Repeat with a big sponge stamp, a cut-out stencil, or by
  directing a partner who places the shapes.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the repeated element and show the repeat with a
  finger.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to alternate two or three elements (triangle, circle,
  square) and to name what comes next in the sequence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner use a repeated shape, line, or color in
  an artwork, and point to the part that repeats?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's patterned artwork, kept in the portfolio (or a
  photo of it).

## Resources

- No external resources required; the claims in this lesson are about the learner's
  own making and about the shared human practice of repeating pattern, which learners
  observe directly.

## Home connection

At home, find a pattern with a grown-up — the weave of a cloth, the stripes of a
blanket, the leaves of a plant. Then make your own small pattern together and point to
the part that repeats.
