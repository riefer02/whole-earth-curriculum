# Lesson 8 — Materials Shape the Making

## Summary

Learners make the same kind of thing with two different sets of **materials** and
notice how the material **shapes** what they can make. They discover that what you can
build depends on what you have — and that this is true everywhere, from grass baskets
to clay pots to stone houses.

## Objectives

- Explain how the materials available shape what can be made, using an example from
  their own making. _(D07.S4.03.02)_

## Connection

A house can be made of mud, wood, stone, ice, cloth, or brick — so houses look very
different around the world, because people build with the **materials** near them. The
same is true of baskets, pots, and toys: grass bends, clay molds, stone stacks, paper
folds. The material you have **shapes** what you can make. Today you will see this with
your own hands.

## Materials

- Two different material sets for the same job (paper and tape; sticks and string; clay)
- A "same job, two materials" chart

## Preparation

- Prepare two (or three) different material sets that can all make the same kind of
  thing — a container, a toy, or a shelter.
- Display or draw the comparison chart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Explaining how materials shape a build is a **skill**, so model
it once — "with paper I can fold a light cup; with sticks I can weave a strong basket;
the material changes what I can make" — before learners try (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Keep the task the same ("make a container") so the only thing changing is
the material — the same fair-test thinking as the rest of the year. The learner's own
example is the point: they must say (or show) how *their* material changed *their*
result. A learner with limited fine motor directs a partner and explains the
difference — deciding and explaining are the heart of the task. A learner who does not
speak points to the material and to the result, or to a word card ("bends," "strong,"
"soft," "stiff"). A learner who is blind or has low vision explores each material by
feel — soft, stiff, bendy, heavy — and names how it shaped the build. Every comparison
belongs.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at your two sets of materials. Today you will make the same
   kind of thing — a container — twice, once with each material, to see how the
   material changes what you can make.
2. **Make with material one (10 min).** Make a container with the first material (say,
   paper and tape). Watch what the material lets you do — fold it, bend it, stick it.
   What did the material make possible?
3. **Make with material two (10 min).** Make a container with the second material (say,
   sticks and string, or clay). What does this material let you do that the first one
   did not? What can it not do?
4. **Compare (10 min).** Put the two side by side. How are they different because the
   materials were different? Use your own example: "with paper I could fold a light
   cup, but with sticks I could weave a strong basket."
5. **Share and close (5 min).** Tell (or point to) your example of how the material
   shaped what you made. Remember: people everywhere build with what they have — grass
   baskets, clay pots, stone houses — and the material shapes the making.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer two very different materials (paper vs clay) and ask one question
  ("which one can you fold?"), then make one container with each.
- **Support (fine motor):** The learner directs a partner and explains the difference —
  deciding and explaining are the heart of the task.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the material and the result, or to a word card
  (bends, strong, soft, stiff).
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Explore each material by feel and name how it shaped
  the build.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to compare three materials and explain how each shaped
  the result, then to imagine which material they would choose for a heavy load and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner explain how the materials shaped what
  they could make, using an example from their own making?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The two builds side by side (or a photo), kept in the
  portfolio.

## Resources

- No external resources required; the claim that people build with local materials is
  observable in the learner's own surroundings and in the materials they compare
  directly.

## Home connection

With a grown-up, make the same little thing twice at home — once with paper, once with
something else (cloth, leaves, a box) — and talk about how the material changed what you
could make.
