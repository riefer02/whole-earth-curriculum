# Lesson 3 — Multiplication as Equal Groups

## Summary

Learners build **equal groups** and **arrays** and discover that multiplication is a
quick way to count equal groups — three groups of four is twelve, written 3 × 4 = 12.
They connect the groups, the array, the repeated addition (4 + 4 + 4), and the
multiplication sentence, so the symbol always has a picture behind it.

## Objectives

- Multiply and divide within 100 and explain the meaning of each operation using
  objects, drawings, and known facts. _(D05.S1.03.02)_

## Connection

A baker puts four buns in each box and fills three boxes; a gardener plants four seeds
in each of three rows; a weaver threads the same count in each stripe of a cloth. In
every case, the same number repeats again and again — and counting "four, eight,
twelve" is faster than counting one at a time. **Multiplication** is the name for
counting equal groups fast. Weavers, builders, and cooks in every culture have used
this idea to plan how much they need, even before it had a written symbol.

## Materials

- About 100 small objects (pebbles, seeds, beans, counters)
- Ten small cups or cloth squares for making groups
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair about 100 objects and ten holders.
- Clear space to make groups and arrays.
- Prepare a quick example of "three groups of four is twelve."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the learner's first formal
**multiplication** — the new skill is reading an **array** (rows and columns) and
writing the multiplication sentence. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13):
build three groups of four, line them into three rows of four, count by fours, and
write 3 × 4 = 12 before learners try their own. Children make sense of multiplication
through these informal strategies long before memorizing facts (Carpenter et al.,
2015). Keep objects first, symbols last. **No one's speed at counting groups measures
their smartness; some children see the total by skip-counting, some by counting every
object, and all are valid paths.** The word "times" and the symbol × are one convention
among many — the idea of equal groups is what matters, and peoples have counted in
groups of two, five, ten, and more (Zaslavsky, 1999). Count in the home language. For
learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, count with sign or taps; for learners who are
blind or low-vision, make groups in cups the hands can feel. Learners with limited
movement direct a partner to move the objects. Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — remember skip counting (5 min).** You already know how to count by twos,
   fives, and tens. Count by fours together: four, eight, twelve ... up to twenty.
   That is multiplication getting ready.
2. **Worked example — three groups of four (8 min).** Watch and help: make three groups
   with four objects in each. Count them: four, eight, twelve. **Three groups of four
   is twelve.** Now line the groups into three rows of four — an **array**. Write it
   two ways: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12, and 3 × 4 = 12. Say it: "three times four equals twelve."
3. **Guided practice — build and write (15 min).** Make your own equal groups and
   arrays. For each one, count by the group size, draw the array, and write both the
   repeated addition and the multiplication sentence.
4. **Turn it around (8 min).** Build an array, then turn your paper one quarter turn.
   Did the array change? The same array is now 4 rows of 3 instead of 3 rows of 4 — but
   the total is still 12. Write both sentences: 3 × 4 = 12 and 4 × 3 = 12.
5. **Check with a friend (5 min).** Trade arrays with a friend and read each other's
   multiplication sentences. Do you agree on the total? Count again together, kindly.
6. **Close (4 min).** Remember: multiplication counts equal groups fast. Three groups
   of four is twelve — and you can see it, draw it, and write it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use groups of two only today, and count by twos; add larger groups
  another day.
- **Support (motor):** Use larger, easy-to-grip objects, or have a partner build while
  the learner counts and writes.
- **Extension:** Find three different arrays that make 12 (1×12, 2×6, 3×4) and draw each
  with its sentence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner build equal groups, arrange them into an
  array, and write the matching multiplication sentence?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's drawn array with its multiplication sentence,
  kept as a record of the meaning of multiplication.

## Home connection

At home, find equal groups — eggs in a carton, tiles in a row, seeds in a pod — and
tell a grown-up the multiplication sentence that counts them.
