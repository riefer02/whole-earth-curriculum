# Lesson 2 — Rounding & Comparing to 1,000

## Summary

Learners place numbers on a number line to 1,000, **round** them to the nearest ten and
nearest hundred by seeing which mark is nearer, and **compare** numbers with
greater-than, less-than, and equal-to. Rounding becomes a picture of "which is nearer,"
not a rule to memorize, and comparing becomes a matter of walking the line.

## Objectives

- Use place value to 1,000 to read, write, round, and compare whole numbers.
  _(D05.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

A cook buys about 250 grams of rice; a bus driver says "about 400 people" rode today; a
gardener counts 347 seeds and says "about 350." When an exact number is hard or not
needed, people **round** to a friendly number that is close. And when two numbers sit
side by side — 347 and 374 — putting them on a line tells you at once which is more.
Rounding and comparing are how people talk about "about how many" and "which is more"
in markets, kitchens, and journeys everywhere.

## Materials

- A long number line to 1,000 (drawn, chalked, or string)
- Small objects or cards with numbers up to 1,000
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Draw (or lay out) a number line to 1,000 with every ten and hundred marked.
- Prepare number cards or objects to place on the line.
- Plan a quick example of "347 is nearer 350 than 340, so it rounds to 350."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It builds on Grade 2's comparing of
two-digit numbers and adds **rounding** — the new skill is deciding which ten or
hundred a number is nearer to. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13): put
347 on the line, count the steps to 340 and to 350, and see that 350 is nearer; do the
same for the nearest hundred. The picture is the point: rounding is "nearest mark,"
not a memorized "five or more goes up." Comparing uses the same line. **A number being
"more" is a fact about quantities, never a worth about a person; no one's speed at
rounding measures their smartness.** The midpoint (five) rule is one convention used in
many school systems; the underlying idea — nearer to this mark than that one — is
universal. Count in the home language; number words differ and all are correct. For
learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, mark "nearer" with hand signs and arrows; for
learners who are blind or low-vision, use a tactile string line with knots at tens and
hundreds so the hands can feel which mark is closer. Learners with limited movement
direct a partner to walk the line. Mastery arrives at its own pace for each child.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — a big line (5 min).** Look at our number line to 1,000. Where is 0? Where
   is 100? 500? 1,000? Touch each hundred as you say it.
2. **Worked example — round to the nearest ten (8 min).** Put 347 on the line. Which is
   nearer, 340 or 350? Count the steps: from 340 it is 7 steps; from 350 it is 3 steps.
   350 is nearer, so **347 rounds to 350**. Say it together.
3. **Round to the nearest hundred (8 min).** Now find the nearest hundred. Is 347 nearer
   300 or 400? It is 47 past 300 and 53 before 400, so **347 rounds to 300**. The two
   answers are both right — they just answer different questions ("which ten?" or
   "which hundred?").
4. **Guided practice — round and compare (15 min).** Choose numbers from the pile. Place
   each on the line, then round it to the nearest ten and nearest hundred. Take two
   numbers: which is **greater**? Which is **less**? Write your answers with the signs:
   347 &gt; 300, and 347 &lt; 400. Read each sentence aloud.
5. **Check with a friend (8 min).** Trade numbers with a friend and check each other's
   rounding and comparing. Do you agree? If not, walk the line again together, kindly.
6. **Close (6 min).** Remember: rounding means "which mark is nearer," and comparing
   means "which number is more." You can now say "about" and "more than" with real
   numbers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Round only to the nearest ten today; add the nearest hundred another day.
  Use a line with every ten marked.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a string line with a knot at each ten and a bigger
  knot at each hundred; feel which knot is closer.
- **Extension:** Round to the nearest ten, then use that rounded number to estimate a
  sum (for example, 347 + 152 is about 350 + 150 = 500), and say whether the estimate
  is high or low.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place a number on the line, say which ten
  and hundred it is nearer to, and write a greater-than/less-than comparison?
- **Self:** Can the learner explain, in their own words, why 347 rounds to 350 (nearest
  ten) but to 300 (nearest hundred)?

## Home connection

At home, round the numbers you see — the price of a food item, the number of steps you
walk, the page of a book — to the nearest ten and the nearest hundred, and tell a
grown-up which two numbers are more and less.
