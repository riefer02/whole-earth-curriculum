# Lesson 12 — Sorting Shapes by Their Attributes

## Summary

Learners sort shapes into categories by their **attributes** — number of sides, number
of angles, straight or curved edges — and name the **rule** of each category. They
discover that a category is a shared attribute, and that one shape can be described by
more than one attribute at once.

## Objectives

- Sort shapes into categories by their attributes and recognize that shapes in a
  category share common properties. _(D05.S3.03.02)_

## Connection

A cook sorts beans by color before cooking; a seed-keeper sorts seeds by size; a
caretaker sorts tools by their job. Sorting is how people bring order to a big mixed
pile — and shapes are no different. Every shape has **attributes**: how many sides,
how many corners, whether its edges are straight or curved. When you sort by an
attribute, you make a category with a rule. Builders, weavers, and tile-makers in
every culture have sorted shapes this way to fit them together.

## Materials

- A set of shape cards or cut-out shapes (triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons,
  hexagons, and shapes with curved edges)
- Three sorting rings, strings, or drawn circles
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each pair a shape set and three sorting rings.
- Clear space to sort on tables or the floor.
- Plan a worked example (sorting by number of sides).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It opens the **shape attributes** strand
— the new skill is *classifying* shapes by an attribute and naming the rule. Teach with
a **worked example** (philosophy §13): sort a mixed set into "three sides," "four
sides," and "five or more sides," and state each rule aloud before learners sort their
own. The key move is seeing that a **category is a shared attribute** — the same shape
can be sorted by sides, by angles, or by straight-vs-curved, and belong in different
piles depending on the rule (Van de Walle et al., 2019). **No one's speed at sorting
measures their smartness; two children can sort by different rules and both be right.**
Sorting is a universal human act — every culture sorts its plants, stones, and tools.
Count in the home language. For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, sign each
attribute and point to the sides; for learners who are blind or low-vision, use cut-out
shapes the hands can feel and sort by touch. Learners with limited movement direct a
partner to place each shape. Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — a mixed pile (5 min).** Look at the pile of shapes. How could you bring
   order to it? What is **the same** about some shapes? That sameness is an
   **attribute**.
2. **Worked example — sort by sides (10 min).** Watch and help: make three rings. Put
   every shape with **three sides** in one ring, every shape with **four sides** in the
   next, and the rest in the third. Say each rule: "this ring is three-sided shapes."
3. **Sort another way (10 min).** Now sort the same shapes by a *different* attribute:
   straight edges or curved edges? How many corners? A square can be "four sides" in
   one sort and "all straight edges" in another. The rule decides the pile.
4. **Guided practice — your own rule (12 min).** Sort your set by a rule you choose.
   Write the rule. Then sort the same set by a second rule and write that one too.
5. **Mystery sort (5 min).** With a partner, sort by a **secret rule** and let them
   guess it. Then swap.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: shapes live in categories by shared attributes. Name
   the rule, and the pile makes sense.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort by one attribute (number of sides) today; add a second attribute
  another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sort cut-out shapes by touch, feeling the sides and
  corners.
- **Extension:** Sort by two attributes at once (for example, "four sides AND all sides
  equal") and describe the smaller category that results.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort shapes by an attribute and name the
  rule of each category?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's drawn sort with its rule, kept as a record of
  classification.

## Home connection

At home, sort a pile of leaves, buttons, or kitchen tools by one attribute, name your
rule, and have a grown-up guess it.
