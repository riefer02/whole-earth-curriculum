# Lesson 16 — Scaled Graphs & Our Data Celebration

## Summary

The unit's capstone: learners ask a real question, collect data, and draw a **scaled
picture graph** and a **scaled bar graph** — where one symbol or one square stands for
two or more answers — then use multiplication, division, and comparison to read what
the data say. They present one finding in a **data celebration**, gathering everything
the unit made possible.

## Objectives

- Draw scaled picture and bar graphs and use them to solve problems and make
  comparisons. _(D05.S4.03.02)_
- Solve two-step word problems using the four operations and explain the relationship
  between addition and subtraction or multiplication and division. _(D05.S2.03.02)_

## Connection

This unit you have counted to a thousand, multiplied and divided, seen patterns, solved
two-step stories, measured area and perimeter, and weighed and poured real things — and
now it all comes together. A class that wants to plan a celebration asks "how will we
get there?" and graphs the answers; a village counts its water jars; a family tallies
its favorite meals. When there are many answers, one mark for each is too many marks —
so people use a **scale**: one symbol stands for two, or five, or ten. The math you
know — multiplying, dividing, comparing — is exactly how you read a scaled graph. Today
you do what data-keepers do everywhere: ask, collect, scale, graph, and share.

## Materials

- A survey question and a way to record answers (per group)
- Paper, grid paper, and a drawing tool
- Small objects or marks for a picture graph

## Preparation

- Help each group choose one respectful question with no "right" answer.
- Prepare recording sheets, grid paper, and a data wall or sharing circle.
- Plan a worked example of a scale (one symbol = 2 people).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the unit's **capstone**, weaving
together the whole unit. The one new skill is the **scale** — drawing a graph where one
symbol or square stands for two, five, or ten answers — and using multiplication and
division to read it. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13) if needed, but
let the groups drive. The celebration is a festival of mastery, not a competition:
every graph is honored, every finding is real, and every answer counts exactly once
(egalitarianism). **No one's graph is "better"; a clear, honest graph is the goal, and
every learner's contribution matters.** For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing,
present findings in sign and point to the bars; for learners who are blind or
low-vision, build a tactile graph and present by counting aloud. A learner who does not
speak presents by pointing to symbols and holding up the number. Celebrate every group.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — the big question (5 min).** Today is our **data celebration**. With a
   partner or small group, choose a real question to ask everyone (favorite fruit, how
   we get to school, a game we like, hours we sleep).
2. **Collect the data (12 min).** Ask your question and tally the answers — one mark
   for each person, so everyone counts exactly once. Keep your tally neat, like a
   data-keeper.
3. **Choose a scale (8 min).** If your counts are big, choose a **scale**: one symbol
   or one square stands for 2 (or 5) people. Show your scale in a **key** so anyone can
   read your graph.
4. **Graph it two ways (15 min).** Draw your data as a **scaled picture graph** and a
   **scaled bar graph**, each with a title and a key. Check that both show the same
   counts — multiply to check (3 symbols × 2 people each = 6 people).
5. **Read and compare (12 min).** Read your graphs. Which has the **most**? The
   **fewest**? Now answer "how many more or less" — multiply by the scale, then
   subtract. Write one finding as a sentence, using the operations of this unit.
6. **Present at the celebration (8 min).** Each group shares one finding, pointing to
   the symbols and bars. Listeners ask kind questions. Every graph is part of the
   celebration.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a two- or three-answer question and a scale of one symbol = 2;
  present one finding aloud with a partner.
- **Support (motor):** Have a partner draw while the learner tallies and counts.
- **Extension:** Write two findings, or combine two groups' data and compare them, or
  predict what a bigger group would answer and say why.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The learner's scaled picture graph and scaled bar graph,
  kept as the unit's data artifact.
- **Observation:** Can the learner collect data, draw it to a scale, and use
  multiplication, division, and comparison to answer "how many more or less"?
- **Peer:** Can the learner present a finding clearly and respond kindly to a
  classmate's graph?

## Home connection

At home, run a mini data celebration: ask your family a question, graph the answers
with a scale (one symbol = 2 people), and present one finding to them.
