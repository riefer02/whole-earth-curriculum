# Lesson 1 — Hundreds, Tens & Ones

## Summary

Learners bundle objects into groups of ten, then bundle ten tens into a hundred, and
discover that a three-digit number is **hundreds, tens, and ones** — for example, four
hundreds, three tens, and two ones is four hundred thirty-two. They read and record
numbers as hundreds, tens, and ones, treating place value as something you can hold
and count, not just a symbol.

## Objectives

- Use place value to 1,000 to read, write, round, and compare whole numbers.
  _(D05.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

Last year you bundled tens and ones to count to one hundred. This year the same idea
grows one step bigger. A market seller counts seeds into piles of ten, then stacks ten
piles into a basket of one hundred; a festival organizer counts people in rows of ten
and blocks of one hundred; a village counts its mangoes in sacks of one hundred. Most
peoples group by ten because ten fingers make ten easy to see. Some peoples have kept
count other ways — by fives, by twenties, with knots on a cord, or with tally marks —
and every counting way is a real way to keep track. Today you add the next bundle: ten
tens is one hundred.

## Materials

- About 300 small objects (pebbles, seeds, beans, buttons)
- Ten small cups, bags, or cloth squares for groups of ten
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair about 300 objects and ten holders.
- Clear space to move, bundle, and count aloud.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) a quick example of "four hundreds, three tens, and two ones."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It builds directly on Grade 2, where
learners bundled tens and ones to read two-digit numbers; today the bundle grows to the
**hundred** — the key idea is that ten tens make one hundred, the same unitizing move
as before, one step bigger (Clements & Sarama, 2014). Teach the one new skill as a
**worked example** (philosophy §13): bundle ten, bundle ten tens into a hundred, then
read hundreds, tens, and ones aloud before learners try it themselves. Keep it
concrete: objects first, symbols last. **No one's speed at bundling measures their
smartness, and a number is a fact about a collection, never a worth about a person.**
The digits 0–9 we use today are one number system among many — they reached the wider
world through Arabic-speaking scholars from India, while other peoples used tally
marks, knots, and many other ways (Ifrah, 2000; Zaslavsky, 1999). Count in the home
language; number words differ across languages and all are correct. For learners who
are Deaf or hard of hearing, count in sign or by tapping each bundle; for learners who
are blind or low-vision, move objects into cups so the number grows by feel and sound.
Learners with limited hand or body movement direct a partner to move each object.
Mastery arrives at its own pace for each child.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — remember the ten (5 min).** Last year you bundled objects into groups of
   ten. Make one group of ten right now. Hold up your ten fingers — that is one ten.
2. **Worked example — ten tens make a hundred (8 min).** Watch and help: make one group
   of ten, then another, all the way to ten groups of ten. Count the tens: ten, twenty,
   thirty ... ninety, one hundred. **Ten tens make one hundred.** Now make four
   hundreds, three tens, and two loose ones. Count: four hundred ... four hundred ten,
   twenty, thirty ... four hundred thirty ... thirty-one, thirty-two. **Four hundreds,
   three tens, and two ones is four hundred thirty-two.** Say it together.
3. **Guided practice — build and read (15 min).** From your own collection, make as many
   hundreds as you can (ten tens each), then tens, then loose ones. Count your hundreds
   by hundreds, your tens by tens, then count on the ones. Say your number. Now write
   it: how many hundreds? How many tens? How many ones?
4. **Trade and check (10 min).** Trade bundles with a friend. Read their hundreds, tens,
   and ones, and let them read yours. Do you both agree on the number? If not, count
   again together, kindly. A second count that matches is a sign you read it well.
5. **Record it (5 min).** Draw your number on paper — four big squares for four
   hundreds, three lines for three tens, and two dots for two ones — and write the
   digits beside your drawing.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: every three-digit number is hundreds, tens, and ones.
   Ten ones is a ten; ten tens is a hundred. You can now hold a big number in your
   hands.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build only up to two hundreds today; add more hundreds another day,
  echoing each hundred number.
- **Support (motor):** Bundle larger, easy-to-grip objects, or have a partner move
  objects while the learner counts and directs.
- **Extension:** Build a number, then build the number that is one hundred more (or ten
  more) and say how you know.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner group ten ones into a ten and ten tens
  into a hundred, count the hundreds by hundreds, and count on the tens and ones to say
  and write the number?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner tell how many hundreds, tens, and ones are in a
  number they built?

## Home connection

At home, gather a pile of beans, stones, or buttons, make groups of ten and groups of a
hundred, and tell a grown-up how many hundreds, tens, and ones there are — and what
number that makes.
