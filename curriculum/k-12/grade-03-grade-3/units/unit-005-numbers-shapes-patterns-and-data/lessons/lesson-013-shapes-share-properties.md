# Lesson 13 — Shapes That Share Properties

## Summary

Learners build and sort **quadrilaterals** and discover the properties they all share —
four sides and four angles — then see how squares, rectangles, and rhombuses are
nested: a **square is both a rectangle and a rhombus**. A shape can wear more than one
name, because names describe shared properties, not separate boxes.

## Objectives

- Sort shapes into categories by their attributes and recognize that shapes in a
  category share common properties. _(D05.S3.03.02)_

## Connection

A square is a rectangle, and a square is also a rhombus — even though it has its own
name too. It is like a person who is both a cousin and a sister and a friend, all at
once: the names overlap because the properties overlap. Tile-makers and builders have
long known this family of four-sided shapes, because fitting them together — in floors,
walls, and cloth — depends on what they share. Today you meet the whole family and
learn who is related to whom.

## Materials

- Quadrilateral shape cards (squares, rectangles, rhombuses, trapezoids, plus a few
  non-quadrilaterals)
- String or sticks for making sides
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each pair a set of shape cards and building sticks or string.
- Clear space to build and sort.
- Plan a worked example (a square is a rectangle and a rhombus).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It deepens sorting by showing that
categories **nest** — the new skill is seeing that a shape can belong to several
categories at once because each name marks a shared property. Teach with a **worked
example** (philosophy §13): build a square, ask "is it a rectangle? (four right angles
— yes) is it a rhombus? (four equal sides — yes)," and conclude **a square is both**.
The key move is *hierarchical* classification, which children grow into slowly — do
not rush it (Van de Walle et al., 2019). **A shape wearing many names is not "wrong";
it is precise. And no learner's speed at naming shapes measures their smartness — some
see the nesting quickly, some need to hold and turn each shape, and both are
mathematical.** Quadrilaterals appear in tiling and design across many cultures. Count
in the home language. For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, sign each property
and point to sides and corners; for learners who are blind or low-vision, feel the
sides and corners of each shape and fold a paper square to test equal sides. Learners
with limited movement direct a partner. Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — the four-sided family (5 min).** What do a square, a rectangle, a
   rhombus, and a trapezoid all have in common? Count their sides: **four**. Count
   their corners: **four**. They are all **quadrilaterals**.
2. **Worked example — a square wears many names (10 min).** Build a square with sticks.
   Is it a **rectangle**? A rectangle has four right angles — yes, a square does. Is it
   a **rhombus**? A rhombus has four equal sides — yes, a square does. So **a square is
   a rectangle and a rhombus** — and a quadrilateral too.
3. **Sort the family (12 min).** Sort your cards: which are rectangles (four right
   angles)? Which are rhombuses (four equal sides)? Which are both? Which are neither?
   Say each shape's full name: "this square is also a rectangle and a rhombus."
4. **Build and change (10 min).** With string or sticks, make a rectangle. Push on it —
   can you make it a rhombus that is not a rectangle? Make a square. What does it keep,
   and what changes?
5. **Check with a friend (5 min).** Trade cards and name each shape with all its names.
   Do you agree? Count sides and corners together, kindly.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: quadrilaterals share four sides and four angles, and a
   shape can wear more than one name. A square is a rectangle *and* a rhombus.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work only on "four sides and four angles = quadrilateral" today; add the
  square-is-a-rectangle idea another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel and fold paper shapes to test equal sides and
  square corners.
- **Extension:** Draw the quadrilateral family tree and write one sentence for each
  link ("every square is a rectangle because ...").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the shared properties of
  quadrilaterals and explain that a square is both a rectangle and a rhombus?
- **Self:** Can the learner name one shape and list all the categories it belongs to?

## Home connection

At home, find four-sided shapes (tiles, windows, doors, cloth patterns) and tell a
grown-up whether each is a rectangle, a rhombus, or both.
