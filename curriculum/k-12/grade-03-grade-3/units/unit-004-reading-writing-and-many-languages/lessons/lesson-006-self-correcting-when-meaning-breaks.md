# Lesson 6 — Self-Correcting When Meaning Breaks Down

## Summary

Learners practice **monitoring** their own reading: noticing the moment the meaning
stops making sense, stopping, and fixing it — by re-reading, trying another word, or
reading on and checking. Self-correction is the mark of a reader who reads for
meaning, not just for sounds.

## Objectives

- Read grade-level texts with accuracy and fluency, self-correcting when meaning
  breaks down. _(D04.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

Imagine you are walking a path you know and suddenly you are in a place you do not
recognize. You do not keep walking — you stop, look around, and find the way. Reading
is the same. When the words stop making sense, a good reader does not just push on;
they stop, go back, and fix it. Meaning is the path, and self-correcting is how you
stay on it.

## Materials

- A grade-level text with one tricky spot (in the home writing system)
- A "stop and fix" bookmark (does it make sense? if not, go back and fix)

## Preparation

- Choose a passage at the learner's level with one word that will trip meaning.
- Prepare the "stop and fix" bookmark.
- Arrange the text so the learner can follow it comfortably.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Self-correction is comprehension in action: the reader notices
meaning broke and repairs it. Teach it explicitly with a worked example — read a
sentence, plant an error, model the "stop," the re-read, and the fix — then guide
practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The routine is script-agnostic and
language-neutral: it works in any writing system and any language. The key idea to
protect: a mistake is not a failure; noticing your own mistake and fixing it is
*skill*, and it is celebrated. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing monitors
meaning in sign the same way — a sign that breaks the sense is stopped and fixed. A
learner who is blind or has low vision monitors meaning in braille, large print, or
by listening — a word that breaks the sense is stopped and fixed the same way. Never
make this a test of reading aloud in front of peers against a timer; it is a
quiet, private practice of attention.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember a path you know well. What do you do the moment you
   realize you are in the wrong place? You stop, look, and find the way.
2. **Meet the routine (5 min).** Learn the self-correct routine: **one, read** and
   listen for meaning; **two, stop** when it no longer makes sense; **three, fix** —
   go back, re-read, or try another word; **four, go on**.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads a sentence with a
   planted wrong word, then stops and says, "Wait — that does not make sense." They
   go back, re-read, try the right word, and continue. Watch the fix.
4. **Practice stopping and fixing (15 min).** Read your passage. When the meaning
   breaks — the tricky spot — use your bookmark: does it make sense? Go back and fix
   it, then read on. Read the whole passage smoothly once more.
5. **Share what you fixed (5 min).** Tell a partner (or write) one spot where the
   meaning broke and how you fixed it.
6. **Close (5 min).** The best readers do not never-make-mistakes. They notice, stop,
   and fix — because meaning is the path, and they stay on it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a very short passage with one planted error; practice stopping and
  fixing that single spot before reading a longer text.
- **Support (Deaf/Hard of Hearing):** Monitor meaning in sign; a sign that breaks the
  sense is stopped and re-signed correctly.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Monitor meaning in braille, large print, or by
  listening; a word that breaks the sense is stopped and fixed.
- **Extension:** Read a longer passage and mark every spot you self-corrected, then
  count them with pride — each mark is evidence of a reading mind at work.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner notice when meaning breaks down,
  stop, and fix it — by re-reading or trying another word — then continue reading
  smoothly?

## Home connection

Tonight, when you read anything at home, ask yourself after each sentence, "Does that
make sense?" If not, stop and fix it — and tell someone how you caught it.
