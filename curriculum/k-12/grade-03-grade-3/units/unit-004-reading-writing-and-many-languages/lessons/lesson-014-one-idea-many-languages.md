# Lesson 14 — One Idea, Many Languages

## Summary

Learners take one everyday idea — such as "thank you" or "water" — and discover it in
many languages, comparing how each language says it in its own way. They see clearly
that every language carries the same meaning with equal worth.

## Objectives

- Notice that the same idea can be said in many languages, and compare a few
  examples. _(D04.S4.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of water. It is the same wet, life-giving thing everywhere — yet every people
has its own word for it, and each word grew from that people's own long story. One
idea, many languages. When you learn to say "thank you" in several languages, you are
not learning several *different* ideas — you are learning one shared idea in many
voices. That is the deep truth about people: we mean the same things, and we say them
in many beautiful ways, none above another.

## Materials

- A "one idea" card (thank you, water, friend)
- A languages chart

## Preparation

- Choose one everyday idea to explore.
- Prepare the languages chart.
- Gather the languages spoken or signed in the room, and any other sources available.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Comparing languages is an act of noticing, not ranking: guide
learners to hear and see that each language says the same idea in its own way, and
that every version is equally good (UNESCO's mother-language value holds that every
language is a bearer of dignity). Keep it script-agnostic — the idea may be written in
any script, spoken, or signed; a sign language version stands exactly as high as any
spoken one. Model the compare: "this one sounds like…, this one looks like…, yet both
mean the same thing." Invite every learner's home language into the chart, each entry
equal in size, none above another. This is an egalitarian and ethical moment: no
language is "better" or "more correct," and no accent is wrong. A learner who is Deaf
or hard of hearing contributes and compares in sign. Where a recording or a
translation tool is used, notice what the tool does — it can bring many voices into
the room — and what it cannot do: it cannot carry the warmth of the person who lives
that language. A tool shows the words; the people in the circle carry the living
voice. A tool is a helper, never a replacement.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one everyday thing you know well — water, thanks,
   friend. You have a word for it in your home language. So does everyone else.
2. **Meet the idea (5 min).** Choose one idea together — say, "thank you." Say or
   sign it in your home language. That is one version of the idea.
3. **Collect the idea in many languages (15 min).** Go around and gather the idea
   from every language in the room. Write or draw each one on the chart, all the same
   size. If you can, hear them from recordings or a translation tool too.
4. **Compare them (10 min).** Look and listen. How are the versions the same — the
   meaning? How are they different — the sounds, the shapes, the signs? Notice: one
   idea, many beautiful ways.
5. **Close (5 min).** One idea, many languages, none better than another. When we
   learn another language's word, we hold a little more of the whole human family in
   our hands.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on just two languages — the learner's home language and one
  other — and notice one thing that is the same and one that differs.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Contribute the idea in sign and compare the sign with a
  spoken version, noticing how each carries the same meaning.
- **Extension:** Collect the same idea in five or more languages and sort them by how
  they sound or how they are written, noticing any that seem to share a family.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner give one idea in at least two
  languages and say one way the versions are the same (the meaning) and one way they
  differ (the sound, shape, or sign)?

## Home connection

At home, ask everyone to say "thank you" in every language they know, and write them
down side by side — one idea, many languages.
