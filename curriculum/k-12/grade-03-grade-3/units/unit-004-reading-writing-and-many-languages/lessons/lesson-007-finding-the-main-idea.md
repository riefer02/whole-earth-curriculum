# Lesson 7 — Finding the Main Idea

## Summary

Learners learn to find the **main idea** of a short informational or narrative text:
the one big idea the whole text is about. They ask "what is this mostly about?" and
state it in a single sentence, using the umbrella-and-rain image to see how one main
idea shelters many details.

## Objectives

- Identify the main idea and supporting details of a short informational or
  narrative text. _(D04.S2.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of a friend telling you about their day. They may say many small things — what
they ate, who they saw, where they went — but one big idea holds it all together:
"my day was full of surprises." That is the main idea. Every story, every sign, every
report is mostly about one thing, and finding it is how a reader understands instead
of just collecting words.

## Materials

- A short informational or narrative text (in the home language)
- A main-idea umbrella chart

## Preparation

- Choose a short grade-level passage clearly about one thing.
- Prepare the umbrella chart (main idea as umbrella, details as raindrops).
- Arrange the text so the learner can read and mark it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Finding the main idea is a taught skill: model the question
"what is this mostly about?" with a worked example, then guide learners to try it on
their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the difference clear: the main
idea is the big idea; the details are the smaller facts and examples that support it.
This works in any language and any writing system — the idea, not the script, is what
matters. Accept the main idea stated in the learner's own words and in their own
language; there is no single "correct" wording. A learner who is Deaf or hard of
hearing states the main idea in sign or writing. A learner who is blind or has low
vision finds the main idea by listening to the text read aloud, or by reading it in
braille or large print, and states it aloud or in writing. Treat this as
sense-making, not as a test with one right answer; honor a well-supported idea even
when worded differently.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of the last story someone told you. What was it *mostly*
   about? That is its main idea.
2. **Meet the question (5 min).** Learn the reader's question: **what is this mostly
   about?** The answer, in one sentence, is the **main idea**. The smaller things that
   tell more about it are the **details**.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads a short text aloud,
   then thinks aloud: "It mentions many small things, but mostly it is about…" They
   state the main idea in one sentence and point to two details. Watch how one big
   umbrella covers many raindrops.
4. **Find the main idea yourself (15 min).** Read your short text. Ask: what is this
   mostly about? Write or say the main idea in one sentence. Then check: do the
   details in the text sit under that umbrella?
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your main idea in one sentence, and point to one
   detail that supports it.
6. **Close (5 min).** One big idea, many small details — that is the shape of every
   story and every report, in every language.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read a very short, single-topic text and give two possible main ideas;
  the learner chooses which one the text is mostly about.
- **Support (non-speaking):** State the main idea by pointing to a picture, signing,
  or circling the sentence that best says it.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Hear the text read aloud, or read it in braille or
  large print; state the main idea aloud or in writing.
- **Extension:** Find the main idea of a longer text and write one sentence that
  states it, plus the two details that most support it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the main idea of a short text in
  one sentence, in their own words and language, and point to a supporting detail?

## Home connection

Tonight, ask someone at home to tell you about their day, then say it back in one
sentence: "So mostly, your day was about…" and ask if you got the main idea.
