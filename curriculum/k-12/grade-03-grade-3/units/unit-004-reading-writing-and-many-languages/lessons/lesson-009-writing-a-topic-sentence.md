# Lesson 9 — Writing a Topic Sentence

## Summary

Learners learn to write a strong **topic sentence** — the first sentence that tells
the reader what the paragraph will be about — and practice the check, "does it tell
what my paragraph is about?"

## Objectives

- Write a paragraph with a topic sentence, at least two supporting details, and a
  closing sentence. _(D04.S3.03.01)_

## Connection

Think of a door to a house: the door tells you what kind of place you are about to
enter. A topic sentence is the door to a paragraph — it tells the reader, in one
sentence, what the whole paragraph will be about. Without it, the reader walks into a
room blind. In every language and every writing system, a clear first sentence opens
the door for the reader.

## Materials

- A topic to write about (a place, an animal, a task, a game)
- A paragraph frame (with the first line highlighted)

## Preparation

- Choose a few friendly topics learners know well.
- Prepare the paragraph frame.
- Have paper and pencils ready.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Writing a topic sentence is a foundational skill, so teach it
explicitly: model a strong topic sentence, contrast it with a weak one, then guide
practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The one check to protect: "does it tell
what the paragraph is about?" A strong topic sentence names the topic and gives the
reader a clear picture of what is coming. Learners write in their home language and
their own writing system — script-agnostic, no Latin alphabet assumed. A learner who
is Deaf or hard of hearing writes, signs, or dictates the sentence; the thinking is
the writing. A learner who is blind or has low vision composes by dictation, on a
braille writer, or by typing and listening back — the thinking is the writing.
Celebrate a clear, honest topic sentence in any wording; there is no single "correct"
sentence.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a door you know. Before you open it, you already know
   a little about what is inside. A topic sentence is a door like that.
2. **Meet the topic sentence (10 min).** Learn: a **topic sentence** is the first
   sentence of a paragraph, and it tells what the paragraph is about. It names the
   topic and gives a clear picture. Try the check: **does it tell what my paragraph
   is about?**
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator writes a weak sentence —
   "It is nice." — then a strong one — "My grandmother's garden is full of color in
   the morning." Watch how the strong sentence opens a door the reader can see
   through.
4. **Write your topic sentence (15 min).** Choose a topic you know well. Say your
   topic sentence aloud first. Then write it on the highlighted line. Read it back
   and ask: does it tell what my paragraph is about? Fix it if not.
5. **Share (5 min).** Read your topic sentence to a partner. The partner guesses:
   "So your paragraph will be about…?" and you say yes or fix it.
6. **Close (5 min).** One clear sentence, and the reader is in. That is the door, and
   you wrote it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence starter — "My paragraph is about…" or "One thing I
  love is…" — and let the learner finish it.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sign, dictate, or point to a picture and have the topic
  sentence written or typed.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Compose by dictation, on a braille writer, or by
  typing and listening back; the thinking is the writing.
- **Extension:** Write two topic sentences for the same topic and choose which opens
  the door more clearly, saying why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner write a topic sentence that tells what
  their paragraph will be about, in their own language and writing system?

## Home connection

Choose something at home you know well — a meal, a place, a pet — and write one topic
sentence about it. Read it to someone and ask, "Do you know what my paragraph will be
about?"
