# Lesson 4 — Reading with Accuracy

## Summary

Learners practice reading grade-level text **accurately** — reading what is really
written, not guessing. They sound out each word, blend it, and check it fits the
meaning, all in their own writing system, whatever that script may be.

## Objectives

- Read grade-level texts with accuracy, self-correcting when meaning breaks down.
  _(D04.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

When you copy a friend's phone number or read a sign to know which road to take, one
wrong mark changes everything. Accuracy means reading what is really there. A recipe,
a medicine label, a road sign — each one works only if the reader reads it truly.
That is why readers everywhere slow down, sound out, and check: accuracy is how
written words keep their promise.

## Materials

- A grade-level text (in the home writing system)
- A reading pointer (finger, stick, or card)

## Preparation

- Choose a short passage at the learner's level, in the home writing system.
- Prepare a pointer for each learner.
- Arrange the text so the learner can follow it comfortably.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Accuracy means reading the word that is actually written, not
a guess that "looks close." Teach the three-step routine explicitly — say the sounds,
blend, check it makes sense — as worked instruction, then guide practice before
independent reading (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The routine is
script-agnostic: the deeper rule — read what is really written, then check that it
makes sense — holds in every writing system, though the exact step looks different in
each. In a sound- or syllable-based script (Hangul, Devanagari, Ethiopic, Arabic, or
the Latin alphabet), you read each part and put the parts together. In a
character-based script (such as Chinese), you recognize each whole character and
check its meaning. Never assume the Latin alphabet; a learner's "letters" may be
characters, syllables, or marks of another kind. A learner who is Deaf or hard
of hearing reads accurately in sign or fingerspelling; accuracy of the word is what
counts. A learner who is blind or has low vision reads accurately in braille, in
large print, or by listening to the text read aloud and echoing it — braille is a
writing system too, and accuracy is the goal in it just the same. Celebrate each
learner's accuracy against their own yesterday, never against a peer.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time one small mistake changed everything — a wrong
   number, a wrong turn. Reading accurately means reading the real thing.
2. **Meet the routine (5 min).** Learn the three steps of accurate reading: **one,
   say the sounds** — read each part of the word; **two, blend** — put the parts
   together; **three, check** — does it make sense in the sentence?
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads one word slowly, doing
   all three steps aloud: say the sounds, blend the word, check it fits. Watch how
   the pointer slides under each word.
4. **Read accurately (15 min).** Read your passage, sliding the pointer under each
   word. For each word, say the sounds, blend it, and check it makes sense. If a
   word is hard, stop and do the three steps again.
5. **Listen to yourself (5 min).** If you can, read to a partner or record yourself.
   Notice: which words did you read truly? Which need another try? Read those again.
6. **Close (5 min).** Accuracy is a kind of honesty — reading the word that is really
   there, in your own writing system, in your own language.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read one short, familiar sentence, doing the three steps slowly on a
  single word; accuracy, not speed, is the only goal.
- **Support (Deaf/Hard of Hearing):** Read accurately in sign or fingerspelling,
  checking each word against the text.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Read in braille, large print, or by listening and
  echoing; check each word against the text in your own way.
- **Extension:** Read a slightly harder passage and mark with a light pencil any
  word you want to try again, then re-read just those words.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner read each word accurately — the
  word that is actually written — using the say-blend-check routine in their own
  writing system?

## Home connection

Read a short line from anything at home — a package, a note, a book — and check each
word against the page, asking, "Did I read what is really written?"

## Resources

- National Reading Panel (2000), report on reading instruction — systematic,
  explicit instruction in the sounds and letters of one's own writing system builds
  accuracy. (Cited as a widely used evidence base.)
