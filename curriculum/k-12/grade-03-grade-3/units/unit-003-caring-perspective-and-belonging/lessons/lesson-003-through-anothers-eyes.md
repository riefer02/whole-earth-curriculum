# Lesson 3 — Through Another's Eyes

## Summary

Learners retell the well-known tale **The Lion and the Mouse** from each character's
point of view. They discover that the same events — the trap, the roar, the rescue —
look completely different depending on whose eyes you see through, and that retelling
from the other side deepens care.

## Objectives

- Retell a story from another character's point of view. _(D02.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

You know the story: a big lion spares a tiny mouse, and later the mouse gnaws the lion
free from a trap. But have you ever told it *from inside the story*? To the lion, the
mouse is a little nothing — until the trap snaps shut. To the mouse, the lion is a
terrifying giant — until she realizes she can help him. Same story, two worlds. Every
story has more than one side, and retelling from the other side is how you learn to
see through another's eyes.

## Materials

- "The Lion and the Mouse" story sheet
- Character point-of-view cards (lion, mouse)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or tell the story sheet — a short, warm telling of the tale.
- Prepare the two point-of-view cards with prompt questions: "What did I see? How did I
  feel? What did I do and why?"

## Facilitator note

This lesson grows Grade 2's "two people can feel differently" into a full **retell from
another's point of view**, the heart of CASEL's social-awareness competency. The Lion
and the Mouse is chosen because it is told in many lands — versions appear across
Africa, Asia, and Europe — so it belongs to everyone and no one. Teach the retell as
worked instruction: model one character's point of view fully, then guide learners to do
the other (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the two points of view side by side
without ranking them: the lion is not "wrong" and the mouse is not "right" — each sees
truthfully from where they stand. This is the egalitarian core of the lesson. Invite
learners to tell the story in a home language if they wish; sign and drawing are full
ways to retell. Read the story aloud with feeling.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a story you know very well. Whose eyes do you usually
   see it through? What if you stepped inside a *different* character?
2. **Hear the story (10 min).** Listen to "The Lion and the Mouse." Picture it twice:
   once as the lion, once as the mouse.
3. **Watch a worked retell (10 min).** The facilitator retells the tale *as the lion*:
   "I am king of this land. This little mouse woke me, and I laughed — what could she
   ever do for me? Then the trap snapped…" Notice: same events, told from inside one
   character's head.
4. **Retell the other view (15 min).** Now you retell it *as the mouse*, with a partner
   or alone. Use the card's questions: what did you see, how did you feel, what did you
   do and why? Swap so each of you tries the side you have not told yet.
5. **Close (5 min).** Same story, two points of view — and both are true. Retelling from
   the other side is how you learn to see through another's eyes.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a picture sequence of the story and a sentence starter ("I am the
  mouse. I saw…"). A non-speaking learner draws the scene from one character's view.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to retell a *third* point of view (the hunter, the
  thorn, the jungle) and compare it to the first two.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner retell the events from the chosen
  character's point of view — using "I" and naming that character's feelings and reasons?
- **Peer (check):** In the swap, did each partner hear a retelling that stayed inside
  one character's point of view?

## Home connection

Retell a favorite family story or tale from a different character's point of view, and
ask someone at home to guess whose eyes you are seeing through.
