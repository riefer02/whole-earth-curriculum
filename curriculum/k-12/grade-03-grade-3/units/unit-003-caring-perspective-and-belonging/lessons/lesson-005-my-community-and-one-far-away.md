# Lesson 5 — My Community and One Far Away

## Summary

Learners compare their own community with one in a **different place or culture** — a
river village, a mountain town, a desert settlement, or a busy city. They find what is
**alike** (everyone needs food, water, shelter, care, and play) and what is
**different** (how each place meets those needs), and they learn that no way of living
is better or worse, only different.

## Objectives

- Compare their own community with a community in a different place or culture.
  _(D02.S3.03.01)_

## Connection

A river village and a mountain town look nothing alike. The river village has houses on
stilts and boats and fish; the mountain town has stone houses and terraced fields and
firewood. But look closer: both people cook food, fetch water, shelter their families,
care for their children, and gather to celebrate. Every human community, everywhere,
solves the same needs — food, water, shelter, care, learning, belonging — in its own
way, shaped by its place and its tools and its story. Comparing them honestly, without
saying one is "better," is how we come to understand the whole human family.

## Materials

- "A community far away" picture sheet
- Same-and-different chart
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the far-away-community picture sheet (choose one place).
- Print the same-and-different chart with two columns.
- Write the shared needs large: food, water, shelter, care, learning, play, belonging.

## Facilitator note

This lesson teaches **comparison without ranking**, the egalitarian core of the unit's
global lens. The risk to guard against is the quiet message that one way of life is
"simple" or "backward" — a river village is not "less" than a city; it is adapted to its
place and its tools. Frame every difference as "this place shapes this way of living"
(the environment and technology lenses), never as better/worse. Choose the far-away
community with care so it is respectful and specific, and let learners from those very
places speak first if present. Cite that anthropologists describe cultures from within,
not from above; we follow that same humility. The same-needs anchor keeps the comparison
fair: every community meets the same human needs, differently. Read the procedure aloud;
model one "alike" and one "different" row before learners fill their own (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Picture your own community: how do people here get water, food,
   and shelter? How do they care for children and celebrate?
2. **Meet the far-away place (10 min).** Look at the picture sheet. Walk through the
   far-away community's day: where does water come from? What do people eat? Where do
   children play? Notice the place and the tools that shape each answer.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** Fill the first two rows together: one thing
   that is **alike** ("both communities need water") and one that is **different**
   ("here we carry it from a tap; there they draw it from the river"). Say it plainly:
   different, not better or worse.
4. **Compare (15 min).** With a partner, fill your chart: find three things alike and
   three things different. Then draw one shared need met in two ways — once in your
   community, once in the far-away one.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every community meets the same needs in its own way. Seeing that
   is how we honor the whole human family — alike in need, different in way.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a ready chart with picture labels for each need; learners place a
  mark under "alike" or "different." A non-speaking learner points to the pictures.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to compare a *third* community and find one need met
  three different ways.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner name at least one thing alike and one
  thing different between the two communities, without ranking one as better?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The same-and-different chart, kept in the learner's
  portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, ask someone about a place their family once lived, and together find one thing
alike and one thing different between that place and where you live now.
