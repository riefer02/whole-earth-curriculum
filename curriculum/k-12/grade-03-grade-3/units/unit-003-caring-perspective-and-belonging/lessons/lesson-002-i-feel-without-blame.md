# Lesson 2 — I Feel, Without Blame

## Summary

Learners learn the four-part **"I statement"** — *I feel… when… because… I need…* — and
practice turning blame words into feeling words. They discover that saying how you feel
without blaming invites the other person to help fix things, instead of making them
defend themselves.

## Objectives

- Use "I" statements to say how they feel without blaming during a conflict.
  _(D02.S1.03.02)_

## Connection

Someone bumps into you, or takes the ball before your turn, or says something sharp.
You could shout, "You're mean! You always grab it!" — and now the other person gets
angry back, and the problem grows. Or you could say, "I feel upset when the ball is
taken before my turn, because I was waiting, and I need a turn next." The first is
**blame**; the second is an **"I" statement**. The I statement says what is true for
*you* without attacking the other person — and that leaves room to fix the problem
together. Communities around the world make arguments gentler in many ways — an elder
who helps two sides listen, a circle that repairs what broke, a quiet apology shared
over food. The I statement is one honest tool among many, and it is the one you are
learning today.

## Materials

- An I-statement frame chart ("I feel ___ when ___ because ___. I need ___.")
- Blame-to-I-statement cards
- A soft turn marker

## Preparation

- Post the I-statement frame where everyone can see it.
- Prepare blame sentences to convert, such as "You always grab the ball!" and
  "You never let me play!"

## Facilitator note

This lesson sharpens Grade 2's "solve it with words" into the precise tool of the
**"I" statement**, a core CASEL relationship-skill. Teach it as explicit, worked
instruction: show the frame, convert a blame sentence together, then let learners try
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Protect the key idea: an I statement names *your*
feeling and *your* need — it never says "you." When a learner offers "I feel you are
mean," gently re-steer: that is still a hidden "you"; try "I feel hurt when…". Keep the
conflicts small and fictional so no learner relives a real hurt. Honor that anger is a
real feeling, not a wrong one — the skill is in saying it without blame, not in hiding
it. Learners who do not speak with their mouth use sign, a picture card, or a written
sentence for the frame. Read the procedure aloud and model each part.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a small conflict — someone cut the line, took your
   turn, said something that stung. What did you want to say? What did you actually
   say?
2. **Meet the frame (10 min).** Learn the four parts of an I statement: **I feel
   ___**, **when ___**, **because ___**, **I need ___.** Say them together. Notice:
   the frame talks about *me*, not *you* — it says what is true for me and asks for
   what I need.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** Take the blame sentence "You always grab the
   ball!" and turn it together into: "I feel upset when the ball is taken before my
   turn, because I was waiting, and I need a turn next." See how the second one says
   the same hurt without attacking.
4. **Convert and practice (15 min).** Take a blame-to-I-statement card. With a
   partner, turn the blame into an I statement using all four parts. Then switch roles:
   one says the I statement, the other listens and answers, "What do you need?" and
   makes a small plan.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: blame makes a wall; an I statement opens a door. You
   can say how you feel — honestly, without blaming.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer the frame with blanks and a word bank of feelings; practice just
  the "I feel ___" part first.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to turn a blame sentence into an I statement *and*
  then say what the other person might feel, using two I statements back to back.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner say how they feel using the I-statement
  frame without blaming ("you") during the role-play?
- **Peer (check):** Did the partner hear a feeling and a need, and was the plan fair to
  both sides?

## Home connection

The next time you feel upset with someone at home, try one I statement — "I feel ___
when ___ because ___. I need ___." Notice how the other person answers.
