# Lesson 3 — Two Peoples, Two Ways to Meet Needs

## Summary

Learners compare how **two peoples** — the **Inuit** of the Arctic and **Bedouin
peoples** of the desert — met the same basic needs for **food**, **shelter**, and
**clothing**, and explain how each **environment** shaped those choices. Grade 2 named
the four needs and saw how people adapt; Grade 3 places two peoples side by side and
traces the line from place to choice, always holding both ways as clever and fitting,
never "primitive" and never ranked.

## Objectives

- Compare how two peoples met basic needs for food, shelter, and clothing and explain
  how their environment shaped those choices. _(D08.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

Every person, everywhere, needs the same three things from the world: **food** to eat,
**shelter** to stay warm and dry, and **clothing** to protect the body. But *how* people
meet each need depends on where they live. Where it is very cold, people need thick,
warm coverings and a snug home. Where it is very hot and dry, they need light cloth and
shade. The need is the same for everyone; the way is shaped by place — and every way is
a real, clever answer.

## Materials

- "Two peoples" picture cards (Arctic/Inuit and desert/Bedouin: food, shelter, clothing)
- A three-row comparison chart (food, shelter, clothing; Arctic and desert columns)
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the picture cards. **Inuit (Arctic):** food — fish and sea food; shelter — a
  snow house (iglu) or a warm tent; clothing — a warm parka of fur and hide. **Bedouin
  peoples (desert):** food — dates and milk from herded animals; shelter — a light tent
  that lets air through; clothing — a long, loose, light robe that shields from sun and
  sand. (See Resources for accuracy.)
- Prepare the three-row comparison chart with space to write "the place shaped this
  because…" under each row.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Hold both peoples with respect, as
homes, not costumes or curiosities.** The Inuit are a living people across the Arctic;
Bedouin peoples live across the deserts of North Africa and the Middle East (see
Resources). Neither is "primitive," and neither place is to be pitied — each place asks
something of its people, and each people answers with deep knowledge, built over many
generations. **Comparing is a foundational skill, so model one full row first**
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): "Food — in the Arctic, people fish; in the desert,
people eat dates and milk. The place shaped the food: a frozen sea gives fish; a hot,
dry land gives date palms and herded animals." Then guide the shelter and clothing
rows. **Never rank:** a parka is not better than a robe, and a snow house is not better
than a tent — each fits its home. Honor every body: describe the cards in words for a
learner who cannot see them; a learner who does not use a speaking voice points or
acts. Use present tense for these living ways of life.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of the coldest place and the hottest place you can
   imagine. What would you need in each?
2. **Meet the two peoples and their places (10 min).** In the **Arctic**, it is very
   cold and icy much of the year. In the **desert**, it is very hot and dry. Both are
   homes where people have lived for thousands of years.
3. **Compare food, shelter, and clothing (15 min).** Sort the cards and fill each row:
   - **Food:** Arctic — fish and sea food; desert — dates and milk.
   - **Shelter:** Arctic — a snow house or warm tent; desert — a light tent.
   - **Clothing:** Arctic — a warm parka; desert — a long, loose, light robe.
   For each row, say how the **place shaped the choice**: "The frozen sea gives fish, so
   people fish." "The hot sun and sand call for light cloth, so people wear robes."
4. **Explain one row in your own words (10 min).** Choose one need. Tell how each
   people meets it and how their environment shaped it. Point, act, draw, or sign.
5. **Close (5 min).** Two peoples, same needs, different places — and each answer is
   clever and fitting. Comparing with respect is how we learn from one another.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare only food for the two peoples, with heavy picture support and a
  single "place shaped it" sentence said together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the objects — a warm wrap and a light cloth, a
  thick cup and a thin cloth — and describe how each fits its place.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort the cards onto the chart and point to each need and
  its "shaped by" spot instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — sort them and sign "cold," "hot,"
  "food," "shelter," and "clothing" as you go.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use larger cards and a stable chart; direct a partner
  to place each card while you point, sign, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Add a third people (a coastal or a mountain people) and tell how their
  place shaped one of the three needs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner match at least one way each people meets
  food, shelter, and clothing?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner explain, for one need, how the
  environment shaped the choice — without ranking either people or place as better?

## Home connection

Think of your own place: what is the weather like, and how does it shape what your
family eats, where you live, and what you wear? Tell a grown-up one way your place has
shaped your family's way of meeting a need.
