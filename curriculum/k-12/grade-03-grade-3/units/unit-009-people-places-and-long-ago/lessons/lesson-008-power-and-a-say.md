# Lesson 8 — Having a Say: Power and Being Left Out

## Summary

Learners explain what it means to have **power** or a **say** in a group — that your
voice helps decide what happens — and what can happen when some people are **left out**.
Grade 2 sorted fair and unfair rules; Grade 3 names the deeper idea behind fairness:
**power** is the ability to help decide, a **say** is a voice that is heard, and a fair
group makes room for every voice. The lesson always ends in the possibility of noticing
and inviting someone in.

## Objectives

- Explain what it means to have power or a say in a group, and what can happen when some
  people are left out. _(D08.S4.03.02)_

## Connection

Every group decides things — your class, your games, your home, your town. When
everyone gets to speak and is heard, the group has a **fair say**. That is what **power
in a group** can mean: not bossing others, but having a voice that helps decide. When
some people are **left out** — not asked, not listened to — the choice can be unfair,
because their needs and ideas were never heard. Noticing who is left out, and inviting
them in, is how groups become fair.

## Materials

- "A say for all" picture cards (everyone speaks; someone left out; invite them in)
- A two-column chart (everyone has a say / someone is left out)
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the cards. **Everyone has a say:** five people in a circle, each with a speech
  bubble — every voice is heard. **Someone is left out:** four people speaking while one
  person sits outside the circle with no bubble. **The fix:** a card where the outside
  person is invited into the circle.
- Prepare the two-column chart, and be ready to model the words: "Having a say means
  your voice helps decide. When someone is left out, their voice is missing, and the
  choice can be unfair."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Hold power as shared, not bossing.**
For this age, "power in a group" is best explained as *the ability to help decide*, and
"a say" as *a voice that is heard*. This is a values commitment, held as such — not a
factual claim — and it carries the curriculum's egalitarian heart: power is meant to be
shared, and a fair group makes room for every voice. **Keep it concrete and blame-free:**
use a real classroom decision (what game to play, where to sit, how to share) and end
with the fix — noticing who is left out and inviting them in. The unfairness is in the
arrangement, never in a child. **The idea connects outward** to the wider world: the
same noticing — who has a say, who is left out — is how fairness in the big human story
begins to be studied in later grades. There is a real, citable anchor: the United
Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child says children have the right to say what
they think and to be listened to (see Resources). Honor every body: a learner who does
not use a speaking voice points or signs; a learner who is blind or has low vision hears
the scenes described clearly. **Never require a learner to share a real experience of
being left out** — the given cards and circle are enough.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time your group decided something. Did everyone get
   to speak? How did it feel?
2. **Meet "a say" and "power" (10 min).** **A say** means your voice is heard and helps
   decide. **Power in a group** can mean the ability to help decide — not bossing others.
   Say it together: "A say = my voice helps decide."
3. **See fair and unfair (15 min).** Look at the two cards. In the first, everyone
   speaks and is heard — the choice is fair. In the second, one person sits outside
   with no voice — the choice can be unfair, because their needs and ideas were never
   heard.
4. **Meet the fix (10 min).** Look at the third card: the person is **invited in**. When
   a group notices someone is left out, it can ask them in, listen, and make the choice
   together. That is how a group becomes fair.
5. **Practice it (10 min).** In a circle, make one small real decision together, giving
   every voice a turn. Then name it: "Everyone had a say, so the choice was fair." Point,
   sign, or act.
6. **Close (5 min).** Every voice matters. A fair group makes room for all — and anyone
   can be the one who notices and invites someone in.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Meet only "a say" and "left out," and finish one sentence together — "When
  someone is left out, the group can…" — with picture support.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sit in a real circle and notice each voice by sound;
  describe aloud what it means when a voice is missing.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort the cards onto the two columns and point to the
  "invite them in" card instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — read them with your eyes, and sign "say,"
  "left out," and "include" as you go.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Direct a partner to move each card while you point,
  sign, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Make up one new way a group could make sure no voice is missed — a
  talking stick, a turn list — and tell why it helps.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain what it means to have a say and
  what happens when someone is left out?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner name one way the group could
  include someone — in their own words, ending in fairness rather than blame?

## Resources

- United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 12 — states that
  children have the right to say what they think and to have their views taken into
  account, supporting the idea that every voice, including a child's, deserves to be
  heard in the groups that affect them.
  https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child

## Home connection

With a grown-up, think of a decision your family makes. Does everyone get a say? What
is one small way to make sure a quieter voice is heard?
