# Lesson 8 — Moving with Balance

## Summary

Learners meet balance. They learn that balance uses a hidden sense inside the ear plus
their muscles, practice balance poses, then walk a straight "tightrope" line and build
a short balance sequence. Moving with balance is moving with control and steadiness.

## Objectives

- Perform a short movement sequence, such as a dance or obstacle course, with rhythm
  and balance. _(D03.S2.03.01)_

## Connection

Think of walking along the edge of a curb, or balancing on one foot to put on a shoe.
Your body is doing something amazing: it is staying steady by talking to itself. People
everywhere balance — tightrope walkers at festivals (a skill with long traditions from
Japan to India to Europe), farmers crossing a narrow log over a stream, a dancer
holding a steady pose, a child on a low wall. Today you will practice the skill of
staying steady.

## Materials

- A straight line on the floor (chalk, tape, or rope)
- A clear floor space
- Balance pose cards

## Preparation

- Lay one or more straight lines on the floor, wide enough apart to be safe.
- Clear the space around the lines.
- Prepare balance pose cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Prepare the lines and cards, then model each pose and the
tightrope walk slowly before learners try (explicit modeling, then guided practice).
This continues the movement strand from Lesson 7's rhythm — now balance joins it, and
the two come together in the capstone. The factual idea here — that balance uses a
sense in the inner ear working with muscles and eyes — is well established (see
Resources). Honor every body: balance can be practiced seated (holding a steady,
upright core and lifting arms or a leg), with wheels, or with a hand lightly touching
a wall or a partner's shoulder for support — and every version counts fully. Never
pressure a learner to balance without support; staying safe is the first skill. Keep
it joyful and non-competitive: falling or wobbling is how balance grows, and it is
never a failure.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Stand still for a moment. Feel your body keeping you upright.
   Balance is a sense you carry inside you. Today you will practice it.
2. **Meet the balance sense (5 min).** Deep inside each ear is a tiny balance sensor
   that works with your muscles and eyes to keep you steady. You do not have to think
   about it — it just works, all day long.
3. **Practice poses (15 min).** Try the balance poses from the cards: stand on one
   foot, arms wide; stand heel-to-toe like a tightrope walker; take slow, steady steps.
   Hold each pose for a few slow breaths. Wobble? That is your body learning — try
   again.
4. **Walk the line (10 min).** Walk along the line like a tightrope, arms wide, eyes
   ahead, slow steps. Walk it forward, then try it sideways. Use a wall or a friend's
   hand for help if you want.
5. **Build a balance sequence (10 min).** Put three moves in order — maybe balance on
   one foot, then slow-step the line, then a final wide-arms pose. Perform it with
   control, in your own body's way.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "my body can balance." How did it feel to stay
   steady on your own?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Keep one hand on a wall or a partner for support; practice one pose at a
  time and a shorter line.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to balance with eyes closed for a moment (near a spotter),
  or to add a slow turn at the end of their line walk.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/performance):** Can the learner hold a balance pose and move
  through a short balance sequence with growing control, in their own body's way?

## Home connection

At home, practice balancing on one foot or walking a line on the floor (a crack in the
tiles, a line of tape) with someone — and teach them what you learned about the balance
sense in your ear.
