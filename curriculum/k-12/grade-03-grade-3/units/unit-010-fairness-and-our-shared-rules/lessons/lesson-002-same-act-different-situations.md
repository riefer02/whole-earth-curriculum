# Lesson 2 — The Same Act, Different Situations

## Summary

Learners discover that an act is not good or bad by itself — it depends on the
**situation**. The same act — shouting, running, pushing, taking — can protect someone
in one situation and hurt someone in another. They practice explaining *why* the
context changes the act's meaning, and hold it as a question rather than a fixed rule.

## Objectives

- Explain why the same act can be good in one situation and not in another.
  _(D09.S1.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of **shouting**. If you shout "Watch out!" to warn a friend about a falling
branch, the shout is good — it protects them. But if you shout at a friend to scare or
shame them, the *same* shout is not good — it hurts them. The act did not change; the
**situation** did. People everywhere reason this way: taking fruit from a shared tree
when you are hungry is different from taking what is not yours; running is welcome on
a field and dangerous beside a baby. Today you will practice seeing how the situation
changes what an act means.

## Materials

- "Same act, two situations" cards (shouting to warn vs. to scare, running in a field
  vs. near a baby, pushing to save vs. to hurt, taking food when hungry vs. taking
  what is not yours)
- Two puppets or soft toys
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the "same act, two situations" card pairs.
- Set up a small space for acting out the same act in two situations.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **context**: an act's meaning
depends on the situation and its effect on others, not on a fixed label. It builds
directly on Grade 2's right-and-wrong with a reason, but complicates it honestly — the
same act can sit on both sides of the line, and that is *why* we must think rather
than follow a rule blindly. Use gentle, concrete pairs and wonder aloud rather than
assert: "what does this act *do* in this situation?" Keep the framing blame-free; the
point is never to excuse harm ("I was just warning them") but to teach that *meaning
depends on effect and intent together*, and that is a question to keep open. A learner
who does not speak aloud points to the two situations or signs; a learner who is deaf
or hard of hearing follows each pair through the cards and sign; a learner who is blind
or low-vision hears each situation described and names why the act changed meaning; a
learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to act the puppets. A learner may
always pass. The goal is the learner's own explanation — "the same act was good here
and not good there because…" — not a memorized phrase.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time the *same* thing you did was fine in one place
   and a problem in another. What changed — you, or the situation?
2. **Meet "it depends on the situation" (10 min).** An **act** is something you do.
   Whether it is good depends on the **situation** — where it happens, who it reaches,
   and what it does for them. The same act can be good in one situation and not good in
   another.
3. **Match the act to its two situations (15 min).** Pick a card pair. Name the act,
   then name the two situations. Wonder together: why is the *same* act good here and
   not good there? What did it do for the other person each time?
4. **Act it out (10 min).** With the puppets, show one act in two situations — shout to
   warn, then shout to scare. Say why it changed. A learner who does not move the
   puppets can point to each situation or direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** An act is not good or bad all by itself. The situation — and what
   it does to another person — changes what it means. That is why we stop and think.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two clear pairs (shout-to-warn vs. shout-to-scare) and model one
  sentence "It was good here because…, and not good there because…"; a non-speaking
  learner points to each situation; a learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to invent their own act with two situations and ask a
  partner to name why the meaning changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain why the same act can be good in
  one situation and not in another?
- **Performance:** The acted-out "same act, two situations" pair, explained aloud.

## Home connection

At home, notice one act today — a shout, a run, a push, a grab — and wonder with a
grown-up: in what situation would it be good, and in what situation would it not? Why?
