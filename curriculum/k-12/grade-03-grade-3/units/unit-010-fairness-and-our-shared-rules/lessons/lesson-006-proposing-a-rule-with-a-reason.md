# Lesson 6 — Proposing a Rule With a Reason

## Summary

Learners move from *explaining* rules to *proposing* one. They choose a shared space,
draft a rule for it, and give the "so that…" reason. A fair rule is one whose reason
everyone can hear — made *with* the group, not handed down to it.

## Objectives

- Explain how rules keep a group safe and fair and propose a rule for a shared space
  with a reason. _(D09.S3.03.01)_ — this lesson focuses on *proposing* a rule for a
  shared space with a reason; Lesson 5 focused on *explaining* how rules keep a group
  safe and fair.

## Connection

Think of a shared space you use every day — a classroom, a playground, a library, a
garden, a water pump, a bus stop. What is one problem there? Maybe everyone grabs at
once, or someone is always left waiting. You can **propose** a rule to fix it: "At the
swing, the rule is *wait your turn*, **so that** everyone gets a turn, not just the
fastest." The rule and its reason go together. People everywhere propose rules this
way — a village agrees when to water the field, a class agrees how to share the books,
a family agrees who clears the table. Today you will propose a rule of your own, with
a reason.

## Materials

- A "propose a rule" page (my shared space, my rule, my reason — "so that…")
- A shared-space picture set (classroom, playground, library, garden, water pump, bus
  stop)
- Crayons and drawing tools

## Preparation

- Prepare the "propose a rule" page with three prompts, or draw it on paper, cloth, or
  the ground.
- Prepare the shared-space pictures; arrange a circle for sharing proposals.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson lets learners **author** rules rather than
only receive them — an egalitarian, civic act. The structure is a simple worked
example: (1) name the shared space, (2) name the rule, (3) give the "so that…" reason.
Model it fully once, then let each learner draft their own. Emphasize that a rule
gains its fairness from its *reason* — a reason that everyone, not just the proposer,
can hear and accept. Hold open the question of whether the group would actually agree
to the rule; a proposed rule is a suggestion to the group, not a command. A learner
who does not speak aloud fills the page by pointing to a picture and to their rule and
reason, or signs; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing drafts through sign and
pictures; a learner who is blind or low-vision states the space, rule, and reason
aloud and has them written down; a learner with a motor difference can direct a
partner to write or draw. A learner may always pass. The goal is a real proposal — "In
the ___, my rule is ___, so that ___" — in the learner's own words.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a shared space you know and one small problem in it.
   What rule could help?
2. **Meet "propose a rule" (10 min).** To **propose** a rule is to suggest it to the
   group. A strong proposal has three parts: the **shared space**, the **rule**, and
   the **reason** — a "so that…" that shows how it keeps everyone safe or fair.
3. **Hear a model (10 min).** Listen to (or build) one model: "In the garden, the rule
   is *water only your own row first*, so that everyone's plants get water before
   anyone takes extra." Name the three parts.
4. **Propose your rule (15 min).** On your page, choose a shared space, write or draw
   your rule, and write or tell the reason. Share your proposal with a partner — say
   the rule and its "so that…" and listen to theirs. A learner who does not write can
   point, sign, or have a partner scribe while they direct.
5. **Close (5 min).** A fair rule is one whose reason everyone can hear. You can
   propose a rule — and explain why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sentence frame ("In the ___, the rule is ___, so that ___") and
  model it once; a non-speaking learner points to a picture for each blank; a learner
  with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to propose two rules for the same space and wonder
  which the group would more likely accept, and why the reason matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner propose a rule for a shared space and
  give a reason?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's "propose a rule" page (or a built, acted, or
  partner-scribed version), kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

At home, propose one rule for a shared family space (the table, the yard, the
bathroom) and give its "so that…" reason. Ask your family if they would agree to it.
