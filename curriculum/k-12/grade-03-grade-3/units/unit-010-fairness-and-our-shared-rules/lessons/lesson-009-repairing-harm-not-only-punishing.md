# Lesson 9 — Repairing Harm, Not Only Punishing

## Summary

Learners explain what it means to **repair harm** rather than only punish: listen,
take responsibility, and make things right — so both people can heal. Punishment alone
can stop an act without fixing the hurt; repair fixes the hurt. This deepens Grade 2's
"real apology" into the fuller idea of repair.

## Objectives

- Explain what it means to repair harm (make things right) rather than only punish,
  using an example. _(D09.S4.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of a block tower knocked over. One way to respond is to **punish** only: send
the child away. The child is stopped, but the tower is still broken and the other
child is still sad — the **harm** is not fixed. Another way is to **repair**: the child
who knocked it over helps rebuild, says they are sorry and means it, and both children
feel better. People everywhere repair rather than only punish — a torn net is mended,
a borrowed tool returned, a trampled garden replanted. Today you will practice telling
the difference, and doing the repair.

## Materials

- A "repair vs. punish" picture strip (two paths from the same harm)
- Two puppets or soft toys
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the "repair vs. punish" strip showing one harm and two paths.
- Set up a small space for acting out the repair path.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **repair** as the fuller form of
justice for children: harm is *made right*, not only answered with a penalty. The
three repair moves are **listen** (hear the one who was hurt), **take responsibility**
(own what happened, without shame — name the *act*, never the child), and **make it
right** (do something that fixes the hurt). Punishment alone is not "bad" — it can
stop harm — but it does not, by itself, heal; repair does. Hold this as a question
("what does each path do for the person who was hurt?"), never as a forced ritual:
never make a learner confess a real, private harm or perform a scripted apology —
invented or acted harms are welcome, and a learner may always pass. A learner who does
not speak aloud points to the two paths or signs; a learner who is deaf or hard of
hearing follows the strip through sign; a learner who is blind or low-vision hears the
two paths described and names which is repair and why; a learner with a motor
difference can direct a partner to do the repair. The aim is that every learner can
say, in their own way, "repair makes things right."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time something got broken or hurt. Was it only
   punished, or was it *made right*? How did each feel?
2. **Meet "repair vs. punish" (10 min).** **Harm** is the hurt done. **Punish** means
   answering harm with a penalty that stops the act. **Repair** means making things
   right — listen, take responsibility, and fix the hurt — so both people can heal.
3. **Compare the two paths (15 min).** Look at the strip: one harm, two paths. For the
   "only punish" path, name what is still broken. For the "repair" path, name what got
   fixed. Wonder together: why does repair help the hurt person more? What does it give
   the person who did the harm?
4. **Act a repair (10 min).** With the puppets, show a small harm, then walk the repair
   path: listen, take responsibility, make it right. A learner who does not move the
   puppets can point to a repair step or direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** Repair makes things right — listen, take responsibility, fix the
   hurt — so both people can heal. Punishment alone stops; repair heals.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare the two paths with just two pictures and model "repair fixes
  the hurt, punishment alone does not"; a non-speaking learner points to the repair
  path; a learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to invent a repair for a harm that cannot be fully
  undone (a broken vase, a hurt word) and wonder what "making it right" means when the
  thing cannot be put back exactly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain what it means to repair harm
  rather than only punish, using an example?
- **Performance:** The acted-out repair path, explained aloud.

## Home connection

At home, if a small harm happens, wonder together: would punishing alone fix the hurt,
or is there a repair — a listen, a sorry, a making-it-right — that would heal better?
