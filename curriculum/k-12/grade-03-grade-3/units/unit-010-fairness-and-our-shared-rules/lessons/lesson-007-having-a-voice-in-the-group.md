# Lesson 7 — Having a Voice in the Group

## Summary

Learners explain what it means to have a **voice** in a group — to share an idea and
be listened to — and what happens when a voice is not heard: good ideas are lost, and
someone feels left out. They practice hearing quieter voices with a turn marker and a
wonder box, because a fair group hears everyone, not only the loudest.

## Objectives

- Explain what it means to have a voice in a group and what can happen when someone's
  voice is not heard. _(D09.S3.03.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time you had an idea but no one listened — you were talked over, or the
decision was made before you spoke. Having a **voice** in a group means you get to
share your idea and others *listen*. When a voice is not heard, two things are lost:
the group loses a good idea it never heard, and the person feels left out and small.
People everywhere know this — a council passes a talking stick so each voice is heard,
a family asks the youngest what they think, a class uses a box for written questions
so quiet voices count too. Today you will practice hearing every voice.

## Materials

- A "voice" picture set (a child speaking in a circle, a turn marker passed; a child
  talked over, a quieter child ignored, a decision made before everyone spoke)
- A soft object as a turn marker
- A "wonder box" and small slips of paper
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the "voice" picture set and the wonder box with slips.
- Set the turn marker in the middle of the circle; arrange a circle for sharing.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **voice and inclusion** — a
centerpiece of fair deciding. Having a voice means being able to share an idea *and*
be listened to; being heard is the point, not merely speaking. Keep it blame-free: no
one is "bad" for talking; the lesson is about *making room* for quieter voices, not
scolding louder ones. Use the **turn marker** (an ancient, global practice — a talking
stick, a shell, a stone) and the **wonder box** (written or drawn, so a learner can
share without speaking up) as concrete tools. Where questioning adults is not safe,
frame everything as *wondering together* (see `docs/contexts.md`). A learner who does
not speak aloud writes or draws a slip for the wonder box, or points to the picture
set; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing shares by sign, writing, or the marker;
a learner who is blind or low-vision hears the situations described and shares aloud
or by touch; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to place their
slip. A learner may always pass. The goal is the learner's own explanation — "a voice
means being heard, and when it is not heard…" — not a memorized phrase.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you were heard and a time you were not. How did
   each feel?
2. **Meet "voice" (10 min).** Having a **voice** means you can share your idea and be
   listened to. Look at the pictures: a voice being **heard** (a turn marker passed, a
   child speaking while others listen) and a voice **not heard** (talked over, ignored,
   decided around).
3. **Notice what is lost (10 min).** For each "not heard" picture, name what the group
   lost and how the person felt. Wonder together: could a quiet idea ever be the best
   idea?
4. **Practice hearing every voice (15 min).** Pass the turn marker around the circle;
   when you hold it, share one idea (or pass it on, or put a written slip in the wonder
   box). Listen when others hold it. Notice how it feels to have your turn held for
   you. If writing or drawing is your way, put an idea in the wonder box and have it
   read aloud later.
5. **Close (5 min).** A fair group hears every voice — not only the loudest. When a
   voice is missed, the group loses an idea and a person feels left out. You can make
   room.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one "heard" and one "not heard" picture and model "when a voice is
  missed, the group loses… and the person feels…"; a non-speaking learner points to
  the picture or puts a slip in the box; a learner with limited movement directs a
  partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to wonder why some voices are quieter (shyness, a
  different language, being new) and propose one more way to hear them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain what it means to have a voice
  in a group and what happens when a voice is not heard?
- **Performance:** The learner's part in the turn-marker circle and wonder box, with
  their own explanation of why every voice matters.

## Home connection

At home, try a "turn marker" at one family talk: whoever holds it speaks, everyone
else listens. Notice whose voice you heard that you usually don't.
