# Lesson 1 — Reasons For and Against

## Summary

Learners meet the habit at the heart of fair deciding: **weighing reasons**. Before
choosing, they collect reasons *for* a choice and reasons *against* it, then say what
they would do and why. A good decision listens to both sides before it decides — and
the "why" matters as much as the "what."

## Objectives

- Give reasons for and against a simple choice and say what they would do and why.
  _(D09.S1.03.01)_

## Connection

Think of a small choice you have faced: sharing the last piece of fruit, letting a
friend go first, keeping or returning something you found. Before you choose, you can
**weigh** the reasons — like a scale with two pans. On one side, the reasons *for*
("they are hungry too," "sharing feels kind"). On the other, the reasons *against*
("I want it first," "I might get less"). Then you decide, and you can say *why*.
People everywhere weigh reasons before they decide — a family choosing who gets the
last bowl of rice, fishers choosing where to cast the net, a class choosing a game.
Today you will practice weighing reasons for and against.

## Materials

- A "reasons for and against" chart (two columns: "for" and "against")
- Choice situation cards (sharing the last fruit, letting a friend go first, keeping
  or returning a found coin, taking a turn first)
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the two-column "for / against" chart, or draw it on the ground, cloth, or a
  shared sheet.
- Prepare the choice situation cards; have stones or seeds ready to mark each reason.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **weighing reasons**, the
foundation of the unit's moral reasoning. Model the move first as a worked example:
pick one choice, say one reason *for* and one reason *against*, then state a decision
and its "because." Keep choices small, everyday, and blame-free — there is no single
correct answer, and a learner may decide either way as long as they can give a reason.
A reason is an honest "because," and every honest reason is welcome; we do not rank
reasons as "better" people, only notice which side has more or stronger reasons. The
**balance-scale** idea is a picture, not a rule: sometimes one strong reason outweighs
several small ones, and that is worth wondering about. Hold fairness as a question
("what does each choice do for others?"), never a command. A learner who does not
speak aloud places a stone on the "for" or "against" side or points to a card; a
learner who is deaf or hard of hearing follows each reason through the chart and sign;
a learner who is blind or low-vision hears each choice and reason described and gives
their reason aloud; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to place
their stone. A learner may always pass. The goal is the learner's own reasoning —
"for… against… so I would…" — not a memorized answer.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a choice you made this week. Did you stop to think of
   reasons *for* and *against*, or just act? What was the "why"?
2. **Meet "weighing reasons" (10 min).** A **reason** is a "because." For any choice
   you can name reasons **for** (why to do it) and reasons **against** (why not to do
   it). Weighing means listening to both sides before you decide.
3. **Weigh it together (15 min).** Pick a choice card. Name reasons for and against,
   and place a stone on each side of the chart for each one. Say, sign, or point to
   what you would do — and why. Wonder together: which side has more reasons? Could one
   strong reason beat several small ones?
4. **Weigh your own choice (10 min).** On your paper, draw or write a small choice, two
   reasons for, and two reasons against. Then write or tell your decision and its
   "because." If drawing is not your way, build it with stones, act it out, or have a
   partner scribe while you direct.
5. **Close (5 min).** Before you decide, weigh the reasons for and against. Then you
   can say what you would do — and why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one choice and model two reasons aloud, one for and one against,
  with a gesture for each; a non-speaking learner places a stone on each side; a
  learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to weigh the *same* choice from two different points
  of view (their own and a friend's) and wonder whether the reasons change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner give reasons for and against a simple
  choice and say what they would do and why?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's own "my choice, reasons for and against, and
  my decision" page (or a built, acted, or partner-scribed version).

## Home connection

Tonight, before a small home decision, stop and name one reason for and one reason
against out loud. Then decide and tell a grown-up your "because."
