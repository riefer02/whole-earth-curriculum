# Lesson 12 — Environments Shape Whether We Move

## Summary

Learners audit the places where they live and work — **city streets, workplaces,
natural spaces** — and see that the environment, not just willpower, shapes whether
people move. They name **features that help movement** (a wide sidewalk, a bench, a
shaded path, a ramp) and **features that block it** (a stair with no ramp, a broken
light, no resting place), and learn that the same feature can help one body and block
another. This opens the unit's environment-and-movement-access proposal.

## Objectives

- Explain how environments — cities, workplaces, and natural spaces — shape people's
  ability to move, and begin proposing changes that widen access. _(D03.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

Two people can want to move equally, and one will walk for an hour while the other
cannot cross the street. The difference is rarely desire; it is *design*. A sidewalk
that ends, a bus with no ramp, a park with no bench, a stairway with no rail, a street
with no shade — these are not personal failures; they are choices someone made, and
different choices would change who can move. Before you ask "why don't people exercise
more?", look at the place they live. That is the question today.

## Materials

- Environment audit sheet
- Access-and-design summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the environment audit sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, the habit loop — today we ask what the *place* does to
  that loop; from Grade 8 (D03.S2.08.02), fair and inclusive movement. Today we make
  the environment the object of study.
- Prepare a plain definition: the **built environment** is the human-made world around
  us — streets, buildings, workplaces — and **access** is whether a person can actually
  use it, which design choices widen or narrow (Universal Design, S-369).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description
plus value**: environments shape movement — design features widen or narrow who can
move — and access is a matter of design choice, not of personal will (Universal Design
principles, S-369; the access-to-technology parallel, World Bank WDR, S-281). The value:
every person has the right to take part in recreation and sport regardless of ability
(CRPD Article 30, S-100), and physical activity is a fundamental right for all, with
opportunities that must be inclusive and accessible (UNESCO Charter, S-342). Keep the
fact (design shapes access) distinct from the value (access should be wide). No blaming
bodies; the environment is the object of scrutiny, never the person who cannot use it.

The **ethics** lens: a barrier is not neutral — it silently says who belongs. The
**egalitarianism** lens: the same neighborhood can be a wide-open field for one body
and a wall for another, and that gap is a fairness question. The **global** lens:
environments differ everywhere — a dense city, a farm, a coastal village, a steep
mountain town — so no single "good street" is the model. The **critical-thinking**
lens: "no one uses the path" can mean no one *can* — check the claim against the
design. The **environment** lens: the physical environment itself — heat, air, terrain, shade, and the natural spaces a person can reach — acts on bodies directly, widening or narrowing movement through climate and geography as much as through design; a life's movement is shaped by the ground it stands on. Preview: Lesson 13 turns the audit into a design that widens access.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11: what makes a habit stick? Today we ask what the
   *place* does to your habit.
2. **Meet helps and blocks (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): one street,
   two people — a runner and a wheelchair user. The curb cut and the ramp help both; the
   stair without a rail blocks one. Name the pattern: a feature that helps one body can
   block another.
3. **Audit your world (15 min).** On the audit sheet, list features that help and
   features that block movement in three places: a city or town space, a workplace or
   school, and a natural space. Use real places you know.
4. **Argue one feature (8 min).** With a partner, choose one feature you listed as a
   block and argue: who does it leave out, and what would a wider-access version be?
5. **Name the biggest barrier (8 min).** Write one sentence on the single change that
   would most widen movement in one place you audited — and who would gain.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, why "people just need to move more" is
   the wrong sentence — and what the truer one is.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Audit one place with two helps and two blocks, and skip the
  cross-place comparison.
- **Extension:** Map one route you know and annotate every help and block along it,
  then estimate who the route currently excludes.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name helps and blocks, explain how a
  feature can help one body and block another, and propose one access-widening change?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed environment audit, filed as the opening
  of the environment-and-movement-access proposal.

## Home connection

Walk your own route home or to a place you go often, and name two things that help
movement and two that block it — for you or for someone else. Write one sentence per
feature.
