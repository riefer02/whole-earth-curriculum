# Lesson 13 — Designing for Every Body

## Summary

Learners turn their environment audit into **action**: they meet the **principles of
universal design** (equitable use, flexibility in use, simple and intuitive use,
perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical effort, size and space for
approach and use — S-369), apply them to **redesign one real place** so more bodies can
move through it, and write a concrete **four-part access-widening proposal** — the
place, the barrier, the change, and who gains. This is the unit's fourth capstone
artifact.

## Objectives

- Propose a concrete change to an environment that widens people's ability to move,
  grounded in inclusive design principles. _(D03.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

A ramp instead of a stair, a bench placed where people tire, a path wide enough for two
wheelchairs to pass, a door that opens without a hard push — each of these is a small,
ordinary choice that quietly decides who belongs. The people who build our cities,
schools, and parks made thousands of such choices, mostly without asking everyone. But
*you* can make the next one, and propose it, and hand it to someone who can build it.
Design is a way of caring for strangers at scale. Today you practice it.

## Materials

- Access proposal template
- Universal-design summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the access proposal template.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 12, your environment audit and the biggest barrier you named.
  Today we turn the audit into a *proposal*.
- Prepare a plain definition: **universal design** is design that works for as many
  people as possible from the start — the seven principles include equitable use,
  flexibility, simplicity, perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical
  effort, and size and space for approach and use (S-369).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **skill plus
value**: good design widens access, and the **seven principles of universal design**
(equitable use, flexibility in use, simple and intuitive use, perceptible information,
tolerance for error, low physical effort, size and space for approach and use — S-369)
are one tested way to get there. The digital parallel — web accessibility standards
(perceivable, operable, understandable, robust — W3C WCAG, S-280) — shows the same
logic in another domain. The value: inclusive access to movement is a right (CRPD
Article 30, S-100; UNESCO Charter, S-342). Teach the four-part proposal scaffold
explicitly (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011). Honor trade-offs:
designs have costs and constraints, and the skill is to widen access within them, not to
pretend constraints away.

The **ethics** lens: design is ethics made concrete — a ramp is a moral statement about
who belongs. The **egalitarianism** lens: universal design widens the door for everyone,
not as a favor to a "special" few but as the baseline. The **global** lens: a change
that works in a dense city may not fit a steep mountain village or a flood-prone coast —
good design answers to its place. The **critical-thinking** lens: a "universal" solution
is a claim to check — test it against bodies and lives unlike your own. The **technology & environment** lens: universal design and web accessibility are the same idea in two materials — a ramp widens a doorway and a screen reader widens a page — so designing for every body applies to the built world and the digital one alike. ## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12: what was the biggest barrier you named? Today we
   propose the fix.
2. **Meet the principles (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a building with
   only stairs. Run it against the principles — is it equitable use? Flexible? Low
   physical effort? Then show the redesign: a ramp *and* the stairs, a handrail, a
   visible sign, a rest landing. Name which principles each change serves.
3. **Sketch the redesign (12 min).** Choose one place from your audit and sketch the
   change — on paper, no need for artistry. Label what you added and why.
4. **Write the proposal (15 min).** On the template, write the four parts: the place,
   the barrier, the change, and who gains. Make the change concrete and buildable.
5. **Peer-test (8 min).** Swap proposals. Each of you asks one question the other must
   answer: "who does your change still leave out, and what would help them too?"
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, why a ramp is not a favor — it is a door.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Propose one change to one barrier and write two of the four parts (place
  and change), discussing the rest aloud.
- **Extension:** Test your redesign against all seven principles and revise for the one
  it most fails, then note the trade-off you accepted.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The four-part access-widening proposal — the unit's fourth
  capstone artifact, linking movement to the shared, built world.
- **Peer:** Could a partner name the barrier, the change, and who gains from the
  proposal?

## Home connection

Show your proposal to someone at home and ask who else the change would help and who it
might still leave out. Write one sentence about each.
