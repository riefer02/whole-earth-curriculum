# Lesson 4 — A Framework I Can Carry and Explain

## Summary

Learners assemble the unit's first three lessons into one **four-spoke framework** —
**notice, name, regulate, reflect** — that turns scattered skills into a portable,
explainable whole. Each learner writes the framework in their own words and then
*explains it to someone else* in plain language. This is the unit's first capstone
artifact: a **written lifelong body–mind framework** the learner can carry and pass on
(D03.S1.12.01).

## Objectives

- Integrate the body–mind connection into a personal framework for lifelong awareness
  and wellbeing, and explain it clearly to others. _(D03.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

A skill you cannot explain is a skill you only half-own. Knowing *that* you feel your
body, read its signals, and help it return to calm is one thing; being able to hand that
to someone else — a friend who is overwhelmed, a younger sibling, a person you are caring
for — is another. The act of explaining is not a test of memory; it is how a framework
becomes *yours*. Today you build the framework and then prove you own it by teaching it.

## Materials

- Framework wheel
- Unit recap (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the framework wheel.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–3, the loop, precise reading, and the stress wave and
  return. Today we *synthesize* them into one framework and practice explaining it.
- Prepare a plain definition: a **framework** is a small set of named steps you can
  carry and reuse — not a rulebook, but a reusable pattern (worked example first:
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a framework is only
yours once you can explain it** — explaining is retrieval and elaboration, which is what
makes learning durable (Dunlosky et al., S-039). The four spokes (notice → name →
regulate → reflect) are offered as *one reusable pattern, not a universal law*: learners
may rename, reorder, or swap spokes, so long as the framework is honest to the real
body–mind connection and something they would actually use. Honor every body: the
framework must work for a body that senses differently, moves differently, or needs
different tools — no single "correct" calm-returning method is imposed.

The **ethics** lens: a framework is judged by what it lets you *do* for yourself and
others, not by how impressive it sounds. The **egalitarianism** lens: the plain-language
version is the real one — if it cannot be explained to a child or a busy friend, it is
not yet owned. The **global** lens: learners may carry body–mind wisdom from their own
tradition; the framework should make room for it rather than replace it. The
**critical-thinking** lens: every step is a hypothesis to check against the body, not a
ritual to obey. The **technology** lens: apps and trackers can prompt the first two spokes — notice and name — but a framework you can carry must work with no device, no signal, and no battery, because it lives in you, not in the tool. Preview: Lesson 5 turns from the present moment to the whole lifetime.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In one line each, name what you learned in Lessons 1, 2, and 3.
   Today we bind them into one thing you can carry.
2. **Meet the wheel (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a person before a
   hard conversation. **Notice** the body (tight chest), **name** it ("I'm braced"),
   **regulate** (slow exhale, drop the shoulders), **reflect** ("what do I actually want
   to say?"). Run the four spokes on one more shared example together.
3. **Write your own (12 min).** In your journal, write the four spokes in your own
   words, with one real example threaded through all four. Add one line on how *your*
   body does each step.
4. **Explain it (10 min).** Pair up. Each of you explains your framework to the other in
   under a minute, then answers one question. Switch.
5. **Refine after explaining (8 min).** Change whatever was unclear when you said it
   aloud — a word, an order, an example. Write the final plain-language version.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, what changed about your framework when you
   had to explain it to someone else.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the four given spokes as-is and explain just the first two aloud,
  leaving the rest to the written version.
- **Extension:** Write a one-line version of the whole framework that a younger child
  could understand, and a one-line version for a stressed adult — and name how the two
  differ and why.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The written lifelong body–mind framework — four spokes in
  the learner's own words, threaded with a real example, and rendered in plain language
  someone else could follow. This is the unit's first capstone artifact.
- **Peer:** Did the partner understand the explanation well enough to restate one spoke
  correctly?

## Home connection

Explain your framework to someone at home and ask them to repeat it back in their own
words. Write one sentence about what they understood easily and one about what they had
to ask you to repeat.
