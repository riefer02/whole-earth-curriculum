# Lesson 7 — Caring for Another's Body and Dignity

## Summary

Learners turn dignity outward: how to **care for another person's body** — across aging,
illness, or disability — while protecting their **agency**. They learn the
**ask–listen–adapt** loop and come to see **assistive technology** (wheelchairs, hearing
aids, and other aids) as ordinary tools that widen a person's own choices (WHO, S-099).
This completes the unit's **across-a-lifetime embodied-health reflection** on sustaining
both one's own and others' dignity.

## Objectives

- Articulate how to sustain others' embodied health and dignity across a lifetime,
  including through caregiving that protects agency. _(D03.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Almost everyone will, at some point, help care for another body — an aging parent, a
sick friend, a child, a partner after an injury. And almost everyone will, at some
point, *be* cared for. The difference between care that honors and care that humiliates
is not how much you do; it is *whose wishes stay in charge*. The skill is learning to
help without taking over — to support another person's dignity so that being helped
never costs them their "yes" and "no."

## Materials

- Care circle
- Assistive-technology summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the care circle.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, the three fields of dignity (autonomy, consent, care);
  from Grade 11 (D03.S4.11.02), designing respectful support for chronic conditions and
  disabilities. Today we put dignity to work in *caregiving*.
- Prepare a plain definition: **assistive technology** is any tool — a wheelchair,
  glasses, a hearing aid, a cane — that helps a person do what they want to do; such
  aids are common, ordinary tools used worldwide (WHO, S-099). **Supported autonomy**
  is help that increases a person's own choices rather than replacing them.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description
plus value**: caregiving can protect or erode dignity, and the difference is whether the
cared-for person's **agency** stays in charge — so the core skill is ask–listen–adapt.
Assistive technology (wheelchairs, hearing aids, prosthetics, white canes) is common and
ordinary, used worldwide (WHO, S-099), and the right to take part in recreation and
sport regardless of disability is a shared human commitment (CRPD Article 30, S-100).
Hold the value plainly as value. Normalize help: needing or giving care is not a mark of
lesser worth on either side.

The **ethics** lens: help that ignores a person's wishes is not help — it is control,
even when kindly meant. The **egalitarianism** lens: care work falls unevenly across
gender, class, and family roles worldwide, and that distribution is a question of
justice, not only of kindness. The **global** lens: cultures arrange care in many ways —
extended family, community, institutions — and none is the single "right" model. The
**critical-thinking** lens: "what is best for you" is a claim to check against the
person's own stated wishes. The **technology** lens: assistive technology — a wheelchair, a hearing aid, a screen reader, a voice device — is dignity made mechanical, widening a person's own choices rather than replacing them; the ask–listen–adapt loop applies to choosing tools, not only to giving help. Preview: Lesson 8 pivots from dignity to movement as a
lifelong plan.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: what are the three fields of dignity? Today we
   ask how to *give* that dignity to another body we are helping.
2. **Meet ask–listen–adapt (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): helping an
   older relative who now needs help dressing. **Ask** what they want, **listen** to the
   real answer (including the "no"), **adapt** your help to their wishes — offer the
   arm, not the takeover. Contrast with the same scene done as control.
3. **Sort the moments (10 min).** With a partner, sort short caregiving moments as
   "supported autonomy" or "took over," and name what each would have to change to move
   toward the first.
4. **Draw your circle (12 min).** On the care circle, write one real or imagined
   example of asking, one of listening, one of adapting — for a person you might one day
   care for.
5. **Reflect across a lifetime (10 min).** Write the across-a-lifetime reflection: one
   paragraph on how you will sustain your *own* embodied dignity and one on how you will
   protect *another's* — through aging, illness, and change.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, how "I'll do everything for you" can fail a
   person, and what the better promise is.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give one example of each ring from your own family or community, and
  write the reflection as two sentences rather than a paragraph.
- **Extension:** Research one assistive technology and how it widens a person's choices,
  then connect it to the dignity frame (e.g., a hearing aid, a screen reader, a
  prosthetic).

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The completed care circle and the two-part
  across-a-lifetime reflection (sustaining one's own and others' dignity) — the unit's
  second capstone artifact.
- **Peer:** Can a partner name the difference between the learner's "supported autonomy"
  and "took over" examples?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home who has helped care for another person what they learned about
protecting the person's dignity. Write one sentence about their answer and one about
what you will do differently.
