# Lesson 8 — Movement Feeds the Whole Person

## Summary

Learners map how **movement feeds the whole person at once** — physical health
(strength, endurance, a working heart), mental health (mood, reduced symptoms of anxiety
and depression), and social health (belonging, shared play). They learn that no single
chemical explains the mental-health link; several mechanisms work together — endorphins,
monoamines, and BDNF among them (Mikkelsen et al., S-408) — and that regular physical
activity carries significant physical and mental health benefits (WHO, S-341). They log
one personal benefit in each branch.

## Objectives

- Explain how movement supports physical, mental, and social health together, building
  toward a lifelong movement plan. _(D03.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last time you moved on purpose — a walk, a game, a dance, a swim, a climb.
Afterward, more than your legs changed: your mood shifted, your head cleared, and if you
moved with someone, you probably felt closer to them. Movement is one of the few things
that feeds the body, the mind, and the company of others *at the same time*. That is why
it belongs in every life, at every age — not as a punishment for eating, but as a
lifelong ally.

## Materials

- Three-branch map
- Exercise-and-mental-health summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-branch map.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D03.S2.08.01), strength, endurance, and flexibility; from
  Lesson 5, the body across a lifetime. Today we ask *why movement matters*, across all
  three dimensions of health.
- Prepare a plain definition: **whole-person benefit** means movement helps physical,
  mental, and social health together — not one at a time.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **evidence,
hedged honestly**: regular physical activity supports significant physical *and* mental
health benefits (WHO, S-341), and the exercise–mood link involves several mechanisms —
endorphins, monoamines (serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline), and BDNF among them — not
one magic chemical (Mikkelsen et al., S-408). Model the honest hedge: this is a
correlation-and-mechanism story, not "exercise cures depression." Movement is *one*
support, not a replacement for care a person may need. No body-shaming, ever: the frame
is "movement as ally," never "you must look or perform a certain way."

The **ethics** lens: prescribing movement to *others* is done with care — it can help and
it can also become a demand. The **egalitarianism** lens: access to safe, joyful movement
is not equal, and the social branch matters most where isolation is deepest. The
**global** lens: what "movement" means differs everywhere — dancing, farming, walking,
games, swimming — and no single form is the default. The **critical-thinking** lens:
"movement improves mood" is a claim with real evidence, but we read it with the
correlation/causation care it deserves. The **technology** lens: labor-saving devices and screens have made sitting the default in many lives, which is exactly why deliberate movement now matters more — and a wearable can measure movement but cannot supply the motive, which still comes from the person. Preview: Lesson 9 names the building blocks of a
movement life.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: how does a body change across life? Today we ask
   what movement gives it at every age.
2. **Meet the three branches (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a person
   who starts a short daily walk. Trace what changes — physical (heart, strength),
   mental (mood, sleep, clearer head), social (a walking partner, a hello). Name the
   three branches and the honest hedge: several mechanisms, not one chemical.
3. **Sort the benefits (10 min).** With a partner, sort a list of benefits into the
   three branches, arguing the ones that span two or three.
4. **Log your own (10 min).** On the three-branch map, write one benefit you have
   actually felt from movement in each branch — physical, mental, social.
5. **Argue the evidence (8 min).** In pairs, take the claim "movement improves mood."
   Is it correlation, causation, or both? Name what would convince you it is causal.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, why movement belongs in *every* life — and
   why it is an ally, not a punishment.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Log one benefit in a single branch and discuss the others aloud; skip the
  correlation/causation argument.
- **Extension:** Find one study or report on exercise and mental health and evaluate
  whether its claim is stronger than "linked to" — and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three branches, sort benefits
  with reasons, and log one authentic benefit in each?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed three-branch map, filed as the opening of
  the lifelong movement plan.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what moving does for them — body, mind, or company. Write one
sentence about their answer and one about which branch matters most to you now.
