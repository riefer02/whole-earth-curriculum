# Lesson 5 — A Body Across a Lifetime

## Summary

Learners map the body across a **whole lifespan** — child, teen, adult, older adult —
and learn that the body **changes at every stage**, trading one set of capacities for
another rather than simply declining. They meet **functional capacity** as something
that shifts across life and can be supported at every age (WHO, S-341). They replace a
fear-of-aging story with a **change-and-adaptation** story in which **dignity** stays
constant even as the body shifts, opening the unit's across-a-lifetime reflection.

## Objectives

- Describe how bodies change across a lifetime and articulate how to honor those
  changes, building toward sustaining embodied health and dignity. _(D03.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Somewhere near you, right now, there is likely a child who can run all day but cannot
tie every shoe, a teenager whose body is changing faster than their sense of it, an
adult whose knees remember old games, and an older person whose balance asks for a rail
but whose patience is deeper than it ever was. None of these is a broken version of the
others. Each is a body at a *stage*, trading one thing for another. The story you inherit
about aging — that it is only loss — is one story, and it is not the truest one. Today
you learn to tell the truer one: a body changes, and you can meet each change with
adaptation and dignity.

## Materials

- Lifespan arc
- Lifespan-movement summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the lifespan arc.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, the framework you built — today we stretch it across time,
  from one moment to a whole life.
- Prepare a plain definition: **functional capacity** is what a body can do, day to day —
  and it shifts across life and can be supported at every age (WHO, S-341). A
  **value-frame** we hold separately: dignity is constant even as the body changes.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description
plus an explicit value**: the body changes across life — capacities shift and can be
supported at every age (WHO's functional-capacity-across-the-lifespan framing, S-341) —
*and* the judgment that every stage carries dignity is a **value we choose**, not a fact
to prove. Keep the two distinct: "bodies change" is evidence; "aging is not decline" is
partly evidence (some capacities hold or grow) and partly a values commitment against
ageism. No single "ideal" body at any age; every stage is a whole stage, not a failed
younger stage.

The **ethics** lens: how we treat aging bodies is a test of how we treat people. The
**egalitarianism** lens: some bodies change early, some late, some through illness or
injury — the arc looks different for everyone, and no arc is "lesser." The **global**
lens: cultures honor elders and aging in sharply different ways — some center elders,
some push them to the edge — and the lesson holds many ways side by side without
ranking. The **critical-thinking** lens: "decline" is a claim to check, not a word to
accept — ask *which* capacity, *when*, and *what supports it*. The **technology & environment** lens: a body changing across life meets a built world that either supports it — a rail, a bench, a ramp, a lift — or abandons it, and assistive and medical technology extend what an aging body can do; the difference between a hard old age and a full one is often design, not biology. from the changing body to the dignity that stays constant.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what are your four spokes? Today we ask how the
   body that runs your framework changes over a lifetime.
2. **Meet the arc (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a single person's body
   at four ages — the child who runs everywhere, the teen in a growth spurt, the adult
   balancing work and movement, the older adult whose balance needs a rail but whose
   knowledge and patience grow. At each stage name one *gain* and one *shift*.
3. **Build your arc (12 min).** On the lifespan arc, write one real or imagined "what a
   body can do" and one "what a body needs" at each stage. Mark where you are now.
4. **Argue change vs. decline (10 min).** With a partner, choose one capacity and argue:
   is its change a loss, a trade, or a gain? Agree on a fairer word than "decline."
5. **Name the constant (8 min).** If the body changes at every stage, what stays the
   same? Write one sentence on the dignity that does not age.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, what is wrong with the sentence "aging is
   only loss" — and what the truer sentence is.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill two stages (your own and one older) with one capability and one need
  each, leaving the full arc aside.
- **Extension:** Interview or read about one older person's life and note one capacity
  that *grew* with age — then connect it to how their community supports (or fails to
  support) them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner describe change across at least two life
  stages, distinguish change from decline, and name what stays constant?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed lifespan arc, filed as the opening of the
  across-a-lifetime embodied-health reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an older person in your life what their body could do at your age and what it does
differently now — and one thing they are *better* at now than then. Write one sentence
about each.
