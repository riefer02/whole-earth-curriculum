# Lesson 11 — Making It Stick and Adapting

## Summary

Learners turn their movement plan into a **durable habit** using a
**cue–routine–reward** loop, and — just as importantly — write a **setback plan** so
that a missed week is a bend in the road, not the end of it. They learn that movement
and sleep are partners in a healthy routine (teenagers need about 8–10 hours, AASM,
S-050) and that adolescence is a formative stretch for building habits that last (WHO,
S-311). This completes the lifelong, adaptable movement strand.

## Objectives

- Make movement a durable, adaptable habit — and recover from setbacks — so the plan
  lasts across a lifetime. _(D03.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Most movement plans do not fail because they are wrong; they fail because a single
missed week becomes a verdict: "I'm not a person who exercises." The truth is quieter.
A habit is a loop — a cue that starts it, a routine you do, a reward that makes you
want to do it again — and a setback is just a loop that broke once. The skill is not
perfection; it is *restarting*. Today you build the loop and, more important, the plan
for what happens when it breaks.

## Materials

- Habit-loop card
- Sleep-and-habit summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the habit-loop card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, your FITT plan; from Lesson 3, the return to calm. Today
  we ask how a plan becomes a *habit* and survives a *setback*.
- Prepare a plain definition: a **habit loop** has three parts — a **cue** (the trigger),
  a **routine** (the action), and a **reward** (what makes you want to repeat it).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **skill plus
value**: a habit is a cue–routine–reward loop, and a **setback plan** is what makes a
habit durable — the restart, not the perfect streak, is the skill. Movement is one part
of a healthy routine that also includes sleep (about 8–10 hours for teenagers, AASM,
S-050), and adolescence is a formative time for building habits that persist (WHO,
S-311). Teach the loop explicitly (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
S-011). No shame about broken streaks — the lesson itself treats a setback as
information, never a verdict.

The **ethics** lens: a promise you restart after breaking is stronger than a promise you
never break, because it is honest about being human. The **egalitarianism** lens: not
everyone's day has room for a gym — the loop should be built on cues and rewards that
fit real, crowded, low-resource lives. The **global** lens: habits are anchored to the
specific textures of a life (a market day, a harvest, a commute, a call to prayer), so
no single cue is universal. The **critical-thinking** lens: "I'm not a person who
exercises" is a claim about identity, not a fact — check it against one small restart.
The **technology** lens: habit apps and phone reminders are today's most common cue-and-reward scaffolds, and they can help — but a durable loop should also anchor to a real-world cue (a door, a tree, a time of day) so the habit survives a lost phone or a dead battery. Preview: Lesson 12 pivots from the plan to the *places* that help or block movement.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10: what are your four FITT levers? Today we ask how
   the plan survives the long run.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a person who wants to
   walk more. **Cue** — shoes by the door; **routine** — a ten-minute walk after a meal;
   **reward** — a calm head and a chat with a neighbor. Show how the cue does the
   remembering so willpower does not have to.
3. **Build your loop (10 min).** On the habit-loop card, write one cue, one routine, and
   one reward for your own plan. Make the cue something already in your day.
4. **Write the setback plan (10 min).** Name a realistic setback — a busy week, an
   illness, a cold season — and write exactly what you will do to restart (start small,
   half the time, a gentler form). Write it as "when …, I will …."
5. **Rehearse the restart (8 min).** With a partner, role-play the setback: one of you
   says "I missed two weeks," the other reads the restart plan back. Swap.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, what matters more than never missing a day —
   and why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build the loop with one cue and one reward from an existing daily
  routine, and write the setback plan as a single "when … I will …" sentence.
- **Extension:** Research how sleep and movement interact, and add one sleep-related cue
  to your loop (e.g., setting the shoes out at the time you wind down).

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three parts of the loop, build
  one for their plan, and write a concrete restart plan?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed habit-loop card with the setback plan,
  filed alongside the movement plan as its "stick" plan.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what habit they have kept for years and what keeps it going. Write
one sentence about their cue and one about what you will borrow.
