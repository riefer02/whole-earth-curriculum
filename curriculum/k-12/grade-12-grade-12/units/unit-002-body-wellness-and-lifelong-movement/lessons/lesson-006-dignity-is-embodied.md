# Lesson 6 — Dignity Is Embodied

## Summary

Learners name **embodied dignity** as something each person carries and tends — not
something earned by a particular kind of body. They fill a three-field shield —
**autonomy, consent, care** — with their own commitments, and learn that dignity is a
right held by every person regardless of ability, grounded in a shared human-rights
frame (UDHR Article 1, S-149) and held here explicitly as a **value commitment**, not a
fact to prove.

## Objectives

- Articulate how to sustain one's own embodied dignity — autonomy, consent, and care for
  the body — across a lifetime. _(D03.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time someone touched you, commented on your body, or made a decision for you
without asking. Or a time someone *asked* first, listened to your no, and left you
feeling more like yourself. Those two moments are the difference dignity is made of. It
is not a luxury reserved for some bodies; it is the baseline every body is owed. The
work of a lifetime is partly external — being treated with respect — and partly internal:
treating your own body, at every age and in every condition, as worthy of care and its
own "yes" and "no."

## Materials

- Dignity shield
- Dignity-and-rights summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the dignity shield.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D03.S4.08.01), body autonomy and consent; from Lesson 5, the
  body that changes across a lifetime. Today we name what stays constant: dignity.
- Prepare a plain definition: **dignity** here is the worth every person carries simply
  by being a person — held as a value commitment (UDHR Article 1, S-149), not a factual
  claim.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **value,
plainly labeled as value**: every person is born with dignity and worth (UDHR Article 1,
S-149), and every person has the right to take part in recreation and sport regardless
of disability (CRPD Article 30, S-100). These are *shared human commitments*, not
scientific facts — the lesson must keep that distinction clean. The three fields
(autonomy, consent, care) are one way to organize the practice of dignity, not a
universal law.

The **ethics** lens: dignity is about what we owe — to ourselves and to others — before
any question of what we can *do*. The **egalitarianism** lens: dignity is not graded by
ability, age, appearance, or productivity; a body that needs help is not a body with
less worth. The **global** lens: how dignity is expressed differs across cultures and
families, so no single expression is the default — but the underlying claim of equal
worth is the shared ground. The **critical-thinking** lens: "care" and "control" can
look alike, and we learn to tell them apart by asking whose "yes" and "no" count.
The **technology** lens: bodily autonomy now extends beyond touch to data — who tracks, stores, and shares information about our bodies — so consent and dignity apply to devices and records as much as to hands. Preview: Lesson 7 extends dignity from oneself to caring for another.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: what changes across a lifetime, and what stays
   constant? Today we give the constant a name — dignity.
2. **Meet the three fields (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): an older
   person, or a person with a disability, deciding about their own care. **Autonomy** —
   the decision is theirs. **Consent** — their yes and no are asked for and honored.
   **Care** — the body is tended with respect, not merely managed. Run one more shared
   example together.
3. **Fill your shield (12 min).** On the dignity shield, write one commitment in each
   field for *your own* body: a boundary you will hold (autonomy), a "no" you will honor
   (consent), and a way you will tend your body with respect rather than shame (care).
4. **Argue care vs. control (10 min).** With a partner, take a caregiving case and argue
   where "caring for" becomes "controlling." Name the test you used to tell them apart.
5. **Name the right (8 min).** Write one sentence on why dignity belongs to every body
   equally — and one on what that requires of you toward your own body.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, what "my body is mine" means at every age,
   including the ages when your body may need help.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill one field (consent) with one clear boundary, and discuss the other
  two aloud rather than in writing.
- **Extension:** Find one real policy or practice where a group's bodily autonomy was
  overridden in the name of care, and argue what a dignity-preserving version would look
  like.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner define the three fields, distinguish care
  from control, and write one authentic commitment in each?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed dignity shield, filed in the journal as
  part of the across-a-lifetime reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what "dignity" means to them when it comes to their own body, and
one way they keep it. Write one sentence about their answer and one about how it differs
from yours.
