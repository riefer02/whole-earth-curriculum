# Lesson 2 — Reading the Body's Signals

## Summary

Learners move from a vague sense that "something feels off" to a **precise,
zone-by-zone reading** of the body's signals. They learn the difference between a raw
**sensation** (a flutter) and an **interpretation** of it ("I'm afraid"), and they meet
**emotional granularity** — the idea that a richer vocabulary of body feeling is linked
to finer awareness and better regulation (Barrett, S-401). They refine two of their own
vague words into precise ones and label their own body map.

## Objectives

- Read the body's signals with greater precision and connect precise awareness to better
  self-care, building toward a personal framework. _(D03.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

"Something feels off" is a real signal — but it is a blurry one. The person who can only
say "I feel bad" has fewer options than the person who can say "my jaw is tight and my
breath is high and shallow, and that usually means I am bracing before a hard
conversation." Precision is not fussiness; it is *information*. The more exactly you can
read your own body, the more exactly you can care for it — whether that care is a slow
breath, a rest, a glass of water, or a hard word spoken on time.

## Materials

- Body-signal map
- Signal word slips (low-tech: paper)
- Emotional-granularity summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the body-signal map; prepare the signal word slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the body–mind loop. Today we sharpen the *reading* of the
  body's half of that loop.
- Prepare a plain definition: **emotional granularity** is the fineness with which a
  person can name a feeling — a richer vocabulary of body feeling, linked to better
  awareness and regulation (one well-supported account, held as description, not a
  ranking of people: Barrett, S-401).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **precision in reading
the body is a skill, not a talent — and it is linked to better self-care** (Barrett's
emotional-granularity account, S-401). Hold it as *description*: finer naming is
associated with better regulation, but no one is ranked by it, and some bodies and
neurotypes signal differently or more quietly — that is difference, not deficit. Never
let this become "your body is wrong or too vague"; the point is a *tool the learner
owns*, not a test they pass.

The **ethics** lens: accurate self-reading lets us act on what is real rather than on a
blurry alarm, which protects both ourselves and others. The **egalitarianism** lens:
access to a rich feeling vocabulary is not evenly distributed — some languages and
cultures name body states far more finely than others, and no vocabulary is "better" as
a person. The **global** lens: different traditions locate feelings in different parts of
the body (the heart, the gut, the chest, the liver), so no single body map is universal.
The **critical-thinking** lens: a sensation is data; an interpretation is a guess — we
learn to keep the two apart and check the guess. The **technology** lens: wearables and health apps give a coarse, external read — heart rate, steps, sleep — but they cannot do the precise inner reading, so a device is a helper to check against, never a replacement for the sensor you already are. ## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: where does a feeling start for you — body or mind?
   Today we read the body's half with more precision.
2. **Meet precision (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): "I feel bad" vs.
   "my chest is tight, my breath is shallow, and I keep holding my shoulders up." The
   second names *where* and *what*, and it opens a door the first cannot — you can act on
   a tight chest (breathe, drop the shoulders); "bad" gives you nothing to do. Name the
   move: sensation first, then interpretation, then a check.
3. **Sort the words (10 min).** With a partner, sort the signal words as "more precise"
   or "more vague," and for each precise one, name a body zone it might come from.
4. **Label your map (12 min).** On your body map, write one precise signal you have
   actually felt in at least three zones, and a vague word that covers each one.
5. **Refine two words (8 min).** Choose two vague words from your own life and refine
   each into a precise phrase (zone + quality + what it usually means). Write what the
   precision lets you *do* that the vague word could not.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, why "I feel bad" is the beginning of
   self-care, not the end of it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Label one zone with one precise word and skip the refinement of vague
  words; build the word bank together instead.
- **Extension:** Compare two languages or traditions you know for how finely they name a
  single body feeling, and write one sentence on what that reveals.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish sensation from
  interpretation, sort words by precision, and refine one vague word into a precise
  phrase with a plausible action?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The labeled body-signal map, filed in the journal as
  part of the emerging framework.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for the most precise word they have for a body feeling you both know,
in any language they speak. Write it down with what it means and one body zone it lives
in.
