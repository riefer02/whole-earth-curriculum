# Lesson 9 — The Building Blocks of a Movement Life

## Summary

Learners name the **four building blocks** of a movement life — **aerobic, strength,
balance, flexibility** — and learn that a balanced routine draws on all four, with
**balance and functional activity** mattering especially as people age (WHO, S-341).
They sort everyday activities under the blocks and discover that many build two or three
at once, laying the foundation for designing their own plan.

## Objectives

- Identify the building blocks of movement and how they work together across a lifetime,
  building toward a personal movement plan. _(D03.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Movement is not one thing. Carrying a child up a hill, dancing all night, sitting on the
floor and rising again, holding a plank — each asks something different of a body. A
movement life, like a sturdy house, is built of a few blocks: the endurance to keep
going (aerobic), the power to lift and carry (strength), the steadiness to stay upright
and catch a fall (balance), and the range to reach and bend (flexibility). You are
probably already using all four every day without naming them. Today you learn the
names, so you can build with them on purpose.

## Materials

- Four-block foundation
- Activity cards (low-tech: paper)
- Balanced-routine summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four-block foundation; prepare the activity cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D03.S2.08.01), strength, endurance, and flexibility; from
  Lesson 8, why movement matters. Today we name the *components* of movement.
- Prepare a plain definition: **aerobic** activity is sustained movement that raises
  heart and breath; **strength** work makes muscles work against resistance;
  **balance** keeps you steady; **flexibility** keeps the range of motion. A balanced
  routine draws on all four (WHO, S-341).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **evidence,
hedged honestly**: a balanced routine includes aerobic (endurance) and muscle-
strengthening activity, with balance and flexibility part of functional capacity across
the lifespan — and balance/functional training matters especially for older adults
(WHO, S-341). This is a *general guide, not a prescription*: intensity, form, and
safety are individual, and every body adapts the blocks to its own needs. No single
"correct" routine is imposed; a wheelchair user, a person with a chronic condition, and
an elite athlete all use the same four blocks, differently.

The **ethics** lens: the blocks are tools for health and joy, never a yardstick of
worth. The **egalitarianism** lens: "exercise" is not only gyms and sports — carrying
water, farming, sweeping, dancing, and care work all build these blocks, and those are
as real as any workout. The **global** lens: the same four blocks appear in countless
forms worldwide, so no single form is the default. The **critical-thinking** lens: a
claim about "the best exercise" is a claim to check against the four blocks and the
person's own body. The **technology & environment** lens: the four blocks were once built invisibly by the physical work of daily environments — farming, carrying, climbing — and labor-saving technology has quietly removed much of that incidental movement, so most people must now add the blocks back on purpose. Preview: Lesson 10 turns the blocks into your own plan.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: what three kinds of health does movement feed?
   Today we name the *components* of movement itself.
2. **Meet the four blocks (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a farmer's
   day — carrying water (strength), walking the slopes (aerobic), steadying on uneven
   ground (balance), bending to plant (flexibility). Name the four blocks and how one
   day builds all of them.
3. **Sort the cards (12 min).** With a partner, sort each activity card under the block
   it most builds — and mark the ones that build two or three at once.
4. **Find your blocks (12 min).** On the four-block foundation, write one real activity
   from your own life for each block. Name one block you have plenty of and one you are
   missing.
5. **Design a balance add-on (8 min).** In pairs, design a simple two-activity balance
   routine someone could do at an older age (e.g., standing on one leg by a wall, then
   a slow sit-and-rise). Note why balance matters more with age (WHO, S-341).
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, why "exercise" is bigger than gyms and
   sports.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest cards and name one activity per block from daily
  life, leaving the balance design aside.
- **Extension:** Audit one person's real day (your own or a family member's) and map
  which blocks it already builds and which it misses.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four blocks, sort activities
  with reasons, and identify a block they have and a block they lack?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed four-block foundation, filed as the
  scaffold for the lifelong movement plan.

## Home connection

Watch one ordinary day at home and name one activity that builds each of the four blocks
for someone there. Write one sentence per block.
