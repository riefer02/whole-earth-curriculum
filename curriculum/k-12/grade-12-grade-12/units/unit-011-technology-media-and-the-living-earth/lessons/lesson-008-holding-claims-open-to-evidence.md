# Lesson 8 — Holding Claims Open to Evidence

## Summary

Learners practice epistemic humility: **confidence in a belief should track the evidence**, and
a person should be able to say **what would change their mind**. They learn how science itself
corrects — a striking result (the 2011 faster-than-light neutrino claim) that was later
withdrawn after it failed replication — and apply the discipline to one belief of their own,
steering between the two traps of dogmatism (my side is certainly right) and cynicism (nothing
can be known).

## Objectives

- Reflect on how they will stay informed in a flood of information, holding claims open to
  evidence rather than settling into certainty. _(D10.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

You already hold hundreds of beliefs with different levels of confidence: you are nearly certain
the sun will rise, fairly sure of a fact you half-remember, and only tentatively convinced by a
claim you read once. The honest mind keeps that difference visible — it says "I am very
confident about this, less about that, and here is what would change my mind." Certainty is not
a sign of strength; matching your confidence to your evidence is.

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the confidence sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, your media diet. Today we go one level deeper — not just *checking*
  claims but holding our *own* beliefs open.
- Prepare the worked example (the OPERA neutrino result) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **confidence should track the
evidence — I can say how sure I am and what would change my mind — and this is the middle path
between dogmatism and cynicism.** Model the confidence sheet and the OPERA example explicitly
(S-011). This is the unit's deepest "evidence not certainty" move; teach it as a habit of
honesty, never as doubt for its own sake, and never as a weapon against a learner's values —
beliefs held as *values* are treated with respect, while *factual* claims are held open to
evidence.

The **ethics** lens: intellectual honesty is an ethical virtue — the willingness to update a
belief is respect for the truth over the comfort of being right. The **egalitarianism** lens:
holding claims open is a leveler — it protects ordinary people from being certain into error by
the loudest voices, because anyone can ask "what would change your mind?" (S-278). The
**global** lens: the self-correcting habit is universal to science worldwide — peer review and
replication are how a community, not any one authority, converges on what is true (S-489).
The **technology** lens: the flood and the filter bubble reward certainty, because confident,
simple claims travel fastest — which is exactly why the discipline of tracking evidence is a
counterweight (S-481). The **environment** lens: on the living Earth, overconfidence about a
risky claim can do lasting harm, while honest uncertainty lets people act carefully as evidence
grows (S-005). Preview: Lesson 9 turns to the Earth itself as one interconnected system.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, one habit of your media diet. Today we apply the same
   honesty to our *own* beliefs.
2. **Learn the discipline (10 min).** Confidence should **track the evidence**: state a claim,
   rate your confidence (low, medium, high), list evidence for and against, and — most
   important — write **what would change your mind**. A belief you cannot imagine changing is
   held as faith, not as evidence. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — why
   "what would change my mind" matters.
3. **Worked example — science correcting itself (15 min).** In 2011, an experiment reported
   neutrinos apparently traveling faster than light — a result that would overturn a pillar of
   physics. Instead of celebrating, scientists asked for replication; the result failed to hold
   and was traced to a measurement error, and was withdrawn (S-488). This is not a failure of
   science — it is science working: **claims are held open, checked, and updated** (S-489).
4. **Apply it to a belief of your own (15 min).** Choose one factual belief you hold. Fill the
   confidence sheet: the claim, your confidence, the evidence for and against, and what would
   change your mind.
5. **Peer-check (10 min).** With a partner, share only what you choose. Ask: *does the
   confidence match the evidence? is "what would change my mind" specific and honest?* Give one
   reflection.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-drawn sheet with starters ("The claim is…; I am (low/medium/high)
  confident because…; something that would change my mind is…"); the learner fills one line
  each.
- **Extension:** Name one belief you *value* (not a factual claim) and one factual belief, and
  say how the two are held differently — values with conviction, facts open to evidence.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic. Confidence is rated with
  words (low/medium/high), not numbers; provide a pre-printed sheet so nothing must be counted,
  ordered, or computed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or
  peer-checking — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner state a belief, rate their confidence so it
  tracks the evidence, list evidence for and against, and name what would change their mind?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed confidence sheet, opened in the capstone
  technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what they are very confident about, and then — gently, with respect — ask
what would change their mind. Notice how the question lands. Write one sentence about it.
