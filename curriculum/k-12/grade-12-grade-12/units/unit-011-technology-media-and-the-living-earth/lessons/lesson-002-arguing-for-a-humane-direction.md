# Lesson 2 — Arguing for a Humane Direction

## Summary

Learners turn Lesson 1's system map into an argument. They learn the shape of a reasoned
argument — **claim, reason, evidence, who benefits and who is harmed, a counterargument, and a
response** — and use it to argue for a **humane** direction for a technology: one that serves
human flourishing and the living Earth, not only speed, profit, or convenience. They weigh
trade-offs honestly and learn that arguing well means naming the **values** a choice serves.

## Objectives

- Analyze technology as a system shaping society, weighing design, governance, and their own
  choices, and argue for a humane direction. _(D10.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

"Faster," "cheaper," and "more" are not the only ways a technology could go. A water system
could be built to serve the richest neighborhood or the whole city; a messaging app could be
designed to help you connect or to hold your attention as long as possible. The direction a
technology takes is not decided by the machine — it is decided by arguments, and arguments are
decided by people who show up with reasons. You can be one of them.

## Materials

- Humane-direction argument sheet
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a list of local stakeholders
- Enriched: access to one short case study

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the argument scaffold.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the six boxes of the system map. Today we argue about *direction*.
- Prepare a worked example of an argument (claim, reason, evidence, counterargument, response)
  to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an argument for a humane
direction is a claim, a reason, evidence, an honest account of who benefits and who is harmed,
a real counterargument, and a response — and "humane" means a direction that serves human
flourishing and the living Earth, not just efficiency.** Model the argument structure
explicitly, then let learners build their own (S-011). Keep evidence and values distinct: *who*
benefits and *what* a technology does are factual; *which* direction we ought to choose is a
value question to be argued, not assumed (S-236, S-459).

The **ethics** lens: arguing for a humane direction is the unit's central ethical move — the
step from describing a system to taking responsibility for steering it. The **egalitarianism**
lens: a humane direction is judged by how it treats the people who are usually left out — the
miner, the person without access, the downstream neighbor — not only the loudest stakeholder
(S-281, S-483). The **global** lens: what counts as "humane" is argued differently around the
world — a direction good for one place may burden another — so the argument must name whose
flourishing it serves (S-281). The **technology** lens: governance choices (a rule, a standard,
a repair right, a design decision) are the concrete levers a humane direction pulls; the
argument is about which levers to pull (S-481). The **environment** lens: a direction that
serves people but destroys the Earth that sustains them is not humane in the long run, so the
argument must weigh the living world alongside human convenience (S-395). Preview: Lesson 3
turns inward — what technology gives you, and what it costs you.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, your system map. Pick the technology you mapped, or
   choose a shared one — a water system, a messaging app, a road. Today you will argue for its
   *direction*.
2. **Learn the argument shape (10 min).** An argument has five parts: **claim** (what should
   happen), **reason** (why), **evidence** (what supports it), **who benefits and who is
   harmed** (the honest ledger), and a **counterargument + response** (the strongest objection,
   answered). Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — the five parts in order.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Follow a model argument about a technology: the claim ("this
   platform should repair old devices rather than push new ones"), the reason, the evidence,
   who gains and who loses, a counterargument ("repair slows innovation"), and a response. Note
   how the model names its **values**.
4. **Build your own argument (20 min).** Write your own argument for a humane direction. Make
   the claim specific and doable; give a real reason and evidence; name who benefits and who is
   harmed honestly; answer the strongest counterargument. End by naming the value your direction
   serves.
5. **Peer-check (8 min).** Trade arguments. Ask: *is the claim specific? is the evidence real?
   is the counterargument the strongest one, not a straw figure? does it name who is harmed?*
   Give one specific suggestion.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the argument sheet with the five parts as labeled boxes and sentence
  starters ("We should… because…; the evidence is…; this helps… but harms…; some will say…; my
  answer is…").
- **Extension:** Name the value your direction trades off (what is lost even in the better
  choice) and say why the trade-off is still worth making.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic. The argument is a word
  scaffold, doable in speech, writing, sign, or gesture; any figures used as evidence may be
  described in words rather than computed or compared numerically.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or
  peer-checking — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner build a claim-reason-evidence-counterargument-
  response argument for a humane direction that names who benefits and who is harmed?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed argument, opened in the capstone technology &
  Earth journal.

## Home connection

Explain your argument to someone at home in two minutes, and ask them to play the
counterargument. Note one thing they said that you had not considered.
