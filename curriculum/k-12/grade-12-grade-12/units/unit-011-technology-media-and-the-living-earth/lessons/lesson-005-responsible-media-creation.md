# Lesson 5 — Responsible Media Creation

## Summary

Learners begin to create media responsibly. They learn three tests for a message they make —
**accuracy**, **fairness**, and **honesty about sources** — and the **five core questions** of
media literacy (who created it, what techniques attract attention, what is omitted, who
benefits, and for what purpose). They plan and draft one real message about a real issue, then
check it against the tests, preparing to deepen the craft in Lesson 6.

## Objectives

- Practice responsible media creation by making and sharing a message that is accurate, fair,
  and honest about its sources. _(D10.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Every message you send — a post, a poster, a text, a photo caption — becomes part of what other
people know and feel. A message that is accurate, fair, and honest about its sources adds light;
a careless one adds fog. Being a creator carries the same responsibility as being a reader: you
are helping build the shared picture of the world, so the picture should be true.

## Materials

- Responsible-message checklist
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper and markers; a real local issue
- Enriched: a device for drafting and sharing

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the checklist (three tests + five questions).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D10.S2.11.01), propaganda and manipulation; from D10.S2.11.02, the
  responsibilities of creators when information spreads harm. Today we *practice* the creator's
  side.
- Prepare a short example message to test together (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **responsible media creation
means three tests — accurate, fair, honest about sources — guided by five questions: who
created it, what techniques attract attention, what is omitted, who benefits, and for what
purpose.** Model checking a message against the tests, then let learners draft their own
(S-011). Frame this as craft and ethics, not censorship: the goal is truthfulness, not
self-silencing.

The **ethics** lens: the creator's ethics is honesty — saying only what is true, showing the
other side fairly, and telling readers where the information came from, so they can check it
for themselves. The **egalitarianism** lens: fairness means not flattening anyone into a
caricature — a message that shows only one story of a person or a place risks the "single
story" that narrows how they are seen (S-417). The **global** lens: responsible creation is a
global skill — UNESCO's media and information literacy frames the right and duty to create and
share information critically and ethically across cultures (S-217). The **technology** lens:
the tools that let anyone publish also let a message spread far and fast, which raises the
stakes of the three tests rather than lowering them (S-481). The **environment** lens: media is
how people learn about the living Earth — an accurate, fair message about a river or a forest
can move people to care, while a false one can do real harm to the place and the people who
depend on it (S-004). Preview: Lesson 6 turns the three tests into careful practice — verifying
facts, representing others fairly, and attributing sources precisely.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11, one way information manipulation works. Today you are on
   the *making* side, and the task is to inform, not manipulate.
2. **Learn the three tests (10 min).** A responsible message is **accurate** (facts are true),
   **fair** (it represents people and views honestly, not as caricatures), and **honest about
   its sources** (it says where its information came from). Say — or write, sign, gesture, or
   use AAC to show — the three tests in your own words.
3. **Learn the five questions (10 min).** For any message, ask: **who created it? what
   techniques attract attention? what is omitted? who benefits? for what purpose?** These
   questions work for the messages you *read* and the ones you *make* (S-442).
4. **Draft a message (20 min).** Choose a real issue you care about — a local one if you can.
   Decide your **purpose** and **audience**, then draft a short message: a poster, a caption, a
   post, or a news summary. Keep it accurate, represent any other side fairly, and note where
   your facts came from.
5. **Self-check (10 min).** Run your draft through the three tests and five questions. Fix
   anything that is inaccurate, unfair, or missing its source.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a template with the three tests as checkboxes and starters ("This message
  says…; the facts came from…; the other side is…"); the learner fills one line each.
- **Extension:** Identify one thing your message *omits*, decide whether the omission is honest
  or misleading, and either include it or say why you left it out.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic; any figures used in a
  message may be written in words ("many," "a small number") or quoted exactly from a source,
  with no computation required.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or
  self-checking — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner draft a message that is accurate, fair, and
  honest about its sources, and can they name who created it, what is omitted, and who
  benefits?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The drafted message with its three-test self-check, opened in
  the capstone technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Show your draft to someone at home and ask: *is it accurate? is it fair? would you trust it?*
Note one change they suggest.
