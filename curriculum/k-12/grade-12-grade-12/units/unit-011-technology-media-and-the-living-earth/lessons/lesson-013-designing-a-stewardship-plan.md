# Lesson 13 — Designing a Stewardship Plan

## Summary

Learners design and defend a **stewardship plan** for a real place or resource. Working through
six steps — **name** the place or resource, **assess** its condition, **set the goal**, **choose
actions**, **check fairness** to present and future generations, and **plan how to monitor and
defend** it — they learn that stewardship is a designed practice, not a warm feeling, and that a
defensible plan weighs who gains, who gives something up, and what the future inherits.

## Objectives

- Design and defend a stewardship plan for a real place or resource, weighing fairness to
  present and future generations. _(D10.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

Caring about a place is easy; caring *for* it is a design problem. A well, a garden, a river, a
shared library of tools — each stays healthy only if someone thinks through what it needs, what
to do, who is affected, and what to check later. A stewardship plan is that thinking written
down: not a slogan, but a map of how a place or resource can be kept for the people who use it
now and the ones who come after.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a stewardship plan is six
steps — name the place or resource, assess its condition, set the goal, choose actions, check
fairness to present and future generations, and plan how to monitor and defend it — and a plan
is defensible only when it names who gains, who gives something up, and what the future
inherits.** Model one small plan explicitly, then let learners design their own (S-011). Grade
the *thinking*, not the scale: a small, real, fair plan for a real place is worth more than a
grand plan for nowhere.

The **ethics** lens: a plan is where care becomes obligation — it names a duty to the place and
to the people, present and future, who depend on it. The **egalitarianism** lens: every plan
creates winners and losers of a sort — who gets water, who pays for upkeep, whose use is
limited — so a fair plan says so honestly rather than pretending everyone gains equally
(S-459). The **global** lens: communities worldwide have governed shared resources by their own
common rules for centuries — the commons — and a good plan can learn from them without
assuming any one model fits everywhere (S-416). The **technology** lens: technology can serve a
plan (a pump, a sensor, a repair network) but is not the plan itself; the plan keeps the goal
first and the tools in service of it (S-452). The **environment** lens: the fairness check is
the Brundtland test — meeting today's needs without robbing the future — and real-world
stewardship, from tree-planting movements to river protectors, shows the plan in action (S-395,
S-059, S-328). Preview: Lesson 14 turns the plan inward and outward — your own responsibility as
a steward of the Earth.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12, the one change you chose. Today we build something bigger
   than one choice: a plan.
2. **Name and assess (10 min).** Choose a real place or resource you can actually touch — a
   stream, a garden, a well, a tool library, a stretch of coast. Write its name and **assess**
   its condition honestly: what is healthy, what is strained, whose care it depends on.
3. **Set the goal and choose actions (15 min).** Write one clear **goal** ("keep this water
   clean and shared") and three concrete **actions** toward it, each a verb with a who and a
   when. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — the action you are most confident
   you could actually do.
4. **Check fairness (12 min).** Ask the hard question: *who benefits, and who gives something
   up?* Include **future generations** — what will they inherit if this plan works, or fails?
   (S-395). Write the fairness check honestly, including any trade-off.
5. **Plan to monitor and defend (10 min).** Write how you will **monitor** (what you will check,
   and when) and how you will **defend** the plan to someone who questions it — one sentence of
   evidence and one sentence of values.
6. **Peer-defense (8 min).** With a partner, present your plan in two minutes. Your partner asks
   one hard fairness question; you answer it. Then switch.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the six-part template with starters ("The place is…; its condition is…; my
  goal is…; I will…; this benefits… but asks…; I will check…"); the learner fills one line per
  part.
- **Extension:** Name the hardest trade-off your plan makes — who gives something up — and argue
  why the plan is still fair, including to future generations.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic. The plan is a word
  organizer; any quantities (how much water, how often) may be stated in words or marked on a
  calendar, with nothing to compute, order, or compare numerically.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, presenting, defending, or discussing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name and assess a real place or resource, set a
  goal, choose actions, write an honest fairness check including future generations, and defend
  the plan against one hard question?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed six-part stewardship plan, opened in the capstone
  technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone who uses the place you chose — a neighbor, a family member, a caretaker — what they
think it needs. Add one thing you learned from them to your plan.
