# Lesson 11 — My Place in the Living World

## Summary

Learners place themselves within the living world, mapping the **web of life** they are already
part of — the food, water, air, soil, sun, and other species they depend on, and that depend on
them. They meet the idea of **reciprocity** from Indigenous and place-based traditions, and the
**more-than-human** world, and begin to see interdependence not as an abstract fact but as their
own address in the world.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their place within the living world and how understanding interdependence shapes
  how they choose to live. _(D10.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

You do not stand outside nature looking at it. The oxygen in your lungs came from a plant; the
water in your body was once cloud and river; the food on your plate grew in soil made by
countless small lives. Other beings — a pollinating bee, a filtering wetland, a deep taproot —
are quietly doing work you depend on every day. Your place in the living world is not a
location on a map; it is a web of relationships you are already woven into.

## Materials

- Web-of-life sheet
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; one plant, animal, or place you depend on
- Enriched: a short reading from an Indigenous or place-based account of reciprocity

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the web-of-life sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 9–10, Earth as one system and the planetary boundaries. Today we
  locate *yourself* inside that system.
- Frame this as belonging, not burden: the web is what keeps you alive, and it is worth knowing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **I am a living being among
living beings, bound to them by the same air, water, soil, and sun — my place is a web of
relationships, and I can name the beings I depend on and that depend on me.** Keep the tone of
belonging and wonder, not guilt. Treat Indigenous and place-based traditions with respect —
each is a distinct, living tradition, offered here as an example, never reduced to a single
concept or ranked against science.

The **ethics** lens: naming what you depend on is the seed of care — you tend what you know you
owe your life to. The **egalitarianism** lens: the web includes every being, not only the ones
useful to humans, and this is a leveling idea — a beetle and a person are both threads, though
different threads (S-437). The **global** lens: cultures around the world have long named this
kinship — many Indigenous and place-based traditions treat land, water, and other species as
relatives owed care, a way of seeing the unit holds alongside science, not beneath it (S-427,
S-320). The **technology** lens: some traditions and some tools both reveal the web — a
botanist's lens and an elder's plant-knowledge each name real relationships, and both matter
(S-427). The **environment** lens: the web is not metaphor but fact — species are declining
because the threads are being cut, and ecosystem services like pollination and clean water are
the web doing its work (S-437, S-176). Preview: Lesson 12 turns the web into choices — how
knowing you are interdependent changes how you live.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10, one planetary boundary. Today we step inside the system —
   and find *you* there.
2. **Draw your web (15 min).** Put "me" at the center. Draw lines outward to what keeps you
   alive: **food, water, air, soil, sun, and other species** — the plant that made your oxygen,
   the animal that pollinated your food, the wetland that filtered your water. Name at least one
   of them *specifically* (a real tree, a real river, a real bee). Say — or write, sign,
   gesture, or use AAC to show — one thread you had never noticed.
3. **Turn the web around (10 min).** Now ask the reverse: what depends on *you*? The soil you
   walk on, the animal whose home your choices touch, the people downstream. Add those arrows.
4. **Meet reciprocity (10 min).** Some traditions call this **reciprocity**: because the living
   world gives to you, you give back — you take only what you need, and you give in return, so
   all flourishing is mutual (S-427). Write one reciprocity sentence: "Because… gives me…, I
   will…".
5. **Name an ecosystem service (10 min).** Pick one thread of your web and name the **service**
   it provides — pollination, clean water, fertile soil, breathable air (S-437). Notice: the web
   is doing real work for you every day.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one sentence: "My
   place in the living world is…" and finish it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-drawn web with "me" and six labeled nodes; the learner draws and labels
  one line at a time and names one specific living thing.
- **Extension:** For one thread, explain what would happen to you and to others if that thread
  were cut, and name one thing you could do to keep it whole.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson is diagram-based and requires no arithmetic. The
  web is drawn and labeled with words; nothing must be counted, ordered, or computed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner draw a web of life from themselves to the food,
  water, air, soil, sun, and species they depend on, name one specific living thing, and write
  one reciprocity sentence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The labeled web of life with one reciprocity sentence, opened
  in the capstone technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Choose one living thing near your home — a tree, a bird, a garden plant — and spend two quiet
minutes noticing what it gives and what it needs. Write one sentence about what you noticed.
