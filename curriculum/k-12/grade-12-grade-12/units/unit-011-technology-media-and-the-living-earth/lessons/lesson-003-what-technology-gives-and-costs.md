# Lesson 3 — What Technology Gives and Costs

## Summary

Learners turn the systems lens on their own lives, building an honest two-column **ledger** of
what technology gives them (connection, learning, access) and what it costs them (attention,
time, privacy, rest, and — for the Earth — materials and waste). They learn the idea of
**opportunity cost** and practice looking at their own relationship with technology without
worship and without fear, as the first step toward choosing stewardship in Lesson 4.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own relationship with technology — what it gives and what it costs — and how
  they will use it as a steward rather than a consumer. _(D10.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

You already keep a running account of this relationship, even if you have never written it
down. The phone that lets you reach a faraway friend also eats an hour you meant to sleep. The
app that teaches you a new language also harvests your attention and your data. An honest look
at both columns is not about guilt and not about worship — it is about knowing what you are
actually trading, so the choice is yours rather than the machine's.

## Materials

- Gives-and-costs ledger
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; your own memory of one day's device use
- Enriched: a screen-time or attention-tracking tool

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column ledger.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, technology as a system and the argument for a direction. Today
  the system under the lens is *your own relationship*.
- Frame the goal as honesty, not shame: naming a cost is information, never a verdict.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **my relationship with
technology has real gifts and real costs, and I can name both honestly — attention, time,
privacy, rest, and the Earth's materials — so that my use is a choice, not a reflex.** Keep the
"neither worship nor fear" stance: the point is a clear-eyed ledger, not a guilt trip and not a
love letter. Respect privacy — learners record only what they choose to share.

The **ethics** lens: the cost side of the ledger is where unnoticed trade-offs live, and ethics
is partly the discipline of noticing what we are giving up before we consent to give it. The
**egalitarianism** lens: the ledger looks different by circumstance — a person who needs a
screen for school or for accessibility gives and receives differently from one who uses it only
for leisure — so no one's ledger is judged against another's (S-281). The **global** lens: the
"cost" column extends beyond the user to people far away — the miner of cobalt, the worker at
the recycling yard — whose lives carry costs the user's screen does not show (S-398, S-397).
The **technology** lens: some costs are *designed* — feeds ranked for engagement hold attention
on purpose, and personalization can quietly narrow what a person sees (S-481, S-482). The
**environment** lens: the cost column has an Earth-sized line — every device is made of mined
materials and becomes e-waste — so a full ledger includes what the planet pays (S-397).
Preview: Lesson 4 turns the honest ledger into a choice — steward, not consumer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 1–2, what you learned about technology as a system. Today
   the system you will map is *your own relationship* with technology.
2. **Draw the ledger (8 min).** Make two columns: **what technology gives me** and **what
   technology costs me**. Leave rows for connection, learning, access, attention, time, privacy,
   and rest.
3. **Fill the gifts (10 min).** In the left column, write what you genuinely receive — reaching
   people far away, learning a skill, finding information, a job, a voice. Be specific and
   honest; these are real.
4. **Fill the costs (12 min).** In the right column, write what you genuinely pay — attention
   pulled away, sleep lost, time not spent elsewhere, data given up, the pull to compare, the
   quiet narrowing of what you see (S-482). Add one cost the *Earth* pays: the materials and
   the e-waste behind the device (S-397).
5. **Name an opportunity cost (10 min).** Choose one habitual use and write what else that time
   could have been — reading, sleeping, talking, walking, making. An **opportunity cost** is the
   next-best thing you gave up.
6. **Pair-check (8 min).** With a partner, share only what you choose. Ask: *is the ledger
   honest on both sides — no worship, no fear?* Give one reflection, not a grade.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing you
   learned about your own relationship that you did not see before.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-drawn ledger with the seven rows labeled, and fill one phrase per
  cell; gifts and costs may be named aloud, written, or gestured.
- **Extension:** Write one *design question* about a cost you found — "why is this feature so
  hard to put down?" — and connect it to Lesson 2's argument about direction.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic; the ledger is a word
  organizer. Time and attention are described in words ("an hour," "a lot," "a little"), not
  measured or calculated.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name real gifts and real costs of their own
  technology use — including attention, time, privacy, and the Earth's materials — without
  worship and without fear?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed gives-and-costs ledger, opened in the capstone
  technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

For one evening, leave one device in another room for an hour and notice what you do with the
time. Write one sentence about what you noticed.
