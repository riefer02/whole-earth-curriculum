# Lesson 6 — Accurate, Fair, Honest About Sources

## Summary

Learners deepen Lesson 5's draft into a genuinely responsible message. They **verify** each
fact against a second, independent source; **represent** any opposing view fairly rather than
as a straw figure; and **attribute** every claim precisely (who said it, where, and when). They
revise and peer-check, learning that accuracy, fairness, and honest sourcing are habits of
craft, not one-time gestures.

## Objectives

- Practice responsible media creation by making and sharing a message that is accurate, fair,
  and honest about its sources. _(D10.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Two messages can say the same thing and be different in honesty. One says "studies show…" and
stops there; the other says *which* study, *who* ran it, and *where to find it*. One describes
an opponent as "obviously wrong"; the other states what the opponent actually argues, fairly,
before answering it. Accuracy, fairness, and honest sourcing are the difference between a
message that asks for trust and a message that earns it.

## Materials

- Verification-and-attribution sheet
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; two real sources
- Enriched: a device with an internet browser

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-column revision sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, your draft message and its self-check. Today we make it truly
  accurate, fair, and sourced.
- Prepare a worked example of one revision (verify, represent, attribute) to model first
  (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **accuracy means verifying a
fact against a second, independent source; fairness means representing another view as its
holder would, not as a straw figure; honesty about sources means attributing — who said it,
where, and when.** Model one revision explicitly, then let learners revise their own (S-011).
Frame verification as a habit, not a perfection — no one catches everything, which is exactly
why checking matters.

The **ethics** lens: honesty about sources is a respect for the reader's freedom — it lets them
follow your evidence and decide for themselves, rather than taking your word. The
**egalitarianism** lens: fairness is an equal-respect test — you describe the view you disagree
with as carefully as the one you hold, because the people who hold it deserve the same care you
want for yours (S-278). The **global** lens: the verification habit — tracing a claim to its
origin and checking independent reporting — is the same discipline fact-checkers use across
languages and countries (S-394). The **technology** lens: altered and synthetic media (such as
deepfakes) make verification harder, so the habits of tracing and checking become more
important, not less (S-393). The **environment** lens: environmental claims are among the most
shared and the most distorted online, so an accurate, well-sourced message about a real place
or resource is a genuine act of stewardship (S-004). Preview: Lesson 7 turns from making media
to staying informed in the flood.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5, your draft message and the three tests. Today we make
   each test *rigorous*.
2. **Verify the facts (15 min).** Take each factual claim in your draft and check it against a
   **second, independent source** (S-394). If two sources agree, keep it; if they disagree or
   you cannot find a second, soften or remove it. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to
   show — one fact you verified and how.
3. **Represent fairly (15 min).** Find any place your message describes an opposing view or
   person. Rewrite it so the *holder* of that view would agree with your description — no
   **straw figures**. State their strongest point, not the weakest, then answer it.
4. **Attribute the source (10 min).** For every claim, write **who** said it, **where** it
   appeared, and **when**. A precise attribution is the mark of honest sourcing.
5. **Peer-check (10 min).** Trade revised drafts. Ask: *is each fact verifiable against a
   second source? is the other side stated fairly? is every claim attributed?* Give one
   specific suggestion.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the three-column sheet with starters ("The fact is…; my second source is…;
  the other side says…; the source is (who, where, when)…"); the learner fills one line each.
- **Extension:** For one contested claim, find two sources that disagree, say what each gets
  right, and state what you can and cannot conclude from the disagreement.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic; any figures are quoted
  exactly from a source or described in words. Provide a pre-printed attribution table so the
  learner fills in who/where/when without computing or ordering numbers.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or
  peer-checking — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner verify a fact against a second source, represent
  an opposing view fairly, and attribute a claim precisely (who, where, when)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The revised message with verified facts and attributions,
  opened in the capstone technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Take one claim from your message and show a family member both your first and second source.
Ask them if the claim now feels earned. Write one sentence about what they said.
