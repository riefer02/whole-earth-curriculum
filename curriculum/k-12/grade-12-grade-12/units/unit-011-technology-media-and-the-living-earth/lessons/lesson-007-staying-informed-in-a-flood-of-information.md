# Lesson 7 — Staying Informed in a Flood of Information

## Summary

Learners reflect on how to stay informed without drowning or being manipulated. They learn how
algorithmic feeds can narrow what they see (the **filter bubble**), distinguish **misinformation**
(false by mistake) from **disinformation** (false on purpose), and design a personal **media
diet**: pause before sharing, check the source, seek more than one view, and choose a balanced
mix.

## Objectives

- Reflect on how they will stay informed in a flood of information, holding claims open to
  evidence rather than settling into certainty. _(D10.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

You are not a passive receiver of "the news" — you are a curator. Every day a flood of posts,
headlines, videos, and forwarded messages pours past you, and the mix you end up seeing is
shaped partly by the apps and partly by your own choices. Staying informed is not about
consuming everything; it is about choosing, checking, and pausing, so that what reaches your
mind is true and varied rather than just loud and familiar.

## Materials

- My-media-diet plan
- Capstone technology & Earth journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a list of local and wider sources
- Enriched: a device with an internet browser

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the media-diet plan.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, the three tests and the verification habit. Today we apply them
  to *staying* informed, not just making media.
- Frame the goal as balance, not paranoia: the aim is a media diet you can keep, not a world you
  fear.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **staying informed in a flood
means a personal media diet — pause before sharing, check the source, seek more than one view,
and choose a balanced mix — and it means knowing that feeds can narrow what you see (the filter
bubble) and that false information can be a mistake (misinformation) or deliberate
(disinformation).** Hold the "no cynicism, no nihilism" line: the response to a flood is not to
trust nothing, but to check and diversify. Keep partisan framing out — the skills are the same
in every direction.

The **ethics** lens: sharing before checking makes you an unwitting carrier of what is false, so
pausing is a small ethical act you can do every day. The **egalitarianism** lens: the flood
does not hit everyone equally — people with less time, less access, or fewer sources are more
exposed to what is loudest, so a good media diet is also a question of who gets to be informed
(S-281). The **global** lens: the same habits — trace the claim, check independent reporting —
are taught by news-literacy programs around the world, because the flood is global (S-217,
S-278). The **technology** lens: feeds ranked for engagement and personalized recommendations
can quietly surround you with what you already believe — the filter bubble, a real effect whose
strength is still debated (S-481, S-482). The **environment** lens: environmental information
is especially flooded with rumor, so the media diet directly affects whether people can act on
the real state of the living Earth (S-004). Preview: Lesson 8 goes deeper — holding claims open
to evidence rather than settling into certainty.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6, one verification habit you practiced. Today we apply
   those habits to the *stream* of information you live in.
2. **See the narrowing (10 min).** Learn how a **feed** works: it ranks what you see, often for
   engagement, and personalization can leave you inside a **filter bubble** — mostly hearing
   what you already believe (S-481, S-482). Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show —
   what "narrowed" means for what you know.
3. **Know the two falses (10 min).** **Misinformation** is false by mistake; **disinformation**
   is false on purpose. Give one example of each, or invent a careful one. Knowing the
   difference matters for how you respond: correct the first, do not spread the second.
4. **Design your media diet (20 min).** Write a plan you can actually keep: **pause** before
   sharing; **check the source** (who, where, when); **seek more than one view** (read across
   disagreement, not around it); and **choose a balanced mix** of sources — local and wider,
   official and independent, voices that differ from yours.
5. **Peer-check (10 min).** With a partner, ask: *is the plan doable? does it include more than
   one kind of source? does it work when you are tired or in a hurry?* Give one concrete
   improvement.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a four-box plan sheet (pause, check, seek, mix) with starters ("Before I
  share, I will…; to check, I will…; to hear another view, I will…; my mix includes…").
- **Extension:** Distinguish misinformation from disinformation in one real example you have
  seen, and explain why the difference changes how you would respond to it.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson requires no arithmetic. The media-diet plan is a
  word organizer, doable in speech, writing, sign, or gesture; no step requires counting,
  ordering, or calculating.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, reading aloud, discussing, or
  peer-checking — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner explain how feeds narrow what they see,
  distinguish misinformation from disinformation, and write a doable media diet (pause, check,
  seek more than one view, balanced mix)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed media-diet plan, opened in the capstone
  technology & Earth journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home where they get their information, and compare your mix with theirs. Write
one sentence about one source you each trust differently and why.
