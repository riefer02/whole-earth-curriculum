# Lesson 12 — Independent Judgment in Performance

## Summary

Learners turn performance critique into conviction: a **judgment** that is their own,
supported by **evidence** in the movement, and defended with a **warrant** ("because") —
while still honoring the practitioners and community the performance comes from and
distinguishing **appreciation** from **appropriation**. They write one full
claim-evidence-warrant chain and test it against a counter-judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique performance across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions
  while forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

You already defend judgments of movement — you say a dance is joyful *because* of the spring
in the steps and the lifted arms, a greeting is warm *because* of the open hands and the
pause, a work-motion is efficient *because* of the rhythm it keeps. "I liked it" is where
judging starts; "I think *because I see…*" is where it becomes something you can stand
behind and someone else can honestly agree or disagree with.

## Materials

- Judgment-with-evidence sheet (performance)
- Performance-capstone journal
- Low-tech: the Lesson 11 performance-ladder sheet; scratch paper
- Enriched: a shared document or discussion board

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim + evidence + warrant).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D07.S3.10.02), distinguishing respectful engagement with a dance
  or dramatic tradition from appropriation; from Lesson 11, the four-rung ladder. Today we
  build the final rung into a *defended* judgment.
- Prepare a model claim-evidence-warrant chain about the Lesson 11 performance to show first
  (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an independent judgment of
a performance is a claim about the work, backed by evidence in the movement, connected by a
warrant — and it is "independent" because the learner formed it, not because it ignores the
tradition.** Teach the claim-evidence-warrant chain explicitly, then let learners write and
test their own (S-011). Keep "independent" from sliding into "isolated": a defended judgment
is *more* respectful of living practitioners, because it rests on careful watching rather
than borrowed opinion.

The **ethics** lens: a judgment I cannot defend with evidence is only a preference wearing a
judge's robe; defending it honestly means naming what the movement does and does not show.
The **egalitarianism** lens: anyone can be a critic, because the warrant is public — it
invites another person to check the evidence and disagree with reasons, not with status. The
**global** lens: judgment across cultures is only fair when it starts from the performance's
own context (Lesson 11) and when appreciation — credit, permission, respect for living
practitioners — replaces appropriation (S-056). The **technology** lens: a recorded
performance can be judged without the practitioner's context or consent; a defended judgment
should say when it is judging a recording, not the lived occasion. The **environment** lens: a
judgment can weigh where and for what the performance happens — the harvest it marks, the sea
it answers — as part of its evidence. Preview: Lesson 13 turns to design and making.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11: what were the four rungs, and why must describe come
   before evaluate? Today we make the top rung — the judgment — one we can *defend*.
2. **Meet the chain (8 min).** Write it: **claim** (my judgment), **evidence** (what I see in
   the movement), **warrant** (the "because" that connects them). A claim without evidence is
   a preference; evidence without a claim is a list. The three together are a judgment you
   can stand behind.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Build a chain about **capoeira** (S-325). **Claim:** the
   movement feels both playful and serious at once. **Evidence:** the two bodies smile yet
   strike and evade in turn; the circle of singers and clappers holds a clear edge; the
   music drives the pace. **Warrant:** because play and threat alternate without warning
   within one held circle, the form carries both moods at once — so the movement, not my
   mood, creates the effect. The warrant points to the *movement*, not "I just feel it."
4. **Name the distinction (6 min).** A defended judgment is **independent** — it is yours —
   and **accountable** — it can be checked. And when the performance is from another culture
   or community, independence means you judged it on its own terms, with appreciation, not
   appropriation (S-056): credit the practitioners, do not take from them.
5. **Write your own chain (15 min).** Return to the second performance you critiqued in
   Lesson 11. Write one claim, two pieces of evidence from the movement, and a warrant. Then
   write the *strongest counter-judgment* a fair viewer might make, and answer it in one
   sentence.
6. **Share and check (7 min).** Exchange chains with a partner. Check: does the evidence point
   to the movement (not the performer's biography or your mood), and does the warrant connect
   claim to evidence? Give one specific improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the three parts
   of a judgment you can defend.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-written claim and evidence; the learner writes only the warrant
   ("because…") to connect them.
- **Extension:** Write the warrant as a testable statement — "if a viewer removed the music,
   the mood should shift" — and explain how the evidence could be checked.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; "two pieces of evidence" is a
   simple count the sheet already structures with two labeled boxes. A learner may offer one
   strong piece of evidence and still meet the goal of a defended judgment.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, discussing, sharing, or closing —
   can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
   d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. A
   learner who is blind or has low vision defends the judgment from a verbal or tactile
   description of the movement.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the chain contain a claim, evidence from the movement, and
  a warrant, and does the learner answer one counter-judgment in their own words?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One complete judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim, evidence,
  warrant, counter-answer), opened in the performance-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Form and defend one judgment about a movement you know — why one way of doing a task or a
dance works better than another — in a claim, one piece of evidence, and a "because." Read it
to someone and invite them to disagree with a reason.
