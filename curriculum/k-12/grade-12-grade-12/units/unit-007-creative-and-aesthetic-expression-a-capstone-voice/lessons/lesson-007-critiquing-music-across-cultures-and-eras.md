# Lesson 7 — Critiquing Music Across Cultures and Eras

## Summary

Learners meet musical critique as a respectful, evidence-based climb: **describe** what is
heard, **analyze** how the elements of music work, **interpret** what it may mean in its own
context, and **evaluate** with a reason. They climb the ladder on two pieces from different
cultures or eras, honoring each as a **living tradition** and separating observation from
judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique music across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions while
  forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

You already listen with judgment — a song makes you move or cry or remember; a voice is
"rough" or "sweet"; a beat is "right." Musical critique makes that hearing *fair*: it slows
the ear into steps so you can say something true about music from a place or time far from
your own, without flattening it into "good" or "bad" by your own habits.

## Materials

- Listening-ladder sheet
- Music-capstone journal
- Low-tech: two pieces from different places or times; scratch paper
- Enriched: a curated audio playlist spanning two traditions

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the listening-ladder sheet (describe / analyze / interpret / evaluate).
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D07.S2.08.02), critiquing music with evidence and separating
  observation from judgment; from Grade 10 (D07.S2.10.02), distinguishing respectful
  engagement from appropriation. Today we aim those habits at music from *any* culture or era.
- Choose two contrasting pieces to model (e.g., a raga and a maqam, or a spiritual and
  reggae) (S-366, S-367, S-385, S-450).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **musical critique is a
four-step climb — describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate — each step grounded in what is
actually heard, and each tradition met as a living one to honor, not a specimen to grade.**
Teach the ladder explicitly with a worked example, then let learners climb it on a second
piece (S-011). Name the danger plainly: ranking another culture's music by one's own scale or
tuning is not critique, it is flattening.

The **ethics** lens: to critique fairly is to describe before judging and to ask respectful
questions rather than pronounce. The **egalitarianism** lens: the ladder needs no training —
only careful listening and honest wording, open to anyone, instrument or none. The **global**
lens: the same four moves work on an Indian raga (S-366), a Middle Eastern maqam (S-367), an
African American spiritual (S-385), and Jamaican reggae (S-450) — each met as a living
tradition carried by real musicians now. The **technology** lens: recordings and streaming
put many traditions on one device, but a recording is not the occasion — name that you are
hearing a reproduction, and that the living context (place, people, event) matters. The
**environment** lens: much music is made from and about a place — work-songs of a harvest, a
song of the sea — so "where and when is this sung, and with what" is part of faithful
description. Preview: Lesson 8 sharpens the final rung into a defended judgment.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between an *observation* ("the
   pulse is steady") and a *judgment* ("the piece is moving")? Today we put that difference
   to work across cultures and eras.
2. **Meet the ladder (8 min).** Write the four rungs, this time for the ear: **describe**
   (what is literally heard — instruments, voices, tempo, loudness); **analyze** (how the
   elements of music — melody, rhythm, harmony, timbre, texture, form — work together, S-368);
   **interpret** (what it may mean in its own context — and what I do not know);
   **evaluate** (a judgment with a reason). Climb in order.
3. **Worked example — climb on one piece (15 min).** Take a **raga** performance: a singer or
   instrumentalist unfolds a particular scale and its characteristic phrases, building a
   mood (S-366). **Describe:** a drone, a voice or instrument, a scale, a slow build.
   **Analyze:** the melody stays within one scale and returns to its phrases; the rhythm
   marks the piece's structure. **Interpret:** each raga carries its own mood and even time
   of day — and I do not know the full meaning without asking a living musician. **Evaluate:**
   the return to the characteristic phrases gives the piece unity — a *reason*, not just "I
   liked it." Write each rung on your sheet.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** You did not rank; you *listened*. Critique honors the music by
   paying it careful attention and by knowing the limits of what you can claim about a
   tradition that is not your own.
5. **Climb on a second piece (15 min).** With a partner, choose a piece from a different
   place or era — a **maqam** (S-367), a **spiritual** (S-385), **reggae** (S-450), or one
   from your own home. Climb all four rungs in writing. At "interpret," write at least one
   *respectful question* you would ask a living keeper of that tradition.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four rungs
   and why you must not skip from describe to evaluate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the ladder pre-filled for one piece; the learner adds one observation to
  each rung rather than building the critique from scratch.
- **Extension:** For each rung, separate what is a factual property of the sound from what is
  the listener's inference, and mark the claim you would need to verify by asking a living
  musician.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four rungs are a sequence,
  not a computation. The ladder sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work in any order
  without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — listening discussion, reading, sharing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, non-speaking, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode;
  a d/Deaf learner critiques through felt vibration, visual notation, or a written
  description of the sound, and no step requires hearing. A learner who is blind or has low
  vision critiques entirely by ear and verbal description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner climb all four rungs in order, grounding each
  in what is heard, and write one respectful question at the "interpret" rung?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed listening-ladder sheet on a piece from a
  culture or era different from the learner's own, opened in the music-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Choose a song or tune at home that comes from somewhere else. Write the four rungs in one
sentence each, and one respectful question you would ask the person who knows it best.
