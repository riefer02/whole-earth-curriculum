# Lesson 2 — The Artist Statement

## Summary

Learners turn the Lesson 1 body of work into words. They learn that an **artist
statement** is not decoration but a short, honest account of **what** they made, **how**,
**why it matters**, and **what a viewer should notice**. They draft one for their three
studies using a four-prompt frame, then revise it with a partner so the words match the
work.

## Objectives

- Produce a sustained body of artwork that demonstrates technical skill and a coherent
  personal vision, and present it with a written artist statement. _(D07.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

When you show someone a thing you care about, you rarely hand it over in silence — you say
*"look at this, I made it because…"* A potter at a market explains the clay; a child
explains a drawing to a parent; a musician says what a song is about before playing it.
The artist statement is that sentence, written down and made honest, so the work can be
met by a stranger who was not there when you made it.

## Materials

- Capstone art journal (with the Lesson 1 studies)
- Artist-statement frame sheet
- Low-tech: the studies and any writing surface; a partner-listener or voice recorder
- Enriched: a document app or digital portfolio

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the artist-statement frame sheet (four labeled prompts).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D07.S1.11.01), explaining an artwork's intended effect on an
  audience; from Grade 10 (D07.S1.10.01), explaining how a body of work reflects ideas and
  influences. Today we write the statement that *presents* this unit's body of work.
- Prepare a model statement (or draft one from your own example) to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an artist statement is a
short, specific, honest account of what was made, how, why it matters, and what to notice —
written so a stranger can meet the work fairly.** Teach the four-prompt frame explicitly,
then let learners draft and revise (S-011). Guard against two traps: a statement that is
all grand abstraction ("I express the human condition") and one that is all technique with
no why. The good statement names both.

The **ethics** lens: the statement is where the artist takes responsibility — the claim
must match what the work actually shows, or the statement is dishonest. The
**egalitarianism** lens: any maker can write a true statement; it needs no degree and no
"art-world" vocabulary, only honesty about one's own process. The **global** lens: artists
worldwide accompany work with words — titles, labels, spoken introductions — and curators
and weavers alike explain intention to an audience; the statement is one more living
tradition of *explaining your making*. The **technology** lens: captions, alt text, and
audio description are modern forms of the same move — putting the work into words so more
people can meet it, including people who cannot see the image at all. The **environment**
lens: a statement can name where materials came from and where they go — clay dug nearby,
paper reused — making the work's footprint visible. Preview: Lesson 3 turns from making to
critique.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Look again at your three studies from Lesson 1. In one sentence, say —
   or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — what they all share. That sentence is the
   seed of your statement.
2. **Meet the four prompts (8 min).** Write them down: **(1) What I made** (the work, in
   plain words); **(2) How I made it** (materials and process); **(3) Why it matters to me**
   (the feeling or idea behind it); **(4) What I want a viewer to notice.** A statement
   answers these four, in about a paragraph.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Hear or read a model statement about a simple body of work —
   three studies of one place. Notice it names the work, the how, the why, and the
   notice — no vague claims, no jargon. Mark each prompt in the model as you find it.
4. **Draft your statement (12 min).** Using the frame sheet, write your own statement for
   your three studies. Answer all four prompts in your own words. Keep it honest: if the
   work does not show something, do not claim it does.
5. **Revise with a partner (12 min).** Read your draft to a partner (or they read it).
   Ask one question: *does the statement say something the work itself would not tell me,
   and does it leave out something obvious?* Revise one sentence to be more specific.
6. **Close (3 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four
   questions your statement always answers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame sheet with sentence starters ("I made… using… because… I want
  a viewer to notice…"); fill in the blanks rather than composing free.
- **Extension:** Revise for the line between what is visible and what is known only to the
  maker, and cut every word that claims more than the work shows.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four prompts are a list,
  not a computation. The frame sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work one box at a
  time in any order without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
  preferred mode. A learner who is blind or has low vision can compose the statement as a
  spoken or recorded description and have it transcribed, or write it in braille.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the draft answer all four prompts, and does the revised
  sentence make the words match the work more closely?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written artist statement, paired with the three studies
  in the capstone art journal.

## Home connection

Write a one-sentence "statement" for something at home — a meal, a tool, a kept object —
naming what it is, how it was made or kept, and why it matters. Read it to someone and ask
whether the words match the thing.
