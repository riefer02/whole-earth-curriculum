# Lesson 11 — Critiquing Performance Across Cultures and Eras

## Summary

Learners meet performance critique as a respectful, evidence-based climb: **describe** what
is seen or described, **analyze** how movement and expression work, **interpret** what it
may mean in its own context, and **evaluate** with a reason. They climb the ladder on two
performances from different cultures or eras, honoring each as a **living tradition** and
separating observation from judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique performance across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions
  while forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

You already read movement every day — the way someone greets you, the way a crowd moves at a
game, a dance at a celebration, the rhythm of a work crew. You notice, you feel, you decide
whether it "worked." Performance critique makes that noticing *fair*: it slows the glance
into steps so you can say something true about a dance or ritual from a place or time far
from your own, without flattening it into "good" or "bad" by your own habits.

## Materials

- Performance-ladder sheet
- Performance-capstone journal
- Low-tech: two performances from different places or times; scratch paper
- Enriched: a curated set of performance recordings spanning two traditions

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the performance-ladder sheet (describe / analyze / interpret / evaluate).
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D07.S3.08.02), critiquing performance with evidence and separating
  observation from judgment; from Grade 10 (D07.S3.10.02), distinguishing respectful
  engagement from appropriation. Today we aim those habits at performance from *any* culture
  or era.
- Choose two contrasting performances to model (e.g., capoeira and a ritual dance such as
  Mexico's Day of the Dead festivity) (S-325, S-448).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **performance critique is a
four-step climb — describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate — each step grounded in what is
actually seen (or faithfully described), and each tradition met as a living one to honor, not
a specimen to grade.** Teach the ladder explicitly with a worked example, then let learners
climb it on a second performance (S-011). Name the danger plainly: ranking another culture's
dance by one's own standards is not critique, it is flattening.

The **ethics** lens: to critique fairly is to describe before judging and to ask respectful
questions rather than pronounce. The **egalitarianism** lens: the ladder needs no training —
only careful watching and honest wording, open to anyone. The **global** lens: the same four
moves work on capoeira, an Afro-Brazilian art of movement and music (S-325), and on Mexico's
Day of the Dead festivity, a living ritual that links the living to deceased loved ones and
the maize harvest (S-448) — each met as a living tradition carried by real people now. The
**technology** lens: recordings let us watch performances from anywhere, but a recording is
not the occasion — name that you are seeing a reproduction, and that the living context
(place, people, event) matters. The **environment** lens: performance answers its space and
season — a harvest dance, a sea ritual — so "where, when, and for what occasion" is part of
faithful description. Preview: Lesson 12 sharpens the final rung into a defended judgment.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between an *observation* ("the
   dancers move in a circle") and a *judgment* ("the dance is joyful")? Today we put that
   difference to work across cultures and eras.
2. **Meet the ladder (8 min).** Write the four rungs, this time for the moving body:
   **describe** (what is literally seen — movement, gesture, spacing, stillness, posture);
   **analyze** (how movement and expression work together — speed, level, direction,
   repetition); **interpret** (what it may mean in its own context — and what I do not know);
   **evaluate** (a judgment with a reason). Climb in order.
3. **Worked example — climb on one performance (15 min).** Take **capoeira**: two people move
   in a circle (*roda*) around song and instruments, a game of attack and escape that is also
   dance (S-325). **Describe:** two bodies facing, low to the ground, stepping and turning;
   others sing and clap in a ring. **Analyze:** the movement swings between evasion and
   attack; the music sets the pace; the circle holds the space. **Interpret:** a game of
   bodies in dialogue, born among enslaved Africans and carrying strength and identity — and I
   do not know its full meaning without asking a living practitioner. **Evaluate:** the
   constant give-and-take between the two bodies gives the form its tension — a *reason*, not
   just "it is impressive." Write each rung on your sheet.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** You did not rank; you *watched*. Critique honors the
   performance by paying it careful attention and by knowing the limits of what you can claim
   about a tradition that is not your own.
5. **Climb on a second performance (15 min).** With a partner, choose a performance from a
   different place or era — the Day of the Dead festivity (S-448), a family dance, or a
   work-movement from your own home. Climb all four rungs in writing. At "interpret," write
   at least one *respectful question* you would ask a living keeper of that tradition.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four rungs
   and why you must not skip from describe to evaluate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the ladder pre-filled for one performance; the learner adds one observation
  to each rung rather than building the critique from scratch.
- **Extension:** For each rung, separate what is a factual property of the movement from what
  is the viewer's inference, and mark the claim you would need to verify by asking a living
  practitioner.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four rungs are a sequence,
  not a computation. The ladder sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work in any order
  without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — discussing, reading, sharing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. A
  learner who is blind or has low vision critiques through a verbal or tactile description of
  the movement (or a raised-line diagram of the spacing); no step requires sight.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner climb all four rungs in order, grounding each
  in what is seen or described, and write one respectful question at the "interpret" rung?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed performance-ladder sheet on a performance from
  a culture or era different from the learner's own, opened in the performance-capstone
  journal.

## Home connection

Watch a movement at home — a dance, a greeting, a way of working — and write the four rungs
in one sentence each, plus one respectful question you would ask the person who knows it
best.
