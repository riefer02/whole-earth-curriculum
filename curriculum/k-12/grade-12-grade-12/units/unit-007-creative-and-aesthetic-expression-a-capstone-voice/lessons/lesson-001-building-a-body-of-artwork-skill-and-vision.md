# Lesson 1 — Building a Body of Artwork: Skill and Vision

## Summary

Learners meet the unit's first idea: a **body of work** is not a stack of pictures but a
**series** of pieces held together by one **coherent vision** and visible, deliberate
**skill**. They study Jacob Lawrence's 60-panel *Migration Series* (S-363) as a worked
example of a single theme carried across many panels, then choose a theme of their own
and make three **sketch studies** that share a vision.

## Objectives

- Produce a sustained body of artwork that demonstrates technical skill and a coherent
  personal vision, and present it with a written artist statement. _(D07.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

A single song you like, a single dish your family makes, a single photograph of a place —
each is one moment. A *body* of work is what an artist keeps returning to: the potter
whose every pot carries the same hand, the weaver whose every cloth repeats a family
pattern, the photographer who photographs the same river in every season. It is the
difference between saying one thing once and saying it in many ways until it becomes a
voice. Wherever you live, you already have themes you return to — a place, a person, a
feeling, a question.

## Materials

- Capstone art journal
- Series-planning sheet
- Low-tech: any drawing surface and mark-making tool; found images or objects
- Enriched: Krita or Inkscape (free and open source)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the series-planning sheet (theme + three thumbnail boxes + "what stays the
  same" line).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D07.S1.11.01), art that addresses a social or environmental
  issue; from Grade 10 (D07.S1.10.01), developing a personal style in a body of work.
  Today we *begin* the sustained body that becomes this unit's capstone portfolio.
- Prepare the worked example (Jacob Lawrence's Migration Series) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a body of work is a
series of pieces united by one coherent vision and rehearsed, visible skill — not just
"more than one picture."** Teach the worked example explicitly, then let learners choose a
theme and make three studies (S-011). Hold "vision" as something anyone can claim, not a
privilege: a vision is simply *what you keep returning to and caring about*.

The **ethics** lens: a body of work is a promise — the artist is accountable for the theme
they take up, so choose a theme you can stand behind and represent honestly. The
**egalitarianism** lens: Lawrence made his series in tempera on hardboard with simple
materials in a small studio; a body of work needs no expensive tools, only sustained
attention (S-363). The **global** lens: series-making is everywhere — Hokusai's
*Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* returns to one mountain in many weathers (S-364), and
weavers and potters worldwide repeat a motif until it becomes a signature. Meet every
tradition named here as a *living* practice, never a specimen to copy carelessly. The
**technology** lens: free tools such as Krita and Inkscape mean a learner without paper or
paint can still build a series (S-440, S-441); the vision is yours, the tool is optional.
The **environment** lens: returning to one subject across time — a river, a field, a sky —
is also how we notice environmental change; a series can be a record. Preview: Lesson 2
turns the body into words with the artist statement.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: name one artwork you made (or imagined) that spoke to
   a social or environmental issue. Today we ask a bigger question: what does it take to
   make not *one* piece but a *body* of work that speaks with one voice?
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: a **body of work** is a set of pieces held
   together by a shared **theme** and a **coherent vision** — the same eyes, the same
   concerns, the same developing skill across every piece. A single picture is one sentence;
   a body of work is a paragraph that says one thing many ways.
3. **Worked example — Jacob Lawrence's Migration Series (12 min).** Lawrence made sixty
   small painted panels about one subject: the Great Migration, the movement of African
   Americans from the rural South to cities in the North in the early 1900s (S-363). Every
   panel shows the same theme, the same flat, bold shapes, the same colors — a single
   vision sustained across sixty works. Name what unifies them: **theme** (migration) and
   **skill** (a consistent visual language). Write this on your sheet as the model.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** A series is *not* "anything I happened to make." It is a
   decision: *I will return to this.* The artist chooses the thread, then keeps pulling it.
5. **Choose your theme and make three studies (18 min).** Pick one thing you keep returning
   to — a place, a person, a memory, a question, a worry for the Earth. In your journal,
   make **three sketch studies** of it, each a little different in subject but the same in
   feeling and approach. Then write one line: *what stays the same across all three.*
6. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, show your three studies and read your line.
   Ask: *could a viewer tell these three belong together, and how?* Give one specific
   improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the difference
   between "three pictures" and "a body of work."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the series-planning sheet pre-filled with a suggested theme ("a place I
  love"); the learner makes three studies of that one place rather than choosing a theme cold.
- **Extension:** Name the specific technique you are deliberately rehearsing across all three
  pieces (a line quality, a color family, a way of composing), and note where it improves.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. Any counting ("three
  studies," "sixty panels") is a simple count that can be done with the pre-marked
  three-box sheet; a learner may make any number of studies and still meet the goal of
  "several pieces, one vision," spoken or shown without numerals.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, sharing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
  preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound. For learners with low or no
  vision, the three studies can be made as tactile surfaces (raised lines, textures,
  pressed forms) and the "what stays the same" line spoken or signed as a description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state the difference between a single work
  and a body of work, choose a theme, and produce three studies a viewer would recognize as
  belonging together?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three sketch studies with the one-line "what stays the
  same" statement, opened in the capstone art journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a thing they keep returning to — a recipe, a song, a place, a
story. Write one sentence naming their "theme" and one sentence about what you and they
share in how you return to things.
