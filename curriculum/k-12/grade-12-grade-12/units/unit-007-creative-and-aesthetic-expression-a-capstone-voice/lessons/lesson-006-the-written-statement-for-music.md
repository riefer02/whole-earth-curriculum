# Lesson 6 — The Written Statement for Music

## Summary

Learners turn the Lesson 5 body of music into words — a short, honest written statement
naming **what** they made, **how**, **why it matters**, and **what a listener should
notice**. They draft one for their three pieces using a four-prompt frame and revise it so
the words match the sound.

## Objectives

- Produce and perform or present a sustained body of musical work that shows skill and a
  coherent personal voice, with a written statement. _(D07.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Before a song is played, someone usually says a little about it — a singer introduces a
song's story, a program tells you what a piece is and how it was made, a musician names the
tradition it comes from. That is the written statement for music: the words that let a
listener meet the sound fairly, especially when the maker is not there to speak.

## Materials

- Music-capstone journal (with the Lesson 5 pieces)
- Music-statement frame sheet
- Low-tech: the pieces and any writing surface; a partner-listener or voice recorder
- Enriched: a document app or digital portfolio with audio embeds

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the music-statement frame sheet (four prompts).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D07.S2.11.01), explaining a piece's intended effect; from
  Grade 10 (D07.S2.10.01), explaining the techniques used in an original piece. Today we
  write the statement that *presents* this unit's musical body.
- Prepare a model statement to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a music statement is a
short, specific, honest account of what was made, how, why it matters, and what to listen
for — written so a stranger can meet the sound fairly.** Teach the four-prompt frame, then
let learners draft and revise (S-011). Guard against two traps: all mood and no technique
("it expresses joy"), and all technique and no why ("it uses a pentatonic scale"). The good
statement names both.

The **ethics** lens: the statement is where the composer takes responsibility — the claim
must match what is actually audible, or the statement is dishonest. The **egalitarianism**
lens: any maker can write a true statement; it needs no degree or music-theory vocabulary,
only honesty about one's own process. The **global** lens: program notes, song
introductions, and spoken prefaces accompany music worldwide — from the raga's own
characteristics (S-366) to a singer naming a song's people and occasion — all living
traditions of *explaining your sound*. The **technology** lens: written statements now
travel with recordings as liner notes, captions, and text descriptions, so a deaf listener
can meet the music through words and a blind listener through screen-reader text. The
**environment** lens: a statement can name where sounds came from — found, recorded,
borrowed from a place — making the music's relationship to its environment visible.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Play or recall your three pieces from Lesson 5. In one sentence, say —
   or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — what they share. That is the seed of your
   statement.
2. **Meet the four prompts (8 min).** Write them down: **(1) What I made** (the pieces, in
   plain words); **(2) How I made it** (voice, instrument, found sound; the motif and its
   variations); **(3) Why it matters to me** (the feeling or idea); **(4) What I want a
   listener to notice.** A statement answers these four in about a paragraph.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Hear or read a model statement about three pieces built from
   one motif. Notice it names the work, the how, the why, and the listen-for — no vague
   claims, no jargon. Mark each prompt as you find it.
4. **Draft your statement (12 min).** Using the frame sheet, write your own statement for
   your three pieces. Answer all four prompts in your own words. Keep it honest: if the music
   does not do something, do not claim it does.
5. **Revise with a partner (12 min).** Read your draft to a partner (or they read it). Ask:
   *does the statement say something the music itself would not tell a listener, and does it
   leave out something obvious?* Revise one sentence to be more specific.
6. **Close (3 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four
   questions your statement always answers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame sheet with sentence starters ("I made… using… because… I want a
   listener to notice…"); fill in the blanks rather than composing free.
- **Extension:** Revise for the line between what is audible and what only the composer
   knows, and cut every word that claims more than the sound shows.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four prompts are a list,
   not a computation. The frame sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work one box at a
   time in any order without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or
   closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
   non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
   preferred mode. A learner who is blind or has low vision composes the statement as a
   spoken or recorded description (or in braille); a d/Deaf learner pairs the statement with
   a written or signed description of the sound's felt vibration and structure.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the draft answer all four prompts, and does the revised
  sentence make the words match the sound more closely?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written music statement, paired with the three pieces in
  the music-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Write a one-sentence "statement" for a piece of music you know from home — naming what it
is, how or where it is made or sung, and why it matters. Read it to someone and ask whether
the words match the music.
