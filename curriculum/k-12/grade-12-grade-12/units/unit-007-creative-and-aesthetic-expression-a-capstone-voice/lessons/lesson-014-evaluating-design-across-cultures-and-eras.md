# Lesson 14 — Evaluating Design Across Cultures and Eras

## Summary

Learners close the unit by evaluating design: a designed thing is judged against four
criteria — **function**, **fairness**, **footprint**, and **craft** — always honoring the
tradition it comes from, and always ending in a judgment one can defend. They evaluate two
objects from different cultures or eras and write a full claim-evidence-warrant judgment
about good design.

## Objectives

- Evaluate design across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions while
  forming and defending an independent judgment about good design. _(D07.S4.12.02)_

## Connection

You already judge design constantly — a chair that tips, a cup that burns, a bag that
tears, a door that is too heavy. Good design is not "looks nice"; it is *works well, works
for everyone it can, and does not cost the Earth more than it must.* Every place and every
era has judged its tools, vessels, and homes by these tests, even when the words differed.

## Materials

- Design-evaluation sheet
- Design-capstone journal
- Low-tech: two designed objects or places from different places or times; scratch paper
- Enriched: a curated image or object set spanning two design traditions

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the design-evaluation sheet (function / fairness / footprint / craft +
  judgment line).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D07.S4.11.02), evaluating a designed object's full life cycle and
  proposing improvements; from Lesson 13, the design cycle and universal-design checks. Today
  we *evaluate* design from any culture or era — the unit's final move.
- Choose two contrasting objects to model (e.g., a handmade vessel from one tradition and a
  modern mass-produced equivalent) (S-369, S-452).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a designed thing is
evaluated against four criteria — function (does it work), fairness (who can use it, who is
left out), footprint (what it costs the Earth, cradle to cradle), and craft (is it made well
and honestly) — and every evaluation ends in a judgment one can defend.** Teach the four
criteria explicitly with a worked example, then let learners evaluate a second object (S-011).
Name the danger plainly: judging another culture's design only by one's own machine-made
standards is flattening, not evaluation.

The **ethics** lens: to evaluate fairly is to name what a design does and does not do — for
people and for the planet — without pretending any design is neutral. The **egalitarianism**
lens: the four criteria are open to anyone who uses and handles things; they need no
laboratory, only honest attention and a willingness to ask who is left out (S-369). The
**global** lens: every culture's tools, vessels, and homes are living design traditions that
fit their materials, needs, and climate — to honor with respectful questions, never to grade
by a single standard. The **technology** lens: mass production and digital fabrication change
what is possible, but they also change the footprint — a judgment must weigh the whole life
cycle, from materials through making, use, and disposal (S-452). The **environment** lens:
this is the criterion that decides whether "good design" is also *sustainable* design — the
unit closes where making meets stewardship. Preview: the capstone portfolio gathers every
artifact from this unit into one voice.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: what is a "life cycle" — from materials through making,
   use, and disposal? Today we judge designed things against that full arc.
2. **Meet the four criteria (8 min).** Write them down: **function** (does it do its job);
   **fairness** (who can use it, who is left out, and is it made and sold fairly);
   **footprint** (what it costs the Earth across its whole life); **craft** (is it made well
   and honestly, and does it honor its tradition). A judgment about good design weighs all
   four, not just the look.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Evaluate a handmade woven vessel from a living tradition — say,
   a tightly coiled basket of local plant fiber. **Function:** it holds and carries and
   drains. **Fairness:** it can be made by many hands with local materials, and its making
   supports a community rather than a distant factory. **Footprint:** the fiber is local and
   renewable, and the basket returns to the earth at the end of its life (S-452).
   **Craft:** the coiling is even and strong, a skill passed from hand to hand — a living
   tradition, honored with questions, not imitated carelessly (S-056). **Judgment:** this is
   good design *because* its function, fairness, footprint, and craft align — a reason, not
   just "I like it." Write each criterion on your sheet.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** You did not rank by looks; you *weighed*. Evaluation honors the
   design by attending to all four criteria and by knowing that "good" is a judgment you must
   defend.
5. **Evaluate a second object (17 min).** With a partner, choose a designed thing from a
   different place or era — a tool, a chair, a shoe, a home, a modern mass-produced version of
   the vessel. Score it against all four criteria. Then write a full judgment: **claim**,
   one piece of **evidence** per criterion you weigh, and a **warrant** ("because"). At
   "fairness," write at least one *respectful question* you would ask a living maker of that
   tradition.
6. **Share and check (4 min).** Exchange judgments with a partner. Check: does the judgment
   weigh all four criteria, or only looks, and does the warrant connect claim to evidence?
   Give one specific improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four
   criteria and the difference between "looks nice" and "good design."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the evaluation sheet pre-filled for one object; the learner adds one
  observation to each criterion rather than building the evaluation from scratch.
- **Extension:** Weigh the full life cycle of a modern mass-produced version against the
  handmade one, and propose one change that would shrink its footprint or widen its fairness.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; "four criteria" is a simple
  count the sheet already structures with four labeled boxes, and any comparison is made in
  words rather than numbers. A learner may weigh two criteria and still meet the goal of a
  reasoned judgment.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — discussing, presenting, sharing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. A
  learner who is blind or has low vision evaluates by touch, use, and verbal description,
  judging function, fairness, footprint, and craft through handling and hearing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner weigh a design against all four criteria,
  write one respectful question, and end with a claim-evidence-warrant judgment about good
  design?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed design-evaluation sheet (four criteria + a
  defended judgment) on a design from a culture or era different from the learner's own,
  opened in the design-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Evaluate one designed thing at home against the four criteria — function, fairness,
footprint, craft — in one sentence each, and write one improvement you would make and why.
