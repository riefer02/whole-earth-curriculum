# Lesson 4 — Forming and Defending an Independent Judgment

## Summary

Learners turn critique into conviction: a **judgment** that is their own, supported by
**evidence** from the work, and defended with a **warrant** ("because") — while still
honoring the tradition the work belongs to and distinguishing **appreciation** from
**appropriation**. They write one full claim-evidence-warrant chain and test it against a
counter-judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique artwork across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions
  while forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

You already form and defend judgments all the time — you say a meal is good *because* of
the texture and the salt, a road is dangerous *because* of the blind corner, a design works
*because* it is easy to hold. "I think" is where judging starts, but "I think *because I
see…*" is where it becomes something you can stand behind and someone else can honestly
agree or disagree with.

## Materials

- Judgment-with-evidence sheet
- Capstone art journal
- Low-tech: the Lesson 3 critique sheet; scratch paper
- Enriched: a shared document or discussion board

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim + evidence + warrant).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D07.S1.10.02), the difference between appreciation (honoring a
  tradition respectfully) and appropriation (taking from it carelessly); from Lesson 3, the
  four-rung ladder. Today we build the final rung into a *defended* judgment.
- Prepare a model claim-evidence-warrant chain about the Lesson 3 work to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an independent judgment
is a claim about a work, backed by evidence in the work, connected by a warrant — and it is
"independent" because the learner formed it, not because it ignores the tradition.** Teach
the claim-evidence-warrant chain explicitly, then let learners write and test their own
(S-011). Keep "independent" from sliding into "isolated": a defended judgment is *more*
respectful of a living tradition, because it rests on careful evidence rather than borrowed
opinion.

The **ethics** lens: a judgment I cannot defend with evidence is only a preference wearing a
judge's robe; defending it honestly means naming what the evidence does and does not show.
The **egalitarianism** lens: anyone can be a critic, because the warrant is public — it
invites another person to check the evidence and disagree with reasons, not with status.
The **global** lens: judgment across cultures is only fair when it starts from the work's
own context (Lesson 3) and when appreciation — credit, permission, respect for living
keepers — replaces appropriation (S-056, S-084). The **technology** lens: posted images and
reproductions can be judged without the maker's context or consent; a defended judgment
should say when it is judging a reproduction, not the lived work. The **environment** lens:
a judgment can weigh a work's materials and footprint — where the pigment, the timber, the
fabric came from — as part of its evidence. Preview: Lesson 5 carries the same "body of
work" idea into music.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what were the four rungs, and why must describe come
   before evaluate? Today we make the top rung — the judgment — one we can *defend*.
2. **Meet the chain (8 min).** Write it: **claim** (my judgment), **evidence** (what I see
   in the work), **warrant** (the "because" that connects them). A claim without evidence is
   a preference; evidence without a claim is a list. The three together are a judgment you
   can stand behind.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Build a chain about the Lesson 3 work (Hokusai's *Red Fuji*):
   **Claim:** the print feels vast and calm at once. **Evidence:** the mountain fills the
   frame; the sky is mostly open blue; the slope is a single strong diagonal (S-364).
   **Warrant:** because the huge, simple shape leaves little busy detail, the eye rests on one
   grand form — so the composition, not my mood, creates the feeling. Notice the warrant
   points to the *work*, not to "I just feel it."
4. **Name the distinction (6 min).** A defended judgment is **independent** — it is yours —
   and it is also **accountable** — it can be checked. And when the work is from another
   culture, independence means you judged it on its own terms, with appreciation, not
   appropriation (S-056, S-084): credit the tradition, do not take it.
5. **Write your own chain (15 min).** Return to the second work you critiqued in Lesson 3.
   Write one claim, two pieces of evidence from the work, and a warrant. Then write the
   *strongest counter-judgment* a fair reader might make, and answer it in one sentence.
6. **Share and check (7 min).** Exchange chains with a partner. Check: does the evidence
   point to the work (not the maker's biography or your mood), and does the warrant connect
   claim to evidence? Give one specific improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the three parts
   of a judgment you can defend.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-written claim and evidence; the learner writes only the warrant
   ("because…") to connect them.
- **Extension:** Write the warrant as a testable statement — "if a viewer covers the sky, the
   feeling of vastness should drop" — and explain how the evidence could be checked.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; "two pieces of evidence" is a
   simple count the sheet already structures with two labeled boxes. A learner may offer one
   strong piece of evidence and still meet the goal of a defended judgment.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, discussing, sharing, or closing —
   can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
   d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. A
   learner who is blind or has low vision defends the judgment from verbal or tactile
   description, citing what they felt or heard described as evidence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the chain contain a claim, evidence from the work, and a
   warrant, and does the learner answer one counter-judgment in their own words?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One complete judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim, evidence,
   warrant, counter-answer), opened in the capstone art journal.

## Home connection

Form and defend one judgment about something at home — why one tool or recipe works better
than another — in a claim, one piece of evidence, and a "because." Read it to someone and
invite them to disagree with a reason.
