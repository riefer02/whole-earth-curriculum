# Lesson 8 — Independent Judgment in Music

## Summary

Learners turn musical critique into conviction: a **judgment** that is their own, supported
by **evidence** in the sound, and defended with a **warrant** ("because") — while still
honoring the musicians and community the music comes from and distinguishing
**appreciation** from **appropriation**. They write one full claim-evidence-warrant chain
and test it against a counter-judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique music across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions while
  forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

You already defend musical judgments — you say a song is powerful *because* of the bass
line and the way the voice breaks, a rhythm works *because* it leaves space, a piece feels
old *because* of the scale it uses. "I like it" is where judging starts; "I think *because I
hear…*" is where it becomes something you can stand behind and someone else can honestly
agree or disagree with.

## Materials

- Judgment-with-evidence sheet (music)
- Music-capstone journal
- Low-tech: the Lesson 7 listening-ladder sheet; scratch paper
- Enriched: a shared document or discussion board

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim + evidence + warrant).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D07.S2.10.02), distinguishing respectful engagement with a
  tradition from appropriation; from Lesson 7, the four-rung listening ladder. Today we build
  the final rung into a *defended* judgment.
- Prepare a model claim-evidence-warrant chain about the Lesson 7 piece to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an independent musical
judgment is a claim about a piece, backed by evidence in the sound, connected by a warrant —
and it is "independent" because the learner formed it, not because it ignores the tradition.**
Teach the claim-evidence-warrant chain explicitly, then let learners write and test their own
(S-011). Keep "independent" from sliding into "isolated": a defended judgment is *more*
respectful of living musicians, because it rests on careful listening rather than borrowed
opinion.

The **ethics** lens: a judgment I cannot defend with evidence is only a preference wearing a
judge's robe; defending it honestly means naming what the sound does and does not show. The
**egalitarianism** lens: anyone can be a music critic, because the warrant is public — it
invites another person to check the evidence and disagree with reasons, not with status. The
**global** lens: judgment across cultures is only fair when it starts from the music's own
context (Lesson 7) and when appreciation — credit, permission, respect for living musicians —
replaces appropriation (S-056, S-450). The **technology** lens: a recorded or streamed piece
can be judged without the musician's context or consent; a defended judgment should say when
it is judging a recording, not the lived performance. The **environment** lens: a judgment
can weigh where the sound and its instruments come from — the wood, the hide, the metal, the
field — as part of its evidence. Preview: Lesson 9 carries the "body of work" idea into
performance.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: what were the four rungs, and why must describe come
   before evaluate? Today we make the top rung — the judgment — one we can *defend*.
2. **Meet the chain (8 min).** Write it: **claim** (my judgment), **evidence** (what I hear
   in the piece), **warrant** (the "because" that connects them). A claim without evidence is
   a preference; evidence without a claim is a list. The three together are a judgment you
   can stand behind.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Build a chain about a **spiritual** — an African American
   religious song born of the enslaved experience, carrying sorrow, faith, and hope (S-385).
   **Claim:** the song sounds sorrowful and hopeful at once. **Evidence:** the melody is slow
   and low; the words speak of trouble and of deliverance; the voice carries both. **Warrant:**
   because the melody's weight and the words' turn toward deliverance sit side by side, the
   music holds two feelings at once — so the sound, not my mood, creates the effect. The
   warrant points to the *sound*, not "I just feel it."
4. **Name the distinction (6 min).** A defended judgment is **independent** — it is yours —
   and **accountable** — it can be checked. And when the music is from another culture or
   community, independence means you judged it on its own terms, with appreciation, not
   appropriation (S-056, S-450): credit the musicians, do not take from them.
5. **Write your own chain (15 min).** Return to the second piece you critiqued in Lesson 7.
   Write one claim, two pieces of evidence from the sound, and a warrant. Then write the
   *strongest counter-judgment* a fair listener might make, and answer it in one sentence.
6. **Share and check (7 min).** Exchange chains with a partner. Check: does the evidence point
   to the sound (not the musician's biography or your mood), and does the warrant connect
   claim to evidence? Give one specific improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the three parts
   of a judgment you can defend.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-written claim and evidence; the learner writes only the warrant
   ("because…") to connect them.
- **Extension:** Write the warrant as a testable statement — "if a listener removed the drone,
   the mood should shift" — and explain how the evidence could be checked.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; "two pieces of evidence" is a
   simple count the sheet already structures with two labeled boxes. A learner may offer one
   strong piece of evidence and still meet the goal of a defended judgment.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, discussing, sharing, or closing —
   can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
   d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. A
   d/Deaf learner defends the judgment from felt vibration, visual notation, or a written
   description of the sound; a learner who is blind or has low vision defends it by ear.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the chain contain a claim, evidence from the sound, and a
  warrant, and does the learner answer one counter-judgment in their own words?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One complete judgment-with-evidence sheet (claim, evidence,
  warrant, counter-answer), opened in the music-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Form and defend one judgment about a piece of music you know — why one version or
performance works better than another — in a claim, one piece of evidence, and a "because."
Read it to someone and invite them to disagree with a reason.
