# Lesson 10 — The Performance Artist Statement

## Summary

Learners turn the Lesson 9 performance into words — a short, honest written statement
naming **what** they made, **how**, **why it matters**, and **what an audience should
notice**. They draft one for their devised piece using a four-prompt frame and revise it so
the words match the movement.

## Objectives

- Produce and perform a sustained piece that shows technical skill and a coherent creative
  vision, with a written artist statement. _(D07.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

Before a dance or a play begins, something tells you what you are about to see — a program
note, a spoken introduction, a title. That is the performance statement: the words that let
an audience meet the moving body fairly, especially when the performer cannot stand beside
them and explain.

## Materials

- Performance-capstone journal (with the Lesson 9 piece)
- Performance-statement frame sheet
- Low-tech: the devised piece and any writing surface; a partner-listener or voice recorder
- Enriched: a document app or digital portfolio with a video or photo embed

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the performance-statement frame sheet (four prompts).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D07.S3.11.01), explaining a performance's intended effect; from
  Grade 10 (D07.S3.10.01), explaining the choices in an original piece. Today we write the
  statement that *presents* this unit's performance.
- Prepare a model statement to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a performance statement is
a short, specific, honest account of what was made, how, why it matters, and what to watch
for — written so a stranger can meet the moving body fairly.** Teach the four-prompt frame,
then let learners draft and revise (S-011). Guard against two traps: all mood and no craft
("it is about joy"), and all craft and no why ("it uses three levels"). The good statement
names both.

The **ethics** lens: the statement is where the performer takes responsibility — the claim
must match what the body actually does, or the statement is dishonest. The
**egalitarianism** lens: any performer can write a true statement; it needs no degree or
dance-theory vocabulary, only honesty about one's own choices. The **global** lens: spoken
introductions, program notes, and titles accompany performance worldwide — from a dancer
naming the tradition a step belongs to, to a ritual's own occasion (S-056) — all living
traditions of *explaining your movement*. The **technology** lens: statements now travel
with recordings as captions and text descriptions, so an audience that cannot see the
performance can meet it through words, and one that cannot hear can meet the text through a
screen reader. The **environment** lens: a statement can name where the piece lives — the
space, the floor, the weather, the place it answers — making the body's relationship to its
environment visible. Preview: Lesson 11 turns from performing to critiquing performance.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Run or recall your Lesson 9 piece. In one sentence, say — or write,
   sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — the one idea it expresses. That is the seed of your
   statement.
2. **Meet the four prompts (8 min).** Write them down: **(1) What I made** (the piece, in
   plain words); **(2) How I made it** (movement, gesture, space, stillness; the
   beginning-middle-end); **(3) Why it matters to me** (the feeling or idea); **(4) What I
   want an audience to notice.** A statement answers these four in about a paragraph.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Hear or read a model statement about a short devised piece.
   Notice it names the work, the how, the why, and the watch-for — no vague claims, no
   jargon. Mark each prompt as you find it.
4. **Draft your statement (12 min).** Using the frame sheet, write your own statement for
   your piece. Answer all four prompts in your own words. Keep it honest: if the body does
   not do something, do not claim it does.
5. **Revise with a partner (12 min).** Read your draft to a partner (or they read it). Ask:
   *does the statement say something the movement itself would not tell an audience, and does
   it leave out something obvious?* Revise one sentence to be more specific.
6. **Close (3 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four
   questions your statement always answers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame sheet with sentence starters ("I made… using… because… I want an
   audience to notice…"); fill in the blanks rather than composing free.
- **Extension:** Revise for the line between what an audience can see and what only the
   performer knows, and cut every word that claims more than the body shows.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four prompts are a list,
   not a computation. The frame sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work one box at a time
   in any order without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or
   closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
   non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
   preferred mode. A learner who is blind or has low vision composes the statement as a
   spoken or recorded description (or in braille); a learner with limited mobility describes
   the choices their body makes, which are performance.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the draft answer all four prompts, and does the revised
  sentence make the words match the movement more closely?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written performance statement, paired with the piece in
  the performance-capstone journal.

## Home connection

Write a one-sentence "statement" for a movement or ritual you know from home — a greeting, a
dance, a way of working — naming what it is, how it is done, and why it matters. Read it to
someone and ask whether the words match the movement.
