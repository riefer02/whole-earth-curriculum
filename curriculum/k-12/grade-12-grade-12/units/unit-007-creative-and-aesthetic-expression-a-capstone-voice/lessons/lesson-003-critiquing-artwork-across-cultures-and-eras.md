# Lesson 3 — Critiquing Artwork Across Cultures and Eras

## Summary

Learners meet **critique** as a respectful, evidence-based climb: **describe** what is
there, **analyze** how the elements work, **interpret** what it might mean in its own
context, and **evaluate** with a reason. They climb the ladder on two artworks from
different cultures or eras, learning to honor each as a **living tradition** and to
separate observation from judgment.

## Objectives

- Critique artwork across cultures and eras with insight, honoring diverse traditions
  while forming and defending an independent judgment. _(D07.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

You already read images every day — a painted sign, a screen, a cloth pattern, a family
photograph. You notice something, you feel something, you decide whether it works. Critique
makes that noticing *fair*: it slows the glance into four steps so you can say something
true about work made by someone far away in place or time, without flattening it into
"good" or "bad."

## Materials

- Critique-ladder sheet
- Capstone art journal
- Low-tech: any two artworks from different places or times; scratch paper
- Enriched: a museum database or digital image collection

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the critique-ladder sheet (describe / analyze / interpret / evaluate).
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 (D07.S1.08.02), critiquing with evidence and separating
  observation from judgment; from Grade 10 (D07.S1.10.02), distinguishing appreciation from
  appropriation. Today we aim those habits at work from *any* culture or era.
- Choose two artworks from different traditions to model (e.g., Hokusai's woodblock print
  and a Māori kōwhaiwhai pattern or an Akan adinkra cloth) (S-364, S-115, S-084).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **critique is a four-step
climb — describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate — each step grounded in what is actually in
the work, and each tradition met as a living one to honor, not a specimen to grade.** Teach
the ladder explicitly with a worked example, then let learners climb it on a second work
(S-011). Name the danger plainly: a critique that ranks another culture's art by one's own
standards is not critique, it is flattening.

The **ethics** lens: to critique fairly is to describe before judging and to ask respectful
questions rather than pronounce. The **egalitarianism** lens: the ladder needs no special
training — only careful looking and honest wording, open to anyone. The **global** lens: the
same four moves work on a Japanese woodblock print (S-364), a Māori painted rafter pattern
(S-115), and an Akan adinkra cloth stamped with proverbs (S-084) — each met as a living
tradition carried by real people now. The **technology** lens: digital collections and
image search put works from many cultures on one screen, but the screen is not the work —
name that you are seeing a reproduction, and that the living context (place, people,
occasion) matters. The **environment** lens: many traditions make art from local materials
and respond to a local landscape, so "what is it made of and where is it from" is part of
faithful description. Preview: Lesson 4 sharpens the final rung into a defended judgment.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between an *observation*
   ("the figure is in the center") and a *judgment* ("the composition is strong")? Today we
   put that difference to work across cultures and eras.
2. **Meet the ladder (8 min).** Write the four rungs: **describe** (what is literally there —
   subject, materials, colors, shapes); **analyze** (how the elements work together);
   **interpret** (what it may mean in its own context — and what I do not know);
   **evaluate** (a judgment with a reason). Climb in order; do not skip from "look" to
   "verdict."
3. **Worked example — climb on one work (15 min).** Take Hokusai's *South Wind, Clear Sky*
   (*Red Fuji*), a woodblock print from the series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* (S-364).
   **Describe:** a red mountain, a blue sky with cloud streaks, trees below. **Analyze:** the
   strong diagonal of the slope, the repetition of the mountain's triangle. **Interpret:** a
   single sacred mountain seen in a particular weather — and I do not know the artist's full
   meaning without asking. **Evaluate:** the repetition of the mountain's shape across the
   series gives it force — a *reason*, not just "I like it." Write each rung on your sheet.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** You did not rank; you *read*. Critique honors the work by
   paying it careful attention and by knowing the limits of what you can claim about a
   tradition that is not your own.
5. **Climb on a second work (15 min).** With a partner, choose a work from a different
   place or era — a Māori kōwhaiwhai rafter pattern (S-115) or an Akan adinkra cloth (S-084)
   or one from your own home. Climb all four rungs in writing. At "interpret," write at
   least one *respectful question* you would ask a living keeper of that tradition.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four rungs
   and why you must not skip from describe to evaluate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the ladder pre-filled for one work; the learner adds one observation to
  each rung rather than building the critique from scratch.
- **Extension:** For each rung, separate what is factual observation from what is the
  viewer's inference, and mark the claim you would need to verify by asking a living keeper.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four rungs are a sequence,
  not a computation. The ladder sheet is pre-numbered so a learner can work top-down or
  bottom-up in any order without counting.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, discussing, sharing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. For
  a learner who is blind or has low vision, provide a verbal or tactile description of the
  work (or a raised-line or textured reproduction) so "describe" and "analyze" are possible
  through touch and description rather than sight alone.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner climb all four rungs in order, grounding each
  in the work, and write one respectful question at the "interpret" rung?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed critique-ladder sheet on a work from a
  culture or era different from the learner's own, opened in the capstone art journal.

## Home connection

Choose an object or image at home that came from somewhere else — a cloth, a cup, a picture.
Write the four rungs for it in one sentence each, and one respectful question you would ask
the person who knows it best.
