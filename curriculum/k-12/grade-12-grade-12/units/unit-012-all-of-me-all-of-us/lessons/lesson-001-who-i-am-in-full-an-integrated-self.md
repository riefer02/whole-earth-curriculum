# Lesson 1 — Who I Am in Full: An Integrated Self

## Summary

Learners open the closing week by gathering themselves into one whole. They retrieve the
integrated-self work of the year and test its three threads — **who I am**, **what I value**,
and **how I will act** — across the contexts of their own life, watching how the same
through-line holds together. They write a short "who I am in full" statement and celebrate
it as the self they hand to adult life.

## Objectives

- Articulate an integrated sense of who they are, what they value, and how they will act,
  holding together across contexts. _(D01.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

You are not a different person in every room, and yet different parts of you come out in
different places — the you at the family table, the you with friends, the you in community
work, the you online. The question this week asks is not "which one is the real me?" but
*is there one me running through all of them, and can I say who that is?* A woven cloth
holds together because the threads run the whole length; the same is true of a person.

## Materials

- Integrated-self map
- Capstone journal
- Paper and pencil (low-tech); voice, body, pen, screen, or hands (low-tech)
- Narrative-identity page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the integrated-self map (center figure + four context rooms + three thread
  lines + closing line).
- Retrieval: from the unit's earlier work, the integrated-self statement's three threads —
  who I am, what I value, how I will act — and the "one self across contexts" idea
  (D01.S2.12.01). Today they are gathered and tested across four named contexts.
- Prepare a model map (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011), and hold
  the whole explicitly as *a living draft, never a verdict* — identity develops across the
  whole lifespan (Erikson, S-155), and an "I don't know this part yet" is welcome in any room.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an integrated self is
one through-line — who I am, what I value, how I will act — that stays recognizably the
same across every context, and it is celebrated, not graded.** This is the opening move of
a closing week, so there is no new heavy content; the lesson *consolidates* what the year
already built and turns it into a handoff. Model once, then let each learner gather their
own (S-011); the gathering is retrieval across the whole year, which is the science-of-
learning payoff of spacing (Dunlosky et al., S-039).

The **ethics** lens: "how I will act" makes the integrated self an ethical commitment, not
only self-description. The **egalitarianism** lens: every self is a whole honored as it is —
no map is better than another, and no learner's identity is measured against a template or a
ranking. The **global** lens: the lesson refuses any single model of what a person is. The written
map with rooms is one culture's shape for this passage — a self drawn from inside, in a
Western idiom — but many traditions name personhood relationally: Ubuntu (*umuntu ngumuntu
ngabantu*, "a person is a person through other persons," S-508), a Southern African ethic,
holds that one becomes a person *with and through* others, not alone, and other traditions
root the self in kin, roles, or land. The passage itself is also marked differently — a
naming, a song, a vow, or a pilgrimage. None is the default; learners may adapt the form, or
the model, to their own life. The
**technology** lens: "online" is one of the real rooms where a self shows up now, and the
lesson asks whether the same through-line holds there or gets distorted — without moralizing
about being online. The **environment** lens: the whole self is also a body in a place, in
relationship with land, water, and the living world; the closing week gathers that belonging
too (it returns in full in Lesson 3).

**Safeguarding:** the map may include anything a learner *chooses* to include; nothing is
required about trauma, family, or a marginalized identity. A learner may complete the map
privately, share only one line, or leave a room blank with "still becoming" — a complete and
honored answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall the year (8 min).** Without opening your journal, write the three threads of
   your integrated self from memory: **who I am**, **what I value**, **how I will act**.
   Then open your journal and check what you recalled and what you missed (retrieval: S-039).
2. **Meet the map (6 min).** Look at the model map (S-011). It has one center figure and
   four rooms — **family, friends, community, online** — and it asks one question in every
   room: *is the same me here?*
3. **Fill the rooms (14 min).** For each of the four rooms, write one real, specific way the
   three threads show up: something true of who you are, something you value, and something
   you actually do there. Leave any room blank and write "still becoming" if that is honest.
4. **Name the through-line (10 min).** Look across your four rooms. Write the one line that
   runs through all of them — the part that does not change from room to room — and one line
   on where it strains, and how you keep it unbroken.
5. **Write who you are in full (10 min).** In your journal, compose a short statement — no
   more than five lines — beginning: *"I am one person, in every room: ___"*. End it with
   what you hand to adult life.
6. **Celebrate (7 min).** In a circle or with a partner, share one line of your statement —
   spoken, written, or signed. Receive others' lines (by ear, eye, or hand) without ranking
   them; notice what the room repeats.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill two rooms and write a two-line "who I am in full" statement; share one
  line — spoken, written, or signed.
- **Extension:** Distill the statement to a single sentence you could say from memory, and
  test whether it still holds in a future context you expect to enter as an adult.
- **Communication access:** Every step — recalling, filling rooms, naming the through-line,
  sharing, or celebrating — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, typing, or AAC instead of
  speech. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete
  every task in their preferred mode; no step requires hearing or producing sound. A learner
  who is blind or has low vision completes the map through spoken description, dictation, or
  a tactile organizer rather than a printed sheet.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required. "Four rooms" and "three
  threads" are simple counts that the map already structures with labeled boxes, so no
  learner needs to hold numbers in mind to complete the task.

## Assessment

- **Summative + portfolio (unit):** The completed integrated-self map and the "who I am in
  full" statement, filed as closing capstone artifacts — assessed for specificity and
  honesty (does one through-line actually hold across the rooms?), never for agreement with
  any template.
- **Self + peer:** Did the learner name real, specific threads in more than one room and
  state the through-line in their own words? Shared without ranking.

## Home connection

Read your "who I am in full" statement (or one chosen line) to someone at home, and ask them
to name one way they have seen the same you in different rooms. Write one sentence on what
you heard.

- On relational personhood — becoming a person with and through others, not alone: Ubuntu,
