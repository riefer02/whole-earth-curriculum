# Lesson 6 — One Self, Across Contexts

## Summary

Learners open the identity arc by articulating an integrated self — who they are, what
they value, and how they will act — and then testing whether that self holds together
across the different contexts of a real life. They name a through-line that stays
constant, and they meet the idea that identity is an evolving story, not a finished
fact.

## Objectives

- Articulate an integrated sense of who they are, what they value, and how they will
  act, and hold it together across contexts. _(D01.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

You are not the same person in every room — you speak one way at home, another with
friends, another at a job interview. That is not hypocrisy; it is the ordinary art of
living in many contexts at once. The question identity asks is the opposite one: *is
there something underneath that is the same in every room?* Most people who live with
a settled sense of who they are can name a few constants — values and commitments that
do not change with the audience. Today you look for your constants.

## Materials

- Self-map template
- Context cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Narrative identity page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the self-map template; prepare the context cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D01.S2.11.01), articulating values and what a meaningful
  life looks like. Today we add the capstone turn: *integration across contexts.*
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): an
  **integrated self** is one whose parts — identity, values, actions — are consistent
  enough to be the same person across situations. **Narrative identity** is the idea
  that we make sense of ourselves through an evolving life story linking past, present,
  and future (McAdams, S-404) — offered as a *descriptive* account, never a required
  genre, and explicitly not a claim that a story must be tidy (a learner may feel
  fragmented or uncertain, and "I don't know yet" is a complete answer).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an integrated self
is a set of constants — values and commitments — that hold across contexts, not a rigid
sameness in every room.** Identity is a *developing, lifelong process*, not a finished
fact (Erikson, S-155); the "life story" account is one well-supported way to describe
how we knit that process together (McAdams, S-404). Hold both as description, and
never as a verdict: this unit must never hand a learner a label.

The **ethics** lens: the "how I will act" column is an ethical claim — about what kind
of person one intends to be. The **egalitarianism** lens: some contexts let people show
their full self and others force parts of it into hiding; the cost of that hiding is
not evenly distributed. The **global** lens: what "the same person" means varies by
culture — some traditions center the individual self, others center the self-in-
relationship; neither is the default. The **critical-thinking** lens: testing whether
something is *truly* constant across contexts is a check on self-description, not a
performance of consistency.

**Safeguarding:** nothing here requires disclosure of trauma, family secrets, or
marginalized identities. If a learner's "contexts" include real danger or precarity,
allow them to write about any contexts they choose, or none, and honor silence.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: what did you name as your values and your sense
   of a meaningful life? Today we ask whether those hold across every room you enter.
2. **Meet the three columns (10 min).** Work the frame together (S-011): **who I am**
   (facts, roles, identities), **what I value** (the commitments that guide me), **how
   I will act** (what I do because of those values). A facilitator example: *"I am a
   sister and a student; I value honesty; so I will not copy a friend's exam even when
   it is tempting."*
3. **Build your map (12 min).** Fill the three columns for yourself, in your capstone
   journal.
4. **Test across contexts (12 min).** Draw a context card. Ask: in *this* room, which
   of my columns stay the same and which shift? After three cards, name the parts that
   never shifted.
5. **Write the through-line (8 min).** Write one sentence that names the constant: "In
   every room, I am still the person who ___."
6. **Close (5 min).** Say why a through-line is a *center you return to*, not a cage —
   and why "I don't know yet" is also a complete answer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill only "who I am" and "what I value," and name one thing that is the
  same at home and with friends.
- **Extension:** Write the through-line, then test it against a *future* context you
  expect to enter (university, a first job, a new city) and revise it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner fill all three columns and name at least
  one genuine constant across contexts?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The self-map with the through-line sentence, kept in
  the capstone journal (a seed for Lesson 10's statement).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what one thing about *them* stays the same in every room. Write it
down and compare it to your own through-line.
