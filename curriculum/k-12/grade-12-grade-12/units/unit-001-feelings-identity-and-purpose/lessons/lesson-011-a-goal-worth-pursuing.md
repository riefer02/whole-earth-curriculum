# Lesson 11 — A Goal Worth Pursuing

## Summary

Learners open the purpose arc by imagining possible selves — the person they hope to
become, fear becoming, and expect to become — and deriving from them a candidate life
goal. They test that goal against four questions (is it mine, meaningful, reachable,
and worth its cost?) and choose one to carry into the planning lesson. The lesson holds
that purpose grows by trying and reflecting, not by sudden certainty.

## Objectives

- Exercise self-directed agency by choosing a meaningful life goal that is genuinely
  their own. _(D01.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

Ask a hundred people your age what they want to do and most will give you a job title
borrowed from a parent, a feed, or a fear. A *meaningful* goal is different: it is
something you would still care about if no one praised you for it, something that pulls
you forward even when it is hard. The strange fact is that very few people your age
feel certain of their purpose — and that is normal, not a failure. Purpose is not found
fully formed; it is grown by trying, reflecting, and being supported along the way.

## Materials

- Possible-selves map
- Goal test cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Purpose page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the possible-selves map; prepare the goal test cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D01.S2.11.01), values and what a meaningful life looks like;
  from Lesson 10, the integrated self statement. Today the statement gains a *direction*.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): a
  **possible self** is a self a person imagines becoming — hoped-for, feared, or
  expected (Markus & Nurius, S-399); a **meaningful goal** is one that is genuinely
  one's own and worth its cost; **purpose** is a stable, self-chosen direction that
  grows by exploration and reflection, not sudden certainty (Damon, S-400).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a meaningful goal is
chosen, tested, and worth its cost — and not having one yet is normal, not a deficit.**
Possible selves are the bridge between the present self and future motivation (Markus &
Nurius, S-399). Purpose grows by trying, reflecting, and being supported, and only a
minority of young people report a clear sense of it — so the lesson must relieve
pressure, not add it (Damon, S-400).

The **ethics** lens: what makes a goal *worth pursuing* is partly an ethical question —
what it does for others, not only for oneself. The **egalitarianism** lens: the menu of
possible selves is not the same length for everyone — a goal is only genuinely "mine"
if I chose it among real options, and some learners have had fewer; honor that rather
than moralizing about ambition. The **global** lens: what counts as a worthy goal — a
trade, a calling, a role in family, a craft — varies across cultures; none is the
default or the "higher" path. The **critical-thinking** lens: the four tests are a check
that separates a borrowed goal from an authored one.

**Safeguarding:** no learner is required to commit publicly or to reveal a feared self
rooted in trauma. Feared selves may stay private; the map's feared path can be drawn
lightly or left as "one I'd rather not name."

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10: what is your integrated self statement? Today it
   gains a *direction* — a goal worth walking toward.
2. **Meet possible selves (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): from "me now," three
   paths — the self I *hope* to become (a healer, a builder), the self I *fear* becoming
   (a person who gave up), the self I *expect* to become (the likely next step). Notice
   each path ends in a possible goal.
3. **Draw your map (12 min).** In your capstone journal, draw your three paths and name
   the self at each end. (The feared path may stay private or light.)
4. **Test a candidate goal (12 min).** Choose one goal implied by your hoped-for or
   expected path. Run the four tests: *Is it mine or borrowed? Is it meaningful to me?
   Is it reachable in steps? Am I willing to pay its cost?*
5. **Choose (8 min).** Write the goal you will carry into tomorrow's planning lesson,
   and one sentence on which test it passed most easily and which was hardest.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say why "I don't know yet" is a fine place to begin a search for
   purpose — and what small thing would be worth trying this month.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Draw only the hoped-for and expected paths, and test one goal against
  just two questions ("mine?" and "meaningful?").
- **Extension:** Name a feared self that is *motivating* (the person you refuse to
  become) and derive one goal from refusing it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner imagine hoped-for/feared/expected selves,
  test a candidate goal against the four questions, and choose one with reasons?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The possible-selves map and chosen goal, kept in the
  capstone journal (feeds Lesson 12's plan).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what goal they most wanted at your age and whether it stayed theirs
or was borrowed. Write one sentence on what you learned about choosing.
