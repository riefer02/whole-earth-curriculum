# Lesson 4 — Caring at a Distance

## Summary

Learners practice responding to large, emotionally charged global situations with care
that stays clear and useful. They learn the difference between **empathic concern**
(feeling for another, which moves us to act) and **personal distress** (feeling
swamped, which turns us inward), and they notice **numbing** — how care can fade when a
problem feels too big. They map one real situation onto their circles of reach and
commit to one concrete action.

## Objectives

- Respond to a complex, emotionally charged *global* situation with insight, care, and
  emotional wisdom, choosing action over being swamped. _(D01.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

You live in a time when a flood, a war, or a strike on the other side of the world
arrives on your screen before breakfast. The feeling it raises is real — and it is easy
for that feeling to go one of two unhelpful ways: it can wash over you and then drain
away into "too big, not my problem," or it can swamp you until you look away entirely.
Emotional wisdom at this scale is not feeling *less*. It is feeling in a way that keeps
you able to act.

## Materials

- Circles-of-care map
- Global situation cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Empathic concern vs. distress page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the circles-of-care map; prepare the global situation cards, kept
  factual and gentle (no graphic imagery — see Facilitator note).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, the respond loop; from Grade 11 (D01.S2.11.02), what we owe
  people unlike ourselves. Today we scale the loop up to the world.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011):
  **empathic concern** is other-oriented feeling that moves us to help; **personal
  distress** is self-oriented feeling of being overwhelmed by another's pain — two
  different responses with different consequences (Batson, S-230). **Numbing** is the
  fading of feeling when a problem's scale exceeds what we can hold.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **care at a distance
needs a route to action, or it turns into distress or numbness.** The
concern-versus-distress distinction is well evidenced — other-oriented empathic
concern motivates helping, while self-oriented distress motivates escape (Batson,
S-230); empathy is a real, well-studied route to prosocial behavior, and it is *not*
automatic (Decety & Cowell, S-231). Hold this as description, not as a demand that
learners feel a prescribed way about distant suffering.

The **ethics** lens: whether and how to respond to distant suffering is a question of
what we owe strangers. The **egalitarianism** lens: whose suffering gets noticed and
whose gets forgotten is uneven — proximity, race, and wealth shape whose crisis becomes
"the" crisis. The **global** lens: this is the global lens, made practical — a flood in
one river valley, a drought in another, a strike in one city, all real and all
different. The **critical-thinking** lens: verify what you are told about a distant
crisis before your feeling commits you to a response.

**Safeguarding:** describe situations factually and gently; no graphic or gratuitous
detail; always connect to agency (something the learner can do). If a learner has a
lived connection to a crisis, let them speak only if they choose, and hold it as
honored experience, not a case study.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what are the four steps of the respond loop?
   Today we use them on situations too big for one person.
2. **Meet concern vs. distress (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a flood
   displaces thousands of families. Feeling *for* them (concern) makes you ask "what
   can I do from here?" Feeling *swamped by* the images (distress) makes you want to
   close the screen. Both are real; only one keeps you able to act. Name the third
   danger: **numbing** — the feeling fading because the scale is too big.
3. **Map a situation (15 min).** In pairs, take a global situation card. On the
   circles map, note one useful, real response at each reach: what I can feel and
   understand (self), what I can do with family and community, and what I can support
   toward humanity and the living world.
4. **Argue the useful response (8 min).** Which single response is both possible *and*
   useful, rather than merely symbolic? Defend your choice.
5. **Commit to one action (10 min).** Write one concrete action you can actually take
   this month — a donation, a letter, a shift in what you buy, a conversation — and one
   sentence on how you will keep care from curdling into numbness.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, the difference between being moved and
   being swamped — and which one lets you help.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one feeling the affected people might have and one small thing you
  could do, leaving the full map for later.
- **Extension:** Research the situation's facts in two sources and check them against
  each other before writing your action (two-source check).

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish concern from distress, name
  numbing, map a response at multiple reaches, and commit to a concrete, useful action?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed circles-of-care map with the committed
  action, kept in the capstone journal.

## Home connection

Pick one global situation and, with someone at home, find one real thing your household
could do together. Write one sentence on what you decided.
