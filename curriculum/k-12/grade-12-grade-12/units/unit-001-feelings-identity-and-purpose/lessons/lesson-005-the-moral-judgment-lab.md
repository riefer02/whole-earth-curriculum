# Lesson 5 — The Moral Judgment Lab

## Summary

Learners run a full, deliberate moral judgment on a single complex, emotionally
charged case — the week's capstone. They gather the three inputs (reasons, feeling,
evidence), form a first verdict, **surface a dissent**, and then revise. The lesson
makes visible that good judgment is a process with a revision step, not a single flash
of certainty.

## Objectives

- Integrate emotional understanding into ethical judgment across a full deliberation,
  and respond with insight and care under emotional charge. _(D01.S1.12.01, D01.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Real moral decisions are rarely made in a clean instant. A committee weighing whether
to close a polluting factory that employs a whole town, a family deciding who to tell a
painful truth, a student council deciding a penalty for a friend — each one starts with
a strong first feeling, meets reasons and facts, hears a disagreement, and only then
lands. Today you practice landing that way, slowly and on purpose, so that when a real
decision arrives without warning you already know the route.

## Materials

- Judgment lab frame
- Case study
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Dissent page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy the judgment lab frame; prepare the case study — one case that is ethically
  weighty and emotionally charged but one step removed from learners' private lives.
  A reliable shape: a scarce resource, a competing duty, and a real cost no matter the
  choice (e.g., a hospital with one ventilator, a scholarship between two worthy
  students, a community deciding whether to allow a factory that both pays and
  pollutes). Avoid cases that map directly onto a learner's likely trauma.
- Retrieval: the whole week — compass/fog (L1), the triangle (L2), the respond loop
  (L3), concern vs. distress (L4). Today: run them all at once.
- Model the frame's four stages once (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **judgment is a
deliberation with a revision step, and dissent is an asset, not a threat.** Surfacing
disagreement improves decisions — which is why the frame forces a dissent before the
final verdict. Emotion sits *in* the deliberation as an input (Lerner et al., S-333;
Decety & Cowell, S-231), never as the whole verdict.

The **ethics** lens: the case *is* an ethics question, and the verdict is a claim about
what is right. The **egalitarianism** lens: require learners to name whose voice is
weakest in the case and what the verdict does to that person. The **global** lens: note
that the same case, read in a different culture's moral tradition, might weight the
inputs differently — and that is data, not a mistake. The **critical-thinking** lens:
the dissent step is a check on premature certainty; the revision step is a check on
stubbornness.

**Safeguarding:** use the provided case or an equivalent kept one step removed. Do not
require disclosure of personal trauma. If learners grow heated, that is the material —
name it and return to the frame rather than adjudicating their private disputes.

## Procedure

1. **Recall the week (5 min).** In pairs, retrieve the week's four tools: compass/fog,
   the judgment triangle, the respond loop, and concern vs. distress. Say each in one
   phrase.
2. **Read the case (8 min).** Hear or read the case twice. Quietly, note your first
   feeling — name it precisely (RULER, S-015) — but do not announce a verdict yet.
3. **Gather the inputs (12 min).** On the lab frame, list **reasons** (consequences,
   duties), **feeling** (yours and each person's), and **evidence** (what you can
   verify; what you cannot).
4. **Form the first verdict (8 min).** Write it in one sentence, with the single
   strongest reason and the single strongest feeling behind it.
5. **Surface the dissent (8 min).** In your panel, one person argues *against* the
   verdict — as fairly and strongly as they can. Write down the best point the dissent
   made.
6. **Revise (10 min).** Write your revised verdict. It may be the same or different —
   say what the dissent changed, and what feeling added that reason alone would have
   missed.
7. **Close (4 min).** In one sentence: what did the *revision* step do that a single
   flash of certainty would not have?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Complete reasons + feeling only, and revise by choosing between two
  provided verdicts with reasons.
- **Extension:** Write the *principle* your verdict implies, then test it against a
  second, slightly different case — does it still hold? Revise if it does not.

## Assessment

- **Summative + portfolio (unit):** The completed judgment lab frame — inputs, first
  verdict, dissent, revised verdict — as a capstone artifact of emotional-moral
  judgment.
- **Formative (peer + self):** Could the learner name all three inputs, host a fair
  dissent, and explain what revision changed?

## Home connection

Summarize the case to someone at home in two sentences (no names, real or invented),
and ask what their verdict would be and why. Write one sentence on where they
differed from you.
