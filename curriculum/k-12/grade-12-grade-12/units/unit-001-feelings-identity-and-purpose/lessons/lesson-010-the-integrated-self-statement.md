# Lesson 10 — The Integrated Self Statement

## Summary

Learners consolidate the identity arc into a single written artifact: the integrated
self statement. Drawing on the week's work — the through-line, the value-action map,
the authoring map, and the "what I keep" list — they draft, peer-check, and revise a
statement of who they are, what they value, how they will act, and how they author
themselves within the forces around them.

## Objectives

- Articulate an integrated sense of self held across contexts, and explain how they
  author identity within global, cultural, and technological forces. _(D01.S2.12.01,
  D01.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

A capstone is a place where scattered work becomes one thing. This week you wrote a
through-line, named a value-action gap, mapped what you inherited and what you chose,
and listed what no feed decides. Left separate, those are notes. Woven together, they
become something you can carry: a short statement, in your own words, of who you are
and how you intend to live. People in every culture mark the passage to adulthood with
some version of this — a vow, a naming, a commitment. Today you write yours as a draft,
not a verdict.

## Materials

- Statement frame
- Peer-check guide
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Life-story page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the statement frame; prepare the peer-check guide.
- Retrieval: the identity arc — Lesson 6 (through-line), Lesson 7 (value-action map),
  Lesson 8 (authoring map), Lesson 9 (what I keep). Today they become one document.
- Prepare a model statement (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011),
  and hold the frame explicitly as *a draft of a living story*, never a final verdict —
  identity is an evolving process (Erikson, S-155), and an "I don't know yet" line is
  welcome.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the integrated self
is written, and it is written as a draft — a living story, not a verdict.** Identity is
a lifelong, developing process (Erikson, S-155), and a life story is how people knit
past, present, and future together (McAdams, S-404); both are offered as description.
The statement is a capstone *artifact*, not a test of who is "more" integrated.

The **ethics** lens: "how I will act" makes the statement an ethical commitment, not
only self-description. The **egalitarianism** lens: every self is honored as a whole in
the making — no statement is better than another, and no learner's identity is graded
against a template. The **global** lens: the form (a written statement) is one culture's
shape for this; other traditions mark the same passage with a vow, a song, a naming —
none is the default, and learners may adapt the form. The **critical-thinking** lens:
the peer-check's three questions are a check for specificity, consistency, and
authenticity — exactly the habits the unit trains.

**Safeguarding:** the statement may include anything a learner *chooses* to include;
nothing is required about trauma, family, or marginalized identity. A learner may write
it privately or share one line only. The artifact belongs to the learner.

## Procedure

1. **Recall the week (5 min).** In pairs, retrieve the four pieces: your through-line
   (L6), your value-action commitment (L7), your kept/revised/refused (L8), your "what
   I keep" list (L9).
2. **Meet the frame (8 min).** Read the model statement (S-011). Notice it answers four
   prompts: **who I am, what I value, how I will act, how I author myself within
   forces.** A strong line is *specific* and sounds like no one else.
3. **Draft (15 min).** Write your statement in your capstone journal — four lines and a
   title. Write freely; revision comes next.
4. **Peer-check (10 min).** Exchange with a partner. Ask the three questions: *Is it
   specific? Does it hold across contexts? Does it sound like you?* Mark one line to
   strengthen.
5. **Revise (10 min).** Rewrite the one line your partner marked, and read the whole
   statement once aloud, quietly or to your partner.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say why this document is a *draft you will revisit*, not a cage —
   and why "I don't know yet" can live inside it too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Write only "who I am" and "what I value," with the title, and revise one
  line.
- **Extension:** Distill the statement to a single sentence you could say from memory,
  and test whether it holds in a future context you expect to enter.

## Assessment

- **Summative + portfolio (unit):** The integrated self statement (draft), filed as a
  capstone artifact in the capstone journal.
- **Peer + self:** Did the learner answer all four prompts, respond to the three
  questions, and make a visible revision?

## Home connection

Read your statement (or one chosen line) to someone at home. Write one sentence on
their reaction and one on how the statement felt to say aloud.

## Resources

- On narrative identity as an evolving life story: McAdams, *The Psychology of Life

- On worked examples: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011); on spaced retrieval
  across the unit: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
