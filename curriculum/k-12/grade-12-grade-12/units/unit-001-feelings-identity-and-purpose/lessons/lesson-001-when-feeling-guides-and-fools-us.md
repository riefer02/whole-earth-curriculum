# Lesson 1 — When Feeling Guides and Fools Us

## Summary

Learners separate the two faces of emotion in judgment: feeling **informs** decisions
with real, often bodily data (a compass), and feeling **biases** decisions when it
runs ahead of evidence (a fog). They begin the capstone year by naming one moment
each where feeling helped and where feeling misled, and they adopt a first checking
habit — *pause and ask whether this feeling is a compass or a fog.*

## Objectives

- Analyze how emotion both informs and can bias judgment, and begin to tell the
  difference in real cases. _(D01.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Think of a real decision from this week: whether to speak up in a meeting or class,
how fast to trust a message on your phone, whether to lend money, whether to take a
risk. In each one, feeling was probably the first thing to arrive — a tightening, a
warmth, a warning. Sometimes that first feeling was wise: it noticed something real
before your slow thinking caught up. Sometimes it was loud and wrong: it pushed you
toward an answer the evidence did not support. The skill this unit opens with is not
"ignore your feelings" or "always trust your feelings." It is learning to tell the
compass from the fog.

## Materials

- Capstone journal
- Feeling-compass diagram
- Everyday moment slips (low-tech: paper)
- Somatic-marker summary (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the feeling-compass diagram; prepare the moment slips.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D01.S1.11.01), naming the emotional climate of a group;
  from Grade 10 (D01.S1.10.01), how personal history and culture shape how a feeling
  is felt and named. Today we add the capstone turn: *judgment*.
- Prepare a plain definition: a **judgment** is a decision about what is true or what
  to do. A **bias** here is a systematic lean — not a moral failing, but a predictable
  tilt (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description,
never as a claim that feeling is the enemy of reason**: emotion is *integral* to
judgment — it can improve decision quality and it can degrade it (Lerner, Li,
Valdesolo & Kassam, S-333). One respected scientific account of the "informing" half is
the **somatic marker** idea — that bodily responses ("gut feelings") carry information
that guides choice (Damasio, S-406); offer it as *one well-supported story, not settled
fact.* The "biasing" half is captured by the fast, automatic, feeling-laden first
response versus the slower, deliberate check (Kahneman, S-337).

The **ethics** lens: whether to act on a feeling is itself a question about what is
right to do. The **egalitarianism** lens: whose feelings are heard as "intuition" and
whose are dismissed as "too emotional" is not even across people — the word itself has
been used unevenly. The **global** lens: feeling and its value in judgment are
understood differently across cultures, so no single culture's "calm rationality" is
the default. The **critical-thinking** lens: "compass or fog?" is a test we run on a
feeling, not a verdict on a person. Preview: Lesson 2 puts feeling to work inside an
ethical judgment.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: how do you read the emotional climate of a
   group — its hope, fear, or grief? Today we turn that same noticing inward, toward
   your own judgment.
2. **Meet the two faces (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a driver
   slams the brake an instant before a child runs into the road. The bodily jolt
   *informed* the choice — it arrived faster than thought and was right (a compass).
   Now a second example: the same jolt of fear, reading a shocking headline, leads a
   person to share it before checking whether it is true — feeling *biased* the choice
   (a fog). Name the pattern: feeling can carry real data, and feeling can run ahead
   of evidence.
3. **Sort the moments (12 min).** With a partner, sort each everyday moment as
   "feeling helped" (compass), "feeling misled" (fog), or "both." Argue the
   borderline ones and say why.
4. **Name your own (10 min).** In your capstone journal, write one moment where a
   feeling guided you well and one where it misled you. For each, name what the feeling
   was and what the check would have been.
5. **Adopt the check (8 min).** Agree on a first habit: before acting on a strong
   feeling, pause and ask — *Is this feeling carrying real information, or is it loud
   but unproven?* Write it in your own words.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, why "trust your gut" and "never trust your
   feelings" are *both* too simple.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest moments and name the two faces with one word
  each (compass, fog), leaving the borderline cases aside.
- **Extension:** Find one real case from news or history where a leader's emotion
  either saved or wrecked a decision, and argue which face was at work.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the two faces, sort examples with
  reasons, and produce a personal example of each with a plausible check?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed feeling-compass diagram and the
  two-example journal entry, opened in the capstone journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a decision where a feeling turned out to be right, and one
where it turned out to be wrong. Write one sentence about each and one about what
surprised you.

## Resources

- On emotion as integral to — and able to bias — decision-making: Lerner, Li,
  Valdesolo & Kassam (2015), *Emotion and Decision Making* (S-333).
- On gut feelings as information (one respected account): Damasio, *Descartes' Error*
  (somatic marker hypothesis) (S-406).
- On the fast, emotional first response vs. the slower check: Kahneman, *Thinking,
  Fast and Slow* (S-337).
- On worked examples for a new analytical skill: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006)
  (S-011); on retrieval and spacing across the unit: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
