# Lesson 8 — Authoring, Not Inheriting

## Summary

Learners distinguish *inheriting* an identity from *authoring* one. They map the forces
that shaped them — family, culture, place, peers, media — and then name the choices
they have made *about* those forces: what they keep, what they revise, what they
refuse. They meet the four descriptive identity statuses as a way to locate themselves
in the authoring process without being ranked.

## Objectives

- Articulate how identity is authored within shaping forces rather than merely
  received, and locate themselves in that process. _(D01.S2.12.01, D01.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

You did not choose the language you first spoke, the family you were born into, the
place you grew up, or the stories that filled the air around you. Those forces shaped
you before you could consent. But they are not the whole story of you. At some point
you began to *do* something with what you were given — keep a custom, revise a belief,
refuse a label, choose a value your surroundings never offered. That is authorship:
not escaping the forces, but deciding what to do with them.

## Materials

- Authoring map
- Identity-status cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Identity-status page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the authoring map; prepare the identity-status cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, the integrated self; from Grade 10 (D01.S2.10.01), identity
  as socially and historically constructed. Today we add the *agentive* turn: authorship.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011):
  **inheritance** is what shapes us before we choose; **authorship** is the choosing we
  do about it. The four **identity statuses** — whether a person has *explored*
  options and *committed* to them — are a descriptive framework (Marcia, S-403),
  never a ladder, and "moratorium" (open exploration) is a normal, healthy phase, not a
  defect. **Social identity** is the part of self drawn from group membership, which
  can form even around trivial or arbitrary lines (Tajfel, S-232).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **identity is
authored — chosen about — not merely received, and authorship happens *within* forces,
not outside them.** The four statuses describe *how* (exploration vs. commitment), and
are offered as description with explicit non-ranking (Marcia, S-403): open exploration
is healthy and expected at this age. Group membership shapes identity even when the
groups are arbitrary (Tajfel, S-232), which is why the lesson asks learners to notice
*which* groups have quietly shaped them.

The **ethics** lens: what we refuse and what we keep is an ethical choice — about what
kind of person we will be. The **egalitarianism** lens: some people are *allowed* to
author freely and others are *defined by* forces they did not choose (race, class,
disability, migration status); authorship is a right unevenly granted, and that
unevenness is itself a thing to notice. The **global** lens: the forces that shape
identity — family, culture, place — differ everywhere; a village inheritance and a
megacity inheritance are both real and neither is the default. The **critical-thinking**
lens: the map is a check that separates "I was given this" from "I chose this" — the
core move of authorship.

**Safeguarding:** learners name forces they choose; no one is required to disclose
marginalized identities, migration history, or family rupture. Honor refusal and
silence.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10: how is identity shaped by society and history?
   Today we add the other half — what *you* do with that shaping.
2. **Meet inheritance vs. authorship (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a young
   person inherits a family craft and a family expectation to continue it. *Inheriting*
   is the craft and the expectation; *authoring* is choosing to carry it on, transform
   it, or leave it. Name the three moves: **keep, revise, refuse.**
3. **Map your forces (12 min).** On the left, list the forces that shaped you (family,
   culture, place, peers, media). On the right, for each, mark *kept, revised,* or
   *refused* — and one sentence on why.
4. **Meet the statuses (8 min).** With a partner, read the four statuses and locate a
   *person in a dilemma* (not yourself) on them. Note: "still exploring" is healthy,
   not a failure.
5. **Write the authorship sentence (10 min).** Complete: "I was given ___; I chose to
   ___." Name one force you refuse and one you keep, and why each is *yours.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Say why authorship is not escape — it is choosing *inside* the
   forces that shaped you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** List two forces and mark each kept/revised/refused, leaving the statuses
  for later.
- **Extension:** Write the authorship sentence, then test it: what force would you
  *add* to your map that your surroundings never offered, and why?

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish inheritance from authorship,
  map a force to each of keep/revise/refuse, and write a clear authorship sentence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed authoring map, kept in the capstone
  journal (feeds Lesson 10).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about one thing they inherited and what they chose to do with it
(keep, revise, or refuse). Write one sentence on how their choice compares to yours.
