# Lesson 3 — Responding with Wisdom, Not Reaction

## Summary

Learners build a concrete skill for emotionally charged personal moments: the respond
loop — **Pause, Name, Reframe, Respond.** They learn that a feeling can be regulated
without being suppressed, that how we interpret a moment shapes the feeling it
produces, and that wisdom means choosing a response that carries insight and care.

## Objectives

- Respond to a complex, emotionally charged personal situation with insight and care,
  using a deliberate loop instead of raw reaction. _(D01.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Think of the last time a message, a look, or a correction hit you like a wave — and you
answered before you had finished feeling it. Maybe the answer was right; maybe it made
the moment worse. The difference between *reacting* and *responding* is not that
responding people feel less. It is that they give the feeling a short, deliberate
route to travel before it becomes words or actions. That route is learnable — and this
lesson teaches it.

## Materials

- Respond loop card
- Charged-moment slips
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Appraisal-model page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the respond loop card; prepare the charged-moment slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the compass/fog check; from Grade 11 (D01.S1.11.02),
  naming areas of emotional strength and growth. Today we turn insight into a
  practiced response.
- Prepare a worked example of the loop (model it once before guided practice:
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011). Plain definitions: **regulation** is managing a
  feeling's shape and route — not deleting it; **appraisal** is the interpretation that
  sits between an event and the feeling it produces (Beck, S-159); **reframe** is
  trying on a truer, kinder interpretation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **response is a skill,
not a personality trait.** Emotion can be *regulated* — its expression managed and its
interpretation examined — and regulation is distinct from *suppression*, which
decouples the felt emotion from the outward show at a cost (Gross, S-225). The
appraisal idea — that a thought or interpretation shapes the feeling an event produces
— is a well-established account, offered descriptively (Beck, S-159), *not as therapy
and not as a command to feel differently.*

The **ethics** lens: a response that carries insight and care is partly an ethical
claim — about what the other person is owed even when I am upset. The **egalitarianism**
lens: who is *expected* to stay composed and who is allowed to be "passionate" is not
even across people; regulation is a skill everyone deserves to learn, never a demand
placed only on some. The **global** lens: how much feeling may be shown, and to whom,
varies by culture (display rules) — the loop is a tool, not one culture's script for
"calmness." The **critical-thinking** lens: the *reframe* step is a check on the
accuracy of my own interpretation, not a trick to feel good.

**Safeguarding:** slips are everyday, one-step-removed moments. Do not require any
learner to disclose their own conflicts. If a learner brings up real distress, honor it
and redirect to support, not to the exercise.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what is the one question to ask a strong feeling
   before acting (compass or fog)? Today we build the *whole* route from feeling to
   response.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Work the model example together (S-011): a harsh,
   one-line message arrives. **Pause** — one slow breath, no typing yet. **Name** — what
   do I feel? (hurt *and* defensive). **Reframe** — what else might be true? (they may
   be tired, or misread my words; this may not be about me). **Respond** — now choose
   words that carry insight and care.
3. **Practice the loop (15 min).** In pairs, take a charged-moment slip and walk all
   four steps out loud. Let your partner pause you at "Respond" and ask: does this
   carry insight and care?
4. **Distinguish regulate from suppress (8 min).** Argue briefly: what is the
   difference between *managing* a feeling and *swallowing* it? Why does swallowing it
   tend to cost more later?
5. **Write your own response (10 min).** Pick the slip that felt hardest. Write a
   response you would actually send or say, using all four steps.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say the loop in your own four words, and name the step you find
   hardest.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only Pause and Name; for Respond, choose from two
  pre-written options and say why one carries more care.
- **Extension:** Rehearse a second response to the *same* moment from two different
  relationships (a friend vs. a stranger), and explain how the loop changes.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self + performance):** Can the learner walk all four steps,
  distinguish regulating from suppressing, and produce a response that passes the
  "insight and care" test?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written response with the loop annotated, kept in
  the capstone journal.

## Home connection

Next time a heated moment comes up at home, try to Pause–Name–Reframe–Respond before
answering. Afterwards, write one sentence on which step helped most.

## Resources

- On emotion regulation (and the cost of suppression): Gross, *The Emerging Field of
  Emotion Regulation* (S-225).
- On interpretation shaping emotion (appraisal): Beck, *Cognitive Therapy and the
  Emotional Disorders* (S-159).
- On worked examples for a new skill: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011); on
  spaced retrieval: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
