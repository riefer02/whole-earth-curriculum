# Lesson 14 — What Sustains Me

## Summary

Learners close the unit by naming what sustains them. They map four kinds of anchor —
people, practices, values, and hope — each with one specific real example, and say what
it does when things are hard. They then run a closing retrieval pass across the whole
unit, distilling its tools into one sentence they will carry forward. The lesson ends
the capstone arc in agency and belonging, not in finality.

## Objectives

- Sustain self-regulation and purpose through setbacks and constraints by naming what
  sustains them, and carry an integrated self forward. _(D01.S3.12.02, D01.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

A ship in a storm does not survive by wishing the waves away; it survives because of
what holds it — the anchors, the crew, the practiced routines, the destination it still
believes in. The same is true of a life. You have been gathering your anchors all unit:
the people who steady you, the small practices that restore you, the values that point
you, and the hope that keeps the goal worth walking toward. Today you name them out
loud, so they are there when you need them — which is the whole point of the work.

## Materials

- Anchors map
- Unit retrieval sheet
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Habits-and-routine page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the anchors map; prepare the unit retrieval sheet.
- Retrieval: this lesson *is* the unit's closing retrieval pass — spaced across three
  weeks, which is exactly the evidence-backed payoff of spacing and retrieval (Dunlosky
  et al., S-039).
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): an
  **anchor** is something that steadies you — a person, a practice, a value, or a hope;
  **sustain** is what keeps purpose and self-regulation alive through the hard middle.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **what sustains a
person is nameable and portable — people, practices, values, and hope — and naming them
is a skill, not a confession.** Steady routines (sleep, movement, small rituals) are
part of what carries people through hard stretches, which is the honest, hedged core of
the "practices" anchor; it is not a claim that routine fixes suffering.

The **ethics** lens: what we owe ourselves in a hard stretch — care, not contempt — is
an ethical claim. The **egalitarianism** lens: some people have more anchors available
(wealth, family, safety) and some have had to build their own from very little; the
lesson honors every learner's anchors, whatever their size. The **global** lens: what
sustains a person — faith, family, craft, land, community — differs across cultures and
lives; no anchor is the "correct" one. The **critical-thinking** lens: the closing
retrieval is a check on what actually *stuck* across the unit, not a performance of
completion.

**Safeguarding:** anchors are the learner's own and may be private; no one is required
to name a trauma, a loss, or a family in crisis. "I don't have this one yet" is a
complete and honored answer for any anchor.

## Procedure

1. **Recall the unit (8 min).** Using the retrieval sheet, write each tool in one
   phrase: compass/fog, the judgment triangle, the respond loop, concern vs. distress,
   the integrated statement, the goal plan, the detour. (Spaced retrieval — the whole
   unit at once.)
2. **Meet the four anchors (8 min).** Work a model example (S-011): **people** (who
   steadies you?), **practices** (what small routine restores you?), **values** (what
   points you when you are lost?), **hope** (what future keeps the goal worth it?).
3. **Map your anchors (15 min).** For each anchor, name one specific real example and
   write one sentence on what it does when things are hard.
4. **Write what sustains you (12 min).** Compose one sentence — *"What sustains me
   through the hard middle is ___"* — and one sentence on how you will keep it close.
5. **Closing circle (8 min).** Each person who wishes shares one anchor aloud. Notice
   what the room repeats — the shared and the particular.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, what this unit left you that you did not
   have three weeks ago — and what you will carry into the rest of the capstone year.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one person and one practice that help, and write the single closing
  sentence.
- **Extension:** Write a one-page "sustainment plan" — what you will do, and who you
  will reach for, the next time the hard middle arrives — and file it with your capstone
  artifacts.

## Assessment

- **Summative + portfolio (unit):** The anchors map and the "what sustains me" sentence,
  filed as the closing capstone artifact; the unit retrieval sheet as evidence of spaced
  recall.
- **Self:** Did the learner name real, specific anchors and say what they do when things
  are hard?

## Home connection

Share your "what sustains me" sentence with someone at home, and ask them to name one
of *their* anchors. Write one sentence on what you heard.
