# Lesson 9 — Me in the Machine

## Summary

Learners examine how technological forces — algorithms, curated feeds, and social
comparison — quietly shape self-image, and how to keep authorship of identity inside
that machinery. They name what the feed *shows* and what it *hides*, notice how
comparison-based self-worth works (and why it is fragile), and write a "what I keep"
list: the parts of self no algorithm decides.

## Objectives

- Explain how they author their identity within technological forces rather than being
  defined by them. _(D01.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

The feed knows you — or it behaves as if it does. It learns which images hold your eyes,
which posts make you stay, which versions of other people make you feel behind. None of
this is neutral: a feed that shows you only others' best moments quietly teaches you
that *your* ordinary life is failing. Adolescence is exactly when these habits and
self-images are forming (WHO, S-311), which is why the question is not "get off your
phone" — it is *who is writing the story of you, and how do you keep the pen?*

## Materials

- Feed filter frame
- Feed scenario cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Social-media and self-image page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the feed filter frame; prepare the scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 8, authorship vs. inheritance; from Grade 10 (D01.S1.10.02),
  how technology and media influence emotional life. Today we apply authorship to the
  algorithm.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): an
  **algorithm** is a rule the platform uses to choose what to show you; **curation** is
  that selecting-and-hiding at work; **comparison** is measuring yourself against what
  you are shown. Distinguish **self-worth** built on comparison (fragile, depends on
  being "better than") from **self-compassion** (kindness toward yourself that does not
  depend on ranking) (Neff, S-222).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the feed curates and
hides, and comparison-based self-worth is fragile — authorship means deciding what you
keep, not being defined by what you are shown.** The evidence is balanced and hedged:
curated, edited images can distort self-image; social media can both lift (connection,
creativity) and weigh down (comparison, disrupted sleep); adolescence is a formative
time for these habits (WHO, S-311). Self-compassion is offered as a durable alternative
to comparison-based self-evaluation, as description, not moralizing (Neff, S-222).

The **ethics** lens: platforms profit from holding attention, and whether that is fair
to a forming self is an ethical question. The **egalitarianism** lens: whose image is
glorified and whose is invisibilized in feeds is not even; and access to technology is
itself uneven (the digital divide), so "just log off" is not equally available advice.
The **global** lens: feeds differ by region, language, and market — the "default" feed
is no one's universal. The **technology** lens: this is the technology lens made
personal — the effect of the machine on the human. The **critical-thinking** lens: the
filter frame is a media-literacy check: *what is shown, what is hidden, who benefits?*

**Safeguarding:** no learner is required to reveal their actual screen time, accounts,
or body image. Keep analysis on the scenarios and the mechanism, not on learners'
private feeds.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: what is the difference between inheriting and
   authoring? Today we author *inside* a machine.
2. **Meet the filter (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a feed that shows only
   athletes' best plays, everyone's vacation, everyone's acceptance letters. What does
   it *show*? (peak moments.) What does it *hide*? (the losses, the ordinary days, the
   struggle.) What does that hiding teach you about yourself? (that your ordinary life
   is failing.)
3. **Analyze a scenario (12 min).** In pairs, take a scenario card. Fill the frame:
   what the feed shows, what it hides, and how that shapes self-image.
4. **Notice comparison (8 min).** Argue briefly: why is self-worth built on comparison
   fragile? What would *self-compassion* offer instead — and why is it more durable?
5. **Write "what I keep" (10 min).** List the parts of you no feed decides — a
   relationship, a skill, a value, a way of seeing. Then write one rule for keeping
   authorship (e.g., "I follow things that make me more, not less").
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in one sentence, who writes the story of you — and how you
   keep the pen.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one thing a feed shows and one it hides, and write one thing you
  keep.
- **Extension:** Audit your own feed for one day (optional, private) and list what it
  shows vs. hides, then propose one concrete change and its predicted effect.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name what a feed shows and hides, explain
  why comparison-based self-worth is fragile, and write a genuine "what I keep" list
  with a rule?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed feed filter frame, kept in the capstone
  journal (feeds Lesson 10).

## Home connection

With someone at home, compare what you each notice a feed or advertisement assumes
about you. Write one sentence on the difference.
