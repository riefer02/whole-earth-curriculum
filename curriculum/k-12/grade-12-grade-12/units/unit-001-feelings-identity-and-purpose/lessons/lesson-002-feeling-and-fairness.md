# Lesson 2 — Feeling and Fairness

## Summary

Learners put emotion to work *inside* ethical judgment. A fair verdict draws on three
inputs — **reasons** (consequences and duties), **feeling** (one's own and others'),
and **evidence** (what can be verified) — and feeling belongs as a **witness, not the
judge.** They practice on short, age-appropriate dilemmas and name what feeling
contributed and where it needed a check.

## Objectives

- Integrate emotional understanding into ethical judgment, using feeling as an input
  alongside reasons and evidence. _(D01.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

When you must judge a real situation — whether a friend who broke a promise deserves
another chance, whether a rule is fair to everyone, whether to speak up when a group is
unfair to someone — you already use your feelings. Outrage tells you something matters;
warmth tells you someone is hurting. But if feeling is the *only* thing deciding, the
verdict is just a mood with confidence. This lesson teaches you to seat feeling at the
table of judgment, where it can testify, without letting it grab the gavel.

## Materials

- Ethical-judgment triangle
- Dilemma cards
- Spoken dilemma (low-tech)
- Empathic concern excerpt (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the ethical-judgment triangle; prepare the dilemma cards.
- Retrieval: yesterday's compass/fog distinction (Lesson 1). Today we apply it inside
  an ethical decision.
- Choose dilemmas that are real in a senior's life without being raw: e.g., "A student
  is unfairly blamed for a prank; speaking up will cost them popularity," "A coach
  ignores an injury to keep a star player in," "A family must decide whether to spend
  scarce money on one member's education or another's medicine." Avoid anything that
  demands personal trauma disclosure (see Facilitator note).
- Prepare plain definitions: **reasons** are why an act is right or wrong
  (consequences for everyone affected; duties one owes); **evidence** is what can be
  checked; **feeling** is what you and others feel (model the integration once:
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **feeling is an
input to ethical judgment, not its replacement** — it can carry moral information
(empathic concern) while also needing a check against reasons and evidence (Decety &
Cowell, S-231, on the relation between empathy and morality; Lerner et al., S-333, on
emotion as both informing and biasing). Hold the two together rather than collapsing
into "feelings are facts" or "feelings are irrelevant."

The **ethics** lens: this *is* the ethics question — how a right judgment is built.
The **egalitarianism** lens: ask who gets to be heard in the dilemma and whose feeling
tends to be discounted — the quieter person, the less powerful person. The **global**
lens: moral traditions worldwide weight feeling differently (some center care and
relationship, some center duty, some center consequences), and none is the default.
The **critical-thinking** lens: the triangle is a *check* on judgment, not a calculator
— it forces you to name your inputs before you announce a verdict.

**Safeguarding:** use the provided dilemmas or equivalents that stay one step removed
from learners' private pain. Do not require any learner to disclose their own trauma or
history. If a learner volunteers deeply personal material, hold it gently and redirect
to the dilemma, not the disclosure.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From yesterday: what were the two faces of feeling (compass and
   fog)? Today we put feeling to work inside a *fair* judgment.
2. **Meet the triangle (10 min).** Work a model dilemma together first (S-011): the
   unfairly blamed student. Gather the three inputs aloud — **reasons** (what happens
   to everyone if you speak or stay silent; what you owe a classmate), **feeling**
   (your outrage, the blamed student's fear and shame), and **evidence** (what you can
   actually verify about who did it). Then state a verdict.
3. **Run a dilemma in pairs (15 min).** Take a dilemma card. Fill the triangle: list
   reasons, feelings (yours and theirs), and evidence. Mark the place where feeling
   most helped and the place where it most needed a check.
4. **Answer your own counter-argument (8 min).** Write one sentence a fair critic
   would say against your verdict, and one sentence answering it.
5. **Share and test (10 min).** Exchange with another pair. Apply the "would I say it
   out loud?" test: can you defend the verdict using *all three* inputs, not just the
   strongest feeling?
6. **Close (5 min).** In one sentence: what did feeling add to your judgment, and what
   did the check add?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only reasons + feeling (skip evidence) for the first dilemma, and
  name feeling's contribution in one word.
- **Extension:** Add a second dilemma where feeling and reasons *conflict*, and argue
  which should give way and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner gather all three inputs, name where
  feeling helped and where it needed checking, and defend a verdict against a
  counter-argument?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed ethical-judgment triangle with the
  verdict and counter-argument, kept in the capstone journal.

## Home connection

Describe one small dilemma to someone at home (without names) and ask how *they* would
weigh reasons, feeling, and evidence. Write one sentence about where their answer
differed from yours.

## Resources

- On the relation between empathy, feeling, and moral behavior: Decety & Cowell,
  *The Complex Relation Between Morality and Empathy* (S-231).
- On emotion as both informing and biasing judgment: Lerner, Li, Valdesolo & Kassam
  (2015) (S-333).
- On worked examples for a new reasoning skill: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006)
  (S-011); on spaced retrieval across the unit: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
