# Lesson 3 — Who Asks the Questions? Who Benefits?

## Summary

Learners turn the lens of science ethics on the **framing of research itself**: who asks
the questions, who is studied and who is left out, who **benefits** and who **bears the
cost**. They trace real cases and learn to read any finding for the question that shaped it
— because the questions science does *not* ask shape the knowledge we have as much as the
ones it does.

## Objectives

- Reflect on the ethics of scientific practice — who asks the questions, who benefits, and
  how knowledge is used. _(D06.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

A medicine is tested only on young men, and later it works differently — or not at all —
for women and older people. A factory's pollution is studied only after the people living
beside it have been sick for years. A study asks "how do we extract more ore faster?" and
never asks "who drinks the water downstream?" The answers science gives depend on the
questions it was *allowed* to ask — and every question has a someone who asked it and a
someone who benefits.

## Materials

- "Who asks / who benefits" worksheet
- Science journal
- Low-tech: a product label, news item, or study abstract; scratch paper

## Preparation

- Gather two or three real case snippets (a medical-exclusion history note, a
  pollution-justice note, a supply-chain note).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, evidence vs values. Today we ask a *value-laden* but checkable
  question: who is in the room when the questions are written?
- Prepare the worked example (tracing one question) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **science is done by
people in societies, so its questions are framed by who has power — and we can read any
finding for who asked, who was studied, who was left out, who benefits, and who bears the
cost.** Teach the trace explicitly, then let learners run it (S-011). Hold two things
distinct: *factual* claims (women were historically excluded from many clinical trials; the
cobalt in batteries is often mined under harmful conditions) are checkable and cited; the
*value* judgment (exclusion and harm are unjust) is a stance we name openly, not a fact we
pretend is settled by the data alone.

The **ethics** lens: beneficence — doing good and not harm — is not automatic; it has to be
designed in, and its absence is visible in who is excluded. The **egalitarianism** lens: the
distribution of who-asks and who-benefits is the egalitarian question at its most concrete —
whose bodies are the "normal" default, whose knowledge counts, whose neighborhood is the
sacrifice zone (S-484, S-483). The **global** lens: the pattern recurs worldwide — the
cobalt that powers devices is mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo under
conditions Amnesty International has documented as harmful, while the benefit accrues
largely elsewhere (S-398); and Semmelweis's life-saving evidence was resisted partly
because it humiliated the powerful men it corrected (S-357). The **technology** lens: a
device in your hand has a geography and a supply chain — the question "who benefits?" runs
from the mine to the screen (S-398, S-281). The **environment** lens: environmental justice
names the finding that polluting sites and their harms are not distributed evenly across
people (S-483, S-484). Preview: Lesson 4 turns critique into a proposal for a more just
practice.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2, name the difference between a fact and a value. Today
   we ask whose questions produced the facts we have.
2. **Meet the trace (8 min).** Any finding can be traced through five stations: **(1) who
   asked the question, (2) who funded it, (3) who was studied, (4) who was left out, and
   (5) who benefits and who bears the cost.** Write the five stations as a chain.
3. **Worked example — the medicine tested on some (12 min).** Trace a medicine tested only
   on young adult men: who asked (researchers with a standard subject pool), who was studied
   (young men), who was left out (women, older people, children), who benefits (those whose
   bodies match the test), who bears the cost (everyone else, who may get a dose or effect
   not studied in them). The *question* "is this safe for people?" was answered as if one
   kind of body stood for all.
4. **Trace a second case (12 min).** With a partner, trace the cobalt in a rechargeable
   battery: who benefits (users of devices, companies, and the demand for energy), who bears
   the cost (mining communities and workers, where conditions and hazards are concentrated,
   S-398), and whose question — "cheaper batteries" versus "safe, fair mining" — was asked
   first. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — which question was *not* asked.
5. **Read a finding for its question (12 min).** Take a product label, news item, or study
   abstract. Run the five-station trace. Note the question that is missing — the one that,
   if asked, might change the answer.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — why "who asks
   the questions" matters as much as the answers themselves.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Trace only two stations — "who was studied" and "who was left out" — before
   adding the others.
- **Extension:** Identify one *never-asked* question in a real case, and write one sentence
   on how the answer might change who benefits.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The trace is qualitative — no arithmetic is required;
   provide the five-station chain as a pre-printed diagram to fill with names and roles
   rather than numbers, and allow every station to be completed in words or by pointing to a
   card.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing,
   or closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
   non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
   preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace a research question through its five
  stations, name who is left out, and state the missing question that a fuller science
  would ask?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed who-asks/who-benefits trace with a written
  reflection naming the missing question, opened in the science journal.

## Home connection

Pick one product you use. Trace it back as far as you can — who made it, under what
conditions, and who benefits. Write two sentences: one fact you found, and one question you
now wish someone had asked.
