# Lesson 8 — Science and Culture, Side by Side

## Summary

Learners practice holding a **scientific account** of life and a **cultural or spiritual
account** side by side without ranking one above the other. They meet **two-eyed seeing**,
chart what each account asks and offers, and articulate how the two relate — complementary
in some ways, genuinely different in others — with dignity for both.

## Objectives

- Hold scientific accounts of life's origin and diversity alongside cultural and spiritual
  accounts, and articulate how they relate. _(D06.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

The same living thing can be two things at once. A cedar is a photosynthesizing organism,
and it is also a relative, a teacher, a gift, in a people's story of who they are. Neither
description is false; they answer different questions. The mature skill is not choosing one
and discarding the other, but learning to hold both — and to say, clearly, how they stand
beside each other.

## Materials

- Side-by-side chart
- Science journal
- Low-tech: two written accounts, scratch paper

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the side-by-side chart; gather a pair of accounts (one scientific, one
  cultural or spiritual) from a tradition learners can respect at a distance or know up close.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, the scientific account and its open questions. Today we place it
  beside another account.
- Prepare the worked example (charting one pair) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **scientific and cultural
or spiritual accounts of life answer different questions, and the skill is to hold them side
by side without ranking — seeing where they complement and where they differ, with dignity
for both.** Teach the chart explicitly, then let learners work a pair (S-011). This is the
inquiry-over-creed commitment made concrete (philosophy §11): no account is imposed, and no
account is mocked. Frame as *wondering together*, and use written or anonymous questions if
speaking up is not safe for some learners (contexts, cultural power dynamics).

The **ethics** lens: ranking one people's account above another's has been used to justify
harm; refusing the ranking is an ethical stance, not a relativism that says nothing can be
checked. The **egalitarianism** lens: every people's account of life carries dignity — the
Māori tell of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, sky and earth, whose separation made room for life
(S-202); the Haudenosaunee tell of Sky Woman and a world made on a turtle's back (S-203);
the Yoruba tell of Olorun, the supreme creator who works through the orisha to shape the
world (S-500); the Inca tell of Viracocha, who rose from Lake Titicaca to call the world
into being (S-501); the Rig Veda's Hymn of Creation wonders openly whether anyone knows how
it all began (S-502); and the Qur'an tells of one God who made the heavens and the earth
and shaped the first human from clay (S-503) — and none is a lesser way of knowing for
being a story. The **global** lens: "two-eyed
seeing," seeing with both Indigenous and Western-scientific eyes together, is itself a
teaching from Mi'kmaq Elder Albert Marshall, carried into science by Indigenous scholars
such as Robin Wall Kimmerer (S-427) and documented worldwide through living heritage
(S-056). The **technology** lens: science's instruments answer "how" with unmatched power,
but they are silent on "what it means" and "how we should live with it" — questions the
cultural accounts take up. The **environment** lens: many cultural accounts carry ecological
knowledge refined over generations — when to burn, when to plant, how to read a landscape —
that complements rather than contradicts scientific ecology (S-320, S-321, S-427). Preview:
Lesson 9 moves to the physical world's fundamental forces.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, name what the scientific account shows and what it
   leaves open. Today we set it beside another account.
2. **Meet two-eyed seeing (8 min).** "Two-eyed seeing" means looking with one eye through
   Indigenous knowledge and the other through Western science, and using both (S-427). It is
   not blending into mush — it is seeing more, with two strengths at once.
3. **Worked example — chart a pair (15 min).** Take two accounts of a cedar: the scientific
   one (a conifer that photosynthesizes, part of a forest ecosystem) and a cultural one (a
   relative and teacher in a people's story). Chart each: *what does it ask?* (how does it
   work? vs what is it to us?) and *what does it offer?* (mechanism vs meaning and duty).
   Then write the relation: they do not contradict; they answer different questions.
4. **Chart your own pair (18 min).** With a partner, choose a living thing and two accounts
   — one scientific, one cultural or spiritual you can name respectfully (or from the
   examples, S-202, S-203). Fill the chart. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to
   show — one place they complement and one place they genuinely differ.
5. **Name the relation (7 min).** Write one sentence of relation: do they answer different
   questions? Does one carry facts and the other carry meaning and duty? Be precise — no
   ranking, but no pretending they are identical either.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — what it means
   to hold two accounts side by side with dignity for both.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Chart only "what each account offers" before adding "what each asks."
- **Extension:** Name a case where the two accounts appear to conflict (e.g., about a
   species' origin or a landscape's history) and write how you would hold both honestly
   without pretending the difference away.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The chart is qualitative — no arithmetic; provide it
   pre-printed with the two columns labeled so the learner fills phrases and single words,
   and allow the relation sentence to be spoken or recorded rather than written.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing,
   or closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
   non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
   preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner chart two accounts — naming what each asks
  and offers — and write one precise sentence of relation that neither ranks them nor erases
  their difference?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed side-by-side chart with the relation sentence,
  opened in the science journal — a capstone artifact for the science-and-culture strand.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for an account of a living thing — a story, a saying, a belief. Write it
beside the scientific account you know, and one sentence on how the two stand together.
