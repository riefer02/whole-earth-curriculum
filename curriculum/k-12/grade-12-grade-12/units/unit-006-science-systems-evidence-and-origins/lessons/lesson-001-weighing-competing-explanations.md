# Lesson 1 — Weighing Competing Explanations

## Summary

Learners meet the core move of Grade 12 science: when **two explanations compete** for
the same phenomenon, we weigh them against the **same three criteria** — **evidence**,
**coherence**, and **falsifiability** — instead of trusting authority or first
impressions. They work a historical case and score two explanations, then apply the weigh
to a fresh case of their own.

## Objectives

- Evaluate competing scientific explanations of the same phenomenon using criteria of
  evidence, coherence, and falsifiability. _(D06.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Two people give you different answers for the same thing — why the fish are gone from the
river, why a patient is sick, why the sky glows red at dawn. You cannot believe both, and
you need not guess. You can ask each answer the same three questions: *what evidence
supports you, do you fit with everything else we know, and what would prove you wrong?*
That is weighing, and it is a move every person, in every place, can make.

## Materials

- Explanation-weighing sheet
- Science journal
- Low-tech: two written explanations, scratch paper

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the explanation-weighing sheet (evidence / coherence / falsifiability +
  verdict).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D06.S1.11.01), designing a controlled investigation; from
  D06.S1.11.02, comparing ways of knowing. Today we aim those habits at *choosing among
  competing explanations*.
- Prepare the worked example (childbed fever) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **competing explanations
are weighed by the same three public criteria — evidence, coherence, and falsifiability —
not by who said them or how they feel.** Teach the worked example explicitly, then let
learners run the weigh on a fresh case (S-011). Keep two things distinct: a *factual*
question (which explanation fits the evidence better?) and a *value* question (which
outcome would we prefer?) — because the strongest explanation can be one we wish were not
true.

The **ethics** lens: weighing honestly means giving each explanation the same test and
accepting the verdict even when it is inconvenient. The **egalitarianism** lens: the three
criteria are open to anyone with curiosity and a record of observations — they need no
laboratory, no title, no fee. The **global** lens: the same weigh appears wherever people
reason — Semmelweis and John Snow tested germ explanations against "bad air" in 19th-century
Europe (S-357, S-358), and every culture that has compared two accounts of a river, a
sickness, or a sky has weighed evidence against belief. The **technology** lens: instruments
change what counts as evidence — a microscope, a telescope, a particle detector each open a
new court where explanations are tried (S-488). The **environment** lens: the weigh is
exactly what we need for contested environmental questions — overfishing versus pollution
versus a warming river — where more than one explanation is on offer. Preview: Lesson 2
sharpens each criterion into a working tool.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11, name one thing a controlled investigation must hold
   constant. Today we ask a bigger question: when *two whole explanations* compete, how do
   we choose?
2. **Meet the three criteria (8 min).** Write them down: **evidence** (what observations
   support it), **coherence** (does it fit with everything else we know), and
   **falsifiability** (could some possible observation show it wrong). An explanation that
   cannot be shown wrong under *any* observation is not testable.
3. **Worked example — childbed fever (15 min).** In the 1840s Vienna hospital, many mothers
   died of "childbed fever." Two explanations competed: **(A) bad air (miasma)** in the ward,
   or **(B) something carried from the autopsy room on doctors' hands.** Score both: **evidence**
   — Ignaz Semmelweis found that having doctors wash hands made deaths fall sharply (S-357);
   **coherence** — B explains why the ward staffed by midwives (who did not do autopsies) had
   far fewer deaths, which A cannot; **falsifiability** — A makes no prediction that would
   differ between the two wards, so it is hard to test, while B predicts a difference we can
   check. **Verdict:** B fits better. Write the score on your sheet.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** You did not "believe" B; you *weighed* it. The stronger
   explanation is not the one that feels right or comes from authority — it is the one that
   survives the same three questions.
5. **Weigh a fresh case (15 min).** With a partner, choose a contested question — a local
   environmental change, a medical claim, a cosmological debate (such as steady-state versus
   Big Bang, S-497). List two explanations and score each on evidence, coherence, and
   falsifiability. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — your verdict and the
   single strongest reason for it.
6. **Share and check (5 min).** Pairs exchange verdicts and check each other's scoring: did
   both explanations get the *same* three tests? Give one specific improvement.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the three
   questions you now ask any competing explanation before you trust it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the worked example's two explanations as a pre-filled sheet; the learner
  adds one piece of evidence to each column rather than building the case from scratch.
- **Extension:** State the decisive test — the observation that would settle the case — for
  each side, and explain why a test that both sides would survive cannot settle anything.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The lesson's few numbers (the year 1847, "far fewer
  deaths") can all be read as *comparisons* rather than arithmetic; provide the
  explanation-weighing sheet as a pre-printed organizer with the three columns already
  labeled, and allow the verdict to be reached in words — "this one explains the difference
  between the two wards; the other one can't" — with no computation at all.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing,
  or closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
  preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three criteria, apply them to two
  competing explanations, and write a verdict with a reason that names evidence rather than
  authority or feeling?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed explanation-weighing sheet (evidence,
  coherence, falsifiability, verdict), opened in the science journal.

## Home connection

Find two different explanations for the same thing in daily life — why a plant wilted, why a
road flooded, why a device stopped working. Write one sentence scoring each against the three
criteria and your verdict.
