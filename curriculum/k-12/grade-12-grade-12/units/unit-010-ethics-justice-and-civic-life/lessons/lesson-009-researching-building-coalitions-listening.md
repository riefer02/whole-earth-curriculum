# Lesson 9 — Researching, Building Coalitions, Listening

## Summary

Learners practice the first habits of an engaged citizen — **researching** an issue honestly,
**building a coalition** across difference, and **listening** to people they do not already agree
with. They study a real coalition (the Chipko movement of the 1970s, where villagers, especially
women, joined across difference to protect the forests their communities depended on), then begin
their own issue research with a source-check habit, learning that coalitions are built by
listening, not by sameness.

## Objectives

- Practice the habits of an engaged citizen by researching an issue, building coalitions,
  listening across disagreement, and acting collectively toward a fairer common life.
  _(D09.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

A single person acting alone can do a little; many people acting together can change a rule, a
street, or a forest. But coalitions do not begin with agreement — they begin with *listening*:
people who differ on much agree on one thing that matters to all of them, and build from that.
Before any of that, someone had to find out the facts. Research, coalition, and listening are the
three first habits of anyone who wants a fairer common life — and they are skills, learnable by
practice.

## Materials

- Coalition case sheet
- Issue-research sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: one written account of a local issue; scratch paper
- Enriched: primary and secondary sources on one issue

## Preparation

- Copy the coalition case sheet (the Chipko movement) and the research sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, weighing perspectives and listening to the other side; from Grade
  11 (D09.S3.11.02), solidarity across difference. Today we practice the *habits*.
- Prepare the Chipko case accurately: in the 1970s in the Himalayan region of India, villagers —
  especially women — embraced trees to stop them being felled, protecting forests their
  communities depended on, in a nonviolent movement inspired by Gandhi (S-328).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the habits of an engaged
citizen are concrete skills — research an issue honestly (check sources), build a coalition across
difference (people agree on one shared thing, not on everything), and listen across disagreement
(hear the other side's real reasons). Coalitions are built by listening, not by sameness.** Teach
the source-check explicitly (S-011); the Chipko case is the worked example (S-328). Do not turn
the case into a script every learner must copy; it is one real instance of a worldwide pattern
(also suffrage coalitions S-327, the Salt March S-390).

The **ethics** lens: research and listening are ethical acts — an engaged citizen owes others the
accuracy of checking a claim and the respect of hearing their reasons before answering. The
**egalitarianism** lens: coalitions work only when they are not built on sameness of power — the
Chipko movement drew its strength from women and villagers who had been left out of decisions
about their own forests; a coalition that centers the least heard is the one that holds (S-328).
The **global** lens: the pattern recurs worldwide — people who differ on much joining on one
shared thing (suffrage coalitions, S-327; the Salt March, S-390) — and each coalition is weighed
in its own terms, not flattened into one template. The **technology** lens: research today is
shaped by tools — search, feeds, and social platforms can surface one side and bury the other, so
a source-check (who is speaking, who benefits, what does a second source say) is now a basic civic
skill (S-278, S-217, S-481). The **environment** lens: the Chipko case is an environmental
justice case — the forest was a commons the community depended on, and its defense was also
defense of a shared, life-giving place (S-328, S-416). Preview: Lesson 10 turns the habits into a
designed collective action.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6, listening to perspectives we do not share. Today we add
   two more habits — research and coalition — and practice all three.
2. **Meet the three habits (8 min).** Write them: **research** (find out the facts, and check
   *who* is telling you and *what a second source says*); **coalition** (people who differ on much
   joining on one shared thing); **listening** (hearing the other side's real reasons before
   answering).
3. **Worked example — the Chipko movement (15 min).** Read the case: in the 1970s in the
   Himalayan region of India, villagers — especially women — embraced trees to stop them being
   felled, protecting forests their communities depended on, in a nonviolent movement inspired by
   Gandhi (S-328). Map it on your sheet: what was the issue? who joined and why? how did they
   listen across difference? what did they win, and what did they struggle with?
4. **Begin your own research (17 min).** Choose an issue you care about — local or global. On the
   research sheet, write: *what I know, what I need to find out, and where I will look.* For each
   place you look, note *who is speaking and who benefits*, and plan to check the claim against a
   second source (S-278, S-217).
5. **Share one finding (8 min).** With a partner, share one thing you learned from the case about
   *listening*, and one thing your research still needs to find out. Give one specific suggestion.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one habit you will
   practice first.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a case sheet with the Chipko map partly filled in; the learner underlines who
  joined and why, then answers with a starter ("The coalition held together because…").
- **Extension:** Audit one source on your issue for who is speaking and who benefits, and write
  one sentence on what a *second, different* source would add or correct.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. Any dates (the 1970s) are
  pre-printed and read aloud; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. The research sheet is
  a word organizer, doable in speech, writing, sign, or gesture.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, sharing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step
  requires producing or hearing sound. The case may be provided in print, braille, or audio
  description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner map a real coalition (who joined, why, how they
  listened), and begin researching an issue with a source-check habit (in speech, writing, sign,
  or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The coalition map and the begun research sheet, opened in the
  capstone ethics & civic journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about one time people in your community joined together despite their
differences to get something done. Write two lines: the shared thing they agreed on, and how they
listened.
