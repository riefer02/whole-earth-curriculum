# Lesson 3 — How My Values Formed

## Summary

Learners map where their own values came from — **family, community, culture, faith, schooling,
peers, media, and lived experience** — and reflect on which values they **inherited**, which they
**borrowed**, and which they are actively **choosing**. They hold the question of what they value
open rather than settling it as a fixed creed, noticing that values are formed, evolving, and
partly chosen — a reflection, not a verdict.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own evolving values, how they formed, and how they will test them in the world,
  holding questions open rather than settling them as creed. _(D09.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

No one invents their values from nothing. A value — honesty, courage, care, fairness, respect for
elders, love of land — usually arrives first as a voice, a practice, a story, or a rule from
someone who raised us or a place that shaped us. Some of it we keep, some we question, some we
quietly change. Naming where our values came from is not a quiz about loyalty; it is how we come
to *own* what we believe instead of merely carrying it.

## Materials

- Values-map sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a small stone or seed
- Enriched: a memoir excerpt modeling the reflective voice

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the values-map sheet; prepare the root labels (family, community, culture, faith,
  schooling, peers, media, experience).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, that values can be weighed but not ranked as "correct"; from Grade 11
  (D09.S1.11.02), competing accounts of a good life across traditions. Today we turn the lens on
  the self.
- Frame this as a private, low-pressure reflection; no learner must disclose anything they wish to
  keep private.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **values are formed by many
influences — family, community, culture, faith, schooling, peers, media, experience — and coming
to own one's values means noticing which were inherited, which were borrowed, and which are being
chosen; the question of what one values is held open, not settled as a creed.** This is a
reflection, not a confession and not a verdict. Protect privacy: learners may share as much or as
little as they choose, and there is no "right" set of roots. Do not judge any learner's values or
their sources; the skill is self-awareness, not agreement.

The **ethics** lens: reflecting on one's own values is itself an ethical act — the difference
between values that are *examined* and values that are merely *carried*. The **egalitarianism**
lens: ask *whose values get treated as the default and whose are dismissed* — a learner whose
family's values are honored in public has a different starting point than one whose are mocked;
the classroom must make room for every learner's roots without ranking them. The **global** lens:
values differ across cultures, and that diversity is a shared human good (S-389); the lesson
models that no single culture's values are the measuring stick. The **technology** lens: media and
screens are now among the loudest teachers of values, and often the least examined — naming
"media" as one root invites learners to ask what a feed is quietly teaching them (S-481, S-482).
The **environment** lens: for many people, values are inseparable from a place — land, water,
seasons — and the love of a place is a real root, one the map should make room for.
Preview: Lesson 4 asks how these values will be *tested* in the world.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2, that values can be weighed but not ranked as "correct."
   Today we ask where *mine* came from.
2. **Name the roots (10 min).** On your map, list the roots: **family, community, culture,
   faith, schooling, peers, media, lived experience.** For each, jot one value it planted in you —
   a one-word value is enough (honesty, courage, care, fairness, respect, love of land…).
3. **Trace to the trunk (12 min).** Write "my values" on the trunk. Move to it the values that
   feel most yours. For each, mark one of three: **inherited** (I carry it from my people),
   **borrowed** (I picked it up along the way), or **chosen** (I examined it and claimed it).
4. **Mark what is still growing (12 min).** On the branches, write the values that are **still
   forming** and the questions that are **still open** — the ones you are not ready to settle.
   Notice: an open question is not a failure; it is a value still being lived (S-001).
5. **Reflect (12 min).** In your journal, write a short reflection: *one value, where it came
   from, and whether you inherited, borrowed, or chose it — and one question you are holding
   open.* Keep it honest and private if you wish.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one root that
   surprised you when you noticed it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a map pre-labeled with roots and a few example values; the learner underlines
  2–3 values and marks inherited/borrowed/chosen with a simple symbol, then writes one sentence.
- **Extension:** Choose one value you marked "chosen" and write a paragraph on *when and why* you
  claimed it — the experience that moved it from borrowed to owned.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The map is spatial and
  word-based; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. Every step can be drawn, spoken,
  written, signed, or gestured.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, sharing, or closing — can
  be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer, optional share):** Can the learner trace their values to their
  sources, mark which were inherited, borrowed, or chosen, and name one value still forming — as a
  reflection, not a verdict?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The values map and short reflection, opened in the capstone
  ethics & civic journal — the strand's "evolving values" artifact.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What is one value you were raised with that you kept, and one you
changed?" Write their answer and your own in your journal — two lines each.
