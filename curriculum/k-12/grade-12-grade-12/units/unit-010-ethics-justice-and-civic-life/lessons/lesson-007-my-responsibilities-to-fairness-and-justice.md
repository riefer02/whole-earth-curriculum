# Lesson 7 — My Responsibilities to Fairness and Justice

## Summary

Learners meet the evidence that inequality and unequal access are real and measured — within and
between countries, and in basics like clean water — and then reflect on what they owe to fairness
and justice at four scales: **self, close community, wider society, and world**. They carefully
separate *evidence* (inequality is real and widespread) from *value* (what I owe in response),
learning that responsibility is reasoned from both, never assumed, and never a single correct
list.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own responsibilities to fairness and justice in their communities and in the
  wider world, and how they will act. _(D09.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

Fairness is not only a big abstract idea; it shows up in who gets clean water, who can see a
doctor, who gets heard, and who is left out. The facts are real and measured — some people are
born into far more than others, and some basics are still denied to millions. The harder question
is not whether unfairness exists but what it asks *of me* — here, in my own circle, and far away,
where I will never meet the people affected. That is a question of value, and it is answered with
reasons, not reflexes.

## Materials

- Inequality evidence sheet
- Circles-of-responsibility sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: two pre-written facts; chalk or sticks and open ground
- Enriched: an interactive inequality data page

## Preparation

- Copy the evidence sheet with all figures pre-printed and plan to read them aloud (number
  access); copy the circles-of-responsibility sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, defending a position across perspectives. Today we turn from the
  contested question to the *self* — what I owe.
- Distinguish the two moves clearly: *evidence* establishes that inequality exists; *value*
  establishes what, if anything, I owe in response. Neither is skipped, and neither is smuggled
  into the other.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **inequality and unequal access
are real and measured (evidence), and what each of us owes to fairness and justice is a question
of value reasoned from that evidence — at the scale of self, community, society, and world. There
is no single correct list of responsibilities; the skill is to reason from evidence to a value
commitment without confusing the two.** Present the evidence accurately but lightly (the lesson is
about *responsibility*, not about mastering statistics): inequality within and between countries
is large and documented (S-464, S-283); unequal access to basics such as safely managed drinking
water is real and widespread (S-065). Offload all numbers to the pre-printed sheet and read them
aloud; never require computation.

The **ethics** lens: the move from "this is unfair" to "therefore I owe…" is an *inference*, not
automatic — philosophers have long argued about whether the better-off owe the worse-off anything,
and the lesson holds that question open rather than decreeing an answer (S-388, S-459). The
**egalitarianism** lens: the heart of the strand — who benefits and who is left out — is here made
personal: my responsibility is partly shaped by *where I stand* in the unequal order, which is why
the answer differs honestly from one learner to another. The **global** lens: justice is argued
differently across traditions — some center equal treatment, some need, some capability and
freedom (S-388, S-459, S-460) — and a learner's answer may draw on any of them, or on none, as
long as it is reasoned. The **technology** lens: technology can widen or narrow the gaps — digital
access is itself now an inequality (S-006, S-283) — so part of a responsibility to justice is
asking who a new tool leaves out. The **environment** lens: unequal access to clean water and a
safe environment is among the starkest injustices, and the people who did least to cause
environmental harm often bear the most of it (S-065, S-005). Preview: Lesson 8 turns these
responsibilities into concrete actions.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6, a defended position and what it cannot settle. Today the
   question turns inward: *what do I owe?*
2. **Meet the evidence (10 min).** Read the evidence sheet aloud together. Notice what it shows:
   inequality within and between countries is real and large (S-464, S-283); unequal access to
   basics like clean water is real and widespread (S-065). These are *facts* — what is. They do
   not, by themselves, tell you what to do.
3. **Name the value question (5 min).** Write the move: *evidence* tells us the world is unequal;
   *value* answers "what do I owe in response?" The two are different, and honest reasoning keeps
   them different (S-388, S-459, S-460).
4. **Work the four circles (20 min).** On the circles sheet, write one responsibility you
   actually accept at each scale — **self** (a habit of fairness), **close community** (something
   in your school, neighborhood, or family), **wider society** (a rule, a vote, a voice), and
   **world** (a far-away fairness you care about). For each, write *one reason*.
5. **Check the reasoning (10 min).** With a partner, ask of each responsibility: *is this a real
   thing I could do, and is my reason a reason — not just a feeling?* Give one specific
   suggestion.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one responsibility
   you now hold that you had not named before.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a circles sheet with example responsibilities printed at each ring; the learner
  circles two they accept and adds one of their own, with a starter reason ("I owe this because…").
- **Extension:** Distinguish *charity* (a one-way gift that leaves the power where it was) from
  *justice* (changing the rule or the structure), and write which of your responsibilities is
  which, and why (S-345).
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** All figures are offloaded to the pre-printed evidence sheet and
  read aloud; nothing must be computed, compared numerically, or ordered. The circles are spatial,
  not numerical; the whole reflection can be done in words, spoken, written, or signed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading aloud, naming, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. The evidence sheet may be provided in print, braille, or audio
  description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name a responsibility to fairness and justice at
  each scale with a reason, and distinguish the *evidence* that inequality is real from the
  *value* of what they owe (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed circles-of-responsibility sheet, added to the
  capstone ethics & civic journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What is one thing you think we owe to people who have less than us?" Write
their answer and your own — one line each, with one reason each.
