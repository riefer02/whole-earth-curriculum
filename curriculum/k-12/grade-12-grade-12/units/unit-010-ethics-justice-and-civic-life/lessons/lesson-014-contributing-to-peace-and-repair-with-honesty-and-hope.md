# Lesson 14 — Contributing to Peace and Repair with Honesty and Hope

## Summary

Learners close the unit by reflecting on how they can contribute to peace and repair in a divided
world — locally and globally — holding **honesty** about what is broken and hard to repair together
with **hope** grounded in real agency. They study one real attempt at reconciliation (South
Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which traded some punishment for truth, disclosure,
and repair) and write their own contribution, gathering the unit's artifacts into the capstone
journal as a record of a person who can disagree, repair, and build — with inquiry, not creed, as
their guide.

## Objectives

- Reflect on how they can contribute to peace and repair in a divided world, locally and globally,
  with both honesty and hope. _(D09.S4.12.02)_

## Connection

It is easy to fall into two opposite traps about a divided world: despair, which says nothing can
be fixed, and denial, which says nothing is broken. Neither is honest, and neither is helpful. The
harder, truer stance holds both at once — *here is what is broken, and here is what is real to
build on.* That is what peacemakers do: they look squarely at the harm, and they still plant
something. Hope is not pretending; hope is seeing a real seed and watering it.

## Materials

- Honesty-and-hope sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a small seed or sapling
- Enriched: a short account of reconciliation after serious harm

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column sheet; prepare the reconciliation account.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 13, the three restorative questions; from across the unit, reasoning,
  weighing, acting, and repairing. Today we close the capstone.
- Hold the case accurately: South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–1998), after
  apartheid, invited public testimony about past abuses and, in defined cases, offered amnesty in
  exchange for full disclosure, alongside reparations — a real, documented, and contested attempt
  to trade some punishment for truth and repair (S-392).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **contributing to peace and
repair in a divided world means holding honesty (what is broken and hard to repair) together with
hope (what is real to build on) — never despair, and never empty optimism. The learner names a
concrete contribution, locally and globally, and the unit's artifacts are gathered as a record of
a person who can disagree, repair, and build — with inquiry, not creed, as their guide.** Hold the
case accurately and with care (S-392); present it as one real, contested attempt, not a perfect
model. Do not prescribe what any learner's contribution must be; the skill is that it is honest
*and* hopeful at once.

The **ethics** lens: honesty about harm is itself a moral commitment — reconciliation begins with
the truth of what happened, and the TRC's trade of punishment for disclosure is a real ethical
choice with real strengths and limits (S-392), held as a question, not a verdict. The
**egalitarianism** lens: peace that papers over the imbalance between the powerful and the harmed
is not peace — repair must center the people who were harmed, and a contribution that ignores
power is only a wish. The **global** lens: reconciliation and repair are attempted worldwide — the
TRC is one documented case, and humanitarian neutrality (the Red Cross and Red Crescent movement)
is another real, global form of serving people in conflict regardless of side (S-329, S-392);
neither is a universal template, and both are held as examples to learn from. The **technology**
lens: in a connected world, both harm and repair now travel instantly — a lie can go global in
seconds, and so can a truth or a gesture of repair — so a contribution must consider the tools it
uses and who they reach (S-481, S-482). The **environment** lens: peace with one another is
incomplete without peace with the living world — repairing a divided Earth is part of repairing a
divided humanity, and a hopeful contribution can be as small and real as tending one place.
Close: gather the unit's artifacts into the capstone journal.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 13, the three restorative questions. Today we ask the final
   question: *how do I contribute — with honesty and hope?*
2. **Meet the two traps (8 min).** Write them: **despair** (nothing can be fixed) and **denial**
   (nothing is broken). Both are escapes. The peacemaker's stance holds both truths at once: *here
   is what is broken, and here is what is real to build on.*
3. **Study one real attempt (12 min).** Read about South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation
   Commission: after apartheid, it invited public testimony about past abuses and, in defined
   cases, offered amnesty in exchange for full disclosure, alongside reparations (S-392). Notice
   the honesty (naming the harm in public) and the hope (building a shared future). Notice too that
   it was contested — some said too little punishment, others too little repair. Hold it as a
   question, not a perfect model.
4. **Write the two columns (12 min).** On your sheet, write one **honest** thing (what is broken
   and hard to repair, in your world or the wider one) and one **hopeful** thing (what is real to
   build on — a person, a group, a practice, a place). Keep both true; do not let either erase the
   other.
5. **Name your contribution (12 min).** In the center space, write your contribution — one thing,
   local and one thing, global — that you can actually offer to peace and repair. Make it as
   concrete as you can, and let it grow from both columns.
6. **Gather the capstone (6 min).** Open your journal. Look back over the unit's artifacts — the
   dilemma, the defended position, the values map, the action, the vision, the repair. Write one
   closing line: the kind of citizen you intend to be.
7. **Close (0 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one honest and one
   hopeful thing, and the contribution that joins them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sheet with starters ("One honest thing is…; one hopeful thing is…; my
  contribution locally is…, and globally is…").
- **Extension:** Name one place honesty and hope seem to conflict (for example, telling a hard
  truth that might discourage people), and argue how you would hold both without collapsing into
  despair or denial.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The dates (1996–1998) are
  pre-printed and read aloud; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. The two-column sheet
  is word-based, doable in speech, writing, sign, or gesture.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. The case may be provided in print, braille, or audio description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name an honest and a hopeful truth, and write a
  concrete contribution to peace and repair locally and globally, without collapsing into despair
  or denial (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit capstone):** The completed honesty-and-hope sheet and the closing
  reflection, gathered with the unit's other artifacts into the capstone ethics & civic journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What is one thing that gives you hope for a more peaceful world, and one
thing that still needs repair?" Write both, and add one line on what you could do about the
second.
