# Lesson 1 — Reasoning Through a Real Moral Dilemma

## Summary

Learners meet a real, complex moral dilemma with **no clean answer** — a composite
whistleblower case drawn from many documented situations — and learn a **reasoning frame** for
thinking it through: name the dilemma, list the options, trace who is affected and how, and ask
about **consequences**, **duties**, and the kind of person each choice makes. They practice on a
second dilemma and name **what stays unsettled**, learning that a dilemma is reasoned, not solved,
and that the conclusion is never prescribed.

## Objectives

- Reason through a complex, real-world moral dilemma with no clean answer, weighing multiple
  frameworks and consequences, and defend a nuanced position. _(D09.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Most hard choices are not between right and wrong but between two things that both matter. You
find out a friend has done something harmful — telling the truth hurts them, staying silent lets
the harm continue. You learn that the work that feeds your family is quietly damaging the river
everyone drinks from. In a city, a village, or a household, the same shape appears: every option
leaves something important undone. A real dilemma has no clean answer. The question is not
"which choice is obviously right" but "which reasons and which costs can I stand behind, and what
will I do about the harm I cannot avoid?"

## Materials

- Dilemma reasoning sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: a short written dilemma; scratch paper
- Enriched: two documented, anonymized whistleblower case summaries

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the reasoning sheet; prepare the composite whistleblower case (clearly labeled as a
  composite of many real, anonymized situations), or a local dilemma learners recognize.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D09.S1.11.01), how structures shape the choices people can make; from
  Grades 6–9, moral dilemmas and multiple frameworks. Today we add the *method*: a frame for
  reasoning, not a verdict.
- Prepare the worked example to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a real moral dilemma has no
clean answer, and the mature response is to reason it through — name the dilemma, list the
options, trace who is affected and how, and ask about consequences, duties, and character — then
say honestly what stays unsettled. The frame is a tool, not a verdict machine; the conclusion is
the learner's own, never the lesson's.** Teach the frame explicitly with a worked example (S-011),
then let learners apply it — the reasoning, not the answer, is the learning. Do not supply a
"correct" resolution to any dilemma; hold the question open (inquiry over creed).

The **ethics** lens: this is the heart of the strand — reasoning about what is right when the
right thing is genuinely unclear; three questions recur across traditions: what happens
(consequences), what is owed (duties), and who am I becoming (character) (S-456, S-457, S-458).
The **egalitarianism** lens: in every dilemma, ask *who benefits and who is harmed, and whose
voice is least heard* — a dilemma looks different from the position of the person with least
power to choose. The **global** lens: the shape of a dilemma (loyalty vs. honesty, obedience vs.
conscience, promise vs. harm) recurs across cultures and times, but what counts as a live option
differs with a person's position — the frame is universal, the content is local. The
**technology** lens: the whistleblower case is a technology-shaped dilemma — leaked documents,
surveillance, and instant spread change what "speaking out" costs; reasoning must notice the
tool, not just the choice. The **environment** lens: many of the most painful real dilemmas are
about land, water, and shared resources, where the harm falls on people and places with the
least power to refuse it. Preview: Lesson 2 weighs the three frameworks against each other and
turns the frame into a *defended* position.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades, what makes a choice a *dilemma* — two things that
   matter pulling in opposite directions? Today we meet a dilemma with no clean answer, and a
   frame for reasoning it through.
2. **Meet the frame (8 min).** Write it: a **moral dilemma** is a choice where every option
   leaves something important undone. A **reasoning frame** is a set of questions that keeps you
   honest as you think: *name the dilemma; list the options; who is affected and how; what
   values are at stake; what are the consequences; what is owed (duties); what kind of person
   does each choice make?* Three questions stand behind the frame — **consequences**, **duties**,
   and **character** (S-456, S-457, S-458).
3. **Worked example — the composite whistleblower (15 min).** Read the case: a person inside a
   large organization discovers it is quietly causing real harm — covering up unsafe water,
   overcharging the poorest, or hiding a danger. They can stay silent (protecting their job, their
   family, their friends), speak out publicly (exposing the harm, but risking their work and the
   people around them), or work from within (trying to fix it, but slowly, while the harm
   continues). Work the frame aloud on the sheet: who is affected by each option? what is gained
   and lost in each? what is owed to whom? what kind of person does each choice build? Write
   your answers.
4. **Practice on a second dilemma (15 min).** Choose a second dilemma — one from the lesson's
   list or one from your own life (a friend's secret, a family loyalty, a boss asking you to hide
   a mistake). Run the same frame. Do not try to "solve" it; try to *see* it clearly.
5. **Name what stays unsettled (8 min).** In the bottom box, write what the frame could *not*
   settle for you — which value had to give, which harm you could not avoid. A good reasoner
   names this out loud.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing the
   reasoning frame changed about how you see a hard choice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sheet pre-filled with the composite case's options and affected people; the
  learner underlines who is affected in each option and answers with a starter ("If I stay silent,
  the people harmed are…; if I speak out, the people harmed are…").
- **Extension:** Name the exact value that conflicts with another in your dilemma (for example,
  honesty vs. loyalty) and write one sentence on which you would lean toward and why — without
  pretending the other value stops mattering.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. All names, options, and
  steps are words; nothing must be counted, ordered, or computed. The sheet is pre-printed, and
  every step can be spoken, written, signed, or gestured.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. The dilemma text may be provided in print, braille, or audio
  description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner run the frame on a real dilemma — naming the
  options, who is affected, the consequences, duties, and character — and say honestly what stays
  unsettled, rather than asserting a clean answer?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed reasoning sheet, opened in the capstone ethics &
  civic journal — the strand's first "moral reasoning" artifact.

## Home connection

Bring one dilemma from home or the neighborhood — a choice someone is facing with no clean
answer — and run just the first two questions of the frame on it (name it; list the options).
Write two lines in your journal.
