# Lesson 6 — Defending a Reasoned Position Across Perspectives

## Summary

Learners move from weighing to **defending**: they take a reasoned position on the contested
question of movement and safety, support it with reasons and evidence, and — crucially — **credit
the strongest point** of each perspective they do not fully share (steelmanning) while still
standing. They name what their position cannot settle, learning that a defensible position is firm
and honest at once, not a slogan that pretends the other side has nothing real to say.

## Objectives

- Weigh a real, contested question of justice and rights, locally and globally, and defend a
  reasoned position that accounts for multiple perspectives. _(D09.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

A position worth defending is not a wall; it is a stance that has *looked* at the other side and
still stands. The person who can state their opponent's best argument better than their opponent
can is not weakening their own case — they are proving it has been tested. Defending across
perspectives means taking a stand *and* crediting what the other side genuinely sees, then saying
honestly what even your own position cannot settle. That is what separates a reasoned position
from a slogan.

## Materials

- Position-defense organizer
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a talking object
- Enriched: two opposing opinion pieces

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the organizer; prepare a talking object for circle work.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, the four perspectives and where they conflict. Today we *defend* a
  position across them.
- Model "steelmanning" explicitly (S-011): state the strongest version of the view you disagree
  with, then respond to *that*, not to a weak version of it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **defending a reasoned position
across perspectives means taking a stand, supporting it with reasons and evidence, and crediting
the strongest point of each perspective one does not fully share — then naming what one's own
position cannot settle. A defensible position is firm and honest at once; it is not a slogan that
pretends the other side has nothing real to say.** Model steelmanning (S-011); structure the
deliberation so reasons, not volume, carry the day (S-349). Do not push any one position; the
learner's stand is their own, and the skill is that it *accounts for* the others.

The **ethics** lens: crediting an opponent's strongest point is an act of intellectual honesty — a
duty to truth over the comfort of a neat answer; a position that cannot survive a fair steelman is
not yet well defended. The **egalitarianism** lens: deliberation must not simply reproduce who
already has power — use structures (a talking object, turn-taking, written contributions) so the
quietest and least powerful voices carry equal weight (S-238). The **global** lens: contested
questions of rights are argued differently in different places, and a reasoned position should be
able to say *where* it is rooted and what it assumes — not treat one place's framing as universal
(S-460). The **technology** lens: deliberation increasingly happens on screens, where the loudest
and most extreme voices can drown reasons (S-481, S-482); steelmanning is a deliberate antidote to
an algorithm that feeds you only your own side. The **environment** lens: on questions with an
environmental dimension (who bears the cost of a changing climate, who must move because of it), a
defended position must account for future people and distant places who are not in the room to
argue back. Preview: Lesson 7 turns to the learner's own responsibilities to fairness and justice.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5, the contested question and the four perspectives. Today we
   take a stand — and account for the rest.
2. **Learn to steelman (10 min).** Write the move: **steelmanning** is stating the *strongest*
   version of a view you do not fully share, then responding to that version — not to a weak or
   unfair one. Practice once aloud: give the strongest point of the perspective you find hardest
   to agree with, in one honest sentence.
3. **Write your position (12 min).** On the organizer, write your position and its reasons — the
   evidence and values that support it. A position, not a slogan: "I hold that…, because…."
4. **Credit the others (12 min).** In the second row, write the strongest point of *each*
   perspective you do not fully share, credited honestly — "the receiving community's strongest
   point is…, and it is a real one." Note: crediting is not adopting; you can honor a point and
   still stand.
5. **Name what stays unsettled (8 min).** In the final row, write what your own position cannot
   fully settle — the cost it accepts, the person it still cannot fully answer. A firm position
   that names its own limits is *stronger*, not weaker.
6. **Share and check (6 min).** With a partner, read your position. Ask: *does it state reasons,
   credit the strongest opposing point, and name what stays unsettled?* Give one specific
   suggestion.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing crediting
   the other side changed about your own position.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-printed organizer with sentence starters for each row ("I hold that…,
  because…"; "the strongest point of the view I disagree with is…"; "what I still cannot settle
  is…").
- **Extension:** Write the strongest *objection* to your own position from one perspective, then
  respond to it — and say whether the response fully succeeds or leaves a remainder.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. Any evidence is offered on a
  pre-printed organizer and read aloud; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. The whole
  defense can be done in words, spoken, written, or signed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — stating, reading aloud, steelmanning, discussing,
  or closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound. Materials may be provided in print, braille,
  or audio description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner take a reasoned position, support it with reasons
  and evidence, credit the strongest point of each opposing perspective, and name what their
  position cannot settle (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed defense organizer, added to the capstone ethics &
  civic journal.

## Home connection

Choose a disagreement you actually have at home or with a friend. Write one sentence stating
their strongest point as fairly as you can — then one sentence for your own position, crediting
theirs.
