# Lesson 4 — Testing My Values in the World

## Summary

Learners turn one of their values into a testable prediction: they name a value, a real situation
that will test it, what they predict they will do, and a plan to record what actually happens.
They learn to treat the result as **evidence to reflect on** — revising or confirming the value —
rather than as a settled verdict on who they are, holding the question of what they value open as
it meets the world.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own evolving values, how they formed, and how they will test them in the world,
  holding questions open rather than settling them as creed. _(D09.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

A value is only a wish until it meets a cost. "I value honesty" is easy to say and easy to hold —
until telling the truth costs a friendship. "I value fairness" is tested the day sharing means you
get less. A value is not proven by being *stated*; it is tested by being *spent*. The test is not
a pass-or-fail exam on your character. It is a way to learn what you actually believe, and to
revise it honestly when the world shows you something new.

## Materials

- Value-testing sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a calendar or notebook
- Enriched: a personal essay on a value tested under pressure

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the testing sheet; prepare the four columns (value, situation, prediction, what
  happened) and a final "what I learned / what I will revise" row.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, the values map and one value marked "still forming." Today we take
  one value and *test* it.
- Emphasize: the test is for learning, not for grading the self; a value that "fails" a test is
  information, never a verdict (mastery, not ranking).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a value is tested, not
settled; the honest way to test it is to predict what you will do in a real situation, record
what actually happens, and treat the gap between prediction and result as evidence to learn from
— revising or confirming the value while holding the question open rather than closing it as a
creed.** This mirrors the scientific habit of distinguishing evidence from belief, applied to
character (philosophy §5). Guard against shame: a learner who does *not* live up to a stated value
has gained data, not a failing grade; frame every gap as information.

The **ethics** lens: testing a value is the move from *holding* an opinion to *practicing* a
commitment — and practice, not declaration, is what virtue ethics says builds character (S-458).
The **egalitarianism** lens: which values can be tested freely is not equal — a learner with
little power may find "courage" costs more than it does for one with much; the lesson honors that
a test is harder for some, not that some people are better. The **global** lens: the same value —
hospitality, honesty, restraint — is tested in different situations in different places; a test
designed in a city looks unlike one designed on a farm or a boat, and both are real. The
**technology** lens: screens create new test-sites for old values — kindness in a comment thread,
honesty about what you share, restraint with what you click (S-481, S-482); a value is tested
where you actually live, and part of that life is digital. The **environment** lens: some of the
clearest tests are about care for shared things — water, food, a shared space — where restraint
and fairness become concrete, daily, and observable. Preview: Lesson 5 turns from the self to a
contested question of justice and rights.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, your values map. Choose *one* value — ideally one you
   marked "still forming" or "chosen." Today we test it.
2. **Write the prediction (10 min).** On your sheet, name the value. Then name a *real* situation
   in the coming days that will test it — a moment, not a hypothetical. Write what you *predict*
   you will do and why.
3. **Plan the record (8 min).** Decide how you will record what actually happens: a note that
   evening, a checkmark in a calendar, a voice memo, a drawing. A test without a record is only a
   guess.
4. **Run it (over the coming days).** Between now and the next lesson, let the situation happen.
   Do not force it; notice what you actually did.
5. **Read the evidence (10 min, next session).** When the moment has come and gone, write what
   *actually happened* in the fourth column. Compare it with your prediction. Where they match,
   the value is being practiced; where they differ, you have learned something real.
6. **Revise or confirm (10 min).** Write in the final row: what the test taught you, and whether
   you will confirm the value, revise it, or hold it open a little longer. A question still open
   is a value still being lived (S-001).
7. **Close (4 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing testing a
   value taught you that *saying* it could not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Choose a small, low-stakes test (one honest moment, one act of care) with a
  pre-printed record prompt; the learner fills in "I predicted… / it happened…" with a starter.
- **Extension:** Test two values in the same situation and write where they pull against each
  other, and which one held under the actual cost.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The sheet is a four-column
  organizer in words; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. Recording can be a checkmark,
  a drawing, a voice memo, or a single word — no numerals required.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner design a real, recordable test of one value,
  compare prediction with what actually happened, and name what they learned — revising or
  confirming without turning a gap into a verdict on themselves?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed testing sheet, added to the capstone ethics &
  civic journal.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home the value you are testing and the situation that will test it. Ask them to
simply *notice* — and, at the end of the week, compare one line: what you predicted, and what
happened.
