# Lesson 13 — Listening, Taking Responsibility, Repairing

## Summary

Learners practice the habits of a peacemaker — **listening** to the person harmed, **taking
responsibility** for harm, and **repairing** it — using a restorative-practice frame of three
questions: *what happened, who was harmed and how, and what will make it right*. They distinguish
conflict (disagreement) from violence, and accountability from punishment, and role-play a real or
role-played harm in a circle, learning that repair is a practice of listening and responsibility,
never retaliation or coercion.

## Objectives

- Practice the habits of peacemaking by listening across deep disagreement, taking responsibility
  for harm, and repairing relationships and communities. _(D09.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

When harm happens, the easy reflexes are blame and payback — but payback usually leaves the wound
open and adds a new one. A peacemaker does something harder: first *listen* to the person harmed,
then *own* the part that is yours, then work out what would actually *make it right*. This is not
weakness; it is a discipline. It is how a friendship, a family, or a neighborhood mends instead of
breaking further — and it is a set of skills anyone can practice, starting in the small harms of
everyday life.

## Materials

- Restorative practice card
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: a talking object; chairs or stones in a circle
- Enriched: a short restorative-justice case summary

## Preparation

- Copy the restorative practice card; set chairs (or stones) in a circle; prepare the talking
  object.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D09.S4.11.01), analyzing conflict from multiple perspectives; from
  earlier grades, active listening, "I" statements, and repair over punishment. Today we practice
  the full peacemaking sequence.
- Hold the frame accurately: restorative practice responds to harm by repairing it and restoring
  relationships rather than only punishing (S-022, S-161); conflict (disagreement) is distinct
  from violence (S-462).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **peacemaking is a discipline of
three moves — listen to the person harmed, take responsibility for the harm, and repair it — and
it is practiced in a circle with a talking object, through three questions: what happened, who was
harmed and how, and what will make it right. Repair is never retaliation and never coercion;
accountability (owning the harm) is different from punishment.** Model the three questions on a
small, low-stakes worked example first (S-011); then let learners role-play a *role-played or
mildly remembered* harm — never a serious or traumatic one, and never one that would re-harm a
learner in the room. Hold the circle as a safe structure; the talking object guarantees turn and
quiet.

The **ethics** lens: taking responsibility for harm is the strand's central moral act — owning
what is yours, without being forced — and the lesson treats accountability as a gift one *offers*,
not a penalty one *suffers*. The **egalitarianism** lens: the circle equalizes — every voice gets
the same turn, the same talking object, the same standing, so the person with the most power
cannot simply out-talk the person who was harmed (S-022). The **global** lens: restorative
practice has deep roots in Indigenous peacemaking traditions — circles that aim to restore
balance and harmony rather than merely punish — which the field itself acknowledges (S-161); the
lesson honors that lineage rather than presenting repair as one culture's invention. The
**technology** lens: much harm today happens through screens — a cruel post, a leaked image — and
repair must ask what "making it right" means when the harm is digital and the wound is public; the
same three questions still apply, but the repair may mean taking something down, not just saying
something. The **environment** lens: harm to a shared place — a fouled water source, a damaged
common space — is harm to a *relationship* with the land and the people who use it, and repair can
mean restoring the place, not just apologizing to the people. Preview: Lesson 14 reflects on
contributing to peace and repair with honesty and hope.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades, active listening and "I" statements. Today we put
   them into a peacemaker's full sequence: listen, take responsibility, repair.
2. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** Write the restorative frame: **what happened? who was
   harmed, and how? what will make it right?** (S-022, S-161). Note what it is *not*: not "who is
   to blame and how do we pay them back." Repair, not retaliation.
3. **Separate two pairs of ideas (8 min).** Write them: **conflict** (disagreement) is not
   **violence** — disagreements can be healthy when handled well (S-462). And **accountability**
   (owning what you did) is not **punishment** (making someone suffer). The peacemaker works in
   the first of each pair.
4. **Worked example (10 min).** Listen to a small, low-stakes example of harm (a broken promise,
   a cruel word, a shared thing broken). Run the three questions on it aloud: what happened? who
   was harmed and how? what would make it right?
5. **Practice in a circle (15 min).** In your circle, take a *role-played or mildly remembered*
   harm — never a serious or traumatic one. Pass the talking object. Each person, in turn, speaks
   to the three questions. The person harmed speaks first, and is heard; the person responsible
   owns their part without being forced; together you name what repair would require.
6. **Close (7 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing the
   restorative frame did that blame-and-payback does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-written role-play with the three questions printed as starters ("What
  happened was…; the person harmed was…; what would make it right is…"); the learner fills one
  line each.
- **Extension:** Distinguish accountability from punishment in your own words, and write one
  reason a restorative process can sometimes work better than punishment alone — and one place it
  is genuinely hard.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The three questions and the
  circle are word- and turn-based; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. Every step can
  be done in speech, writing, sign, or gesture.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, speaking in the circle, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; the talking
  object may be passed to a written or signed turn, and no step requires producing or hearing
  sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner work the three restorative questions — listening to
  who was harmed, taking responsibility, and naming repair — while distinguishing conflict from
  violence and accountability from punishment (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** A short reflection on the practiced repair, opened in the
  capstone ethics & civic journal — the strand's "peacemaking practice" artifact.

## Home connection

The next time a small harm happens at home or nearby, try the three questions once — what
happened, who was harmed and how, what would make it right — and write two lines on what changed.
