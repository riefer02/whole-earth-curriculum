# Lesson 10 — Acting Collectively for a Fairer Common Life

## Summary

Learners design and carry out a small collective action for a fairer common life: a goal, allies,
concrete steps, a clear-eyed look at the likely effects on people and place, and a way to know if
it helped. They draw on **mutual-aid and cooperative traditions** from across cultures (harambee,
gotong royong, mink'a, barn-raising, cooperatives) as living evidence that people have always
improved the common good by acting together — and they plan one real step they can actually take.

## Objectives

- Practice the habits of an engaged citizen by researching an issue, building coalitions,
  listening across disagreement, and acting collectively toward a fairer common life.
  _(D09.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

Everywhere people live, there is a common life to tend — a shared path, a water source, a
market, a school, a neighborhood. And everywhere, people have found the same way to improve it:
do it together. When a family in Kenya calls *harambee* ("all pull together") to build a school,
when neighbors in Indonesia join in *gotong royong* to raise a hall, when Andean communities work
a field by *mink'a*, they are practicing something universal: the common good is built by common
hands. Designing a collective action is just giving that old practice a plan.

## Materials

- Collective-action design sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: blank paper; a shared local resource to improve
- Enriched: a planning tool or shared document

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the design sheet; identify a shared local resource the group could actually improve
  (a common space, a shared supply, a notice board).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, the three habits. Today we combine them into one designed action.
- Prepare the mutual-aid examples accurately: harambee (Kenyan self-help), gotong royong
  (Indonesian mutual assistance), mink'a/minga (Andean communal work), and barn-raising are real,
  documented traditions of communal work (S-164); cooperatives are a worldwide form of shared
  ownership (S-486).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a collective action for a
fairer common life is designed, not improvised — a goal, allies, concrete steps, a clear-eyed look
at the likely effects on people and place, and a way to know if it helped. People have always
built the common good by acting together (mutual-aid traditions and cooperatives), and the
learner's small action joins that old, worldwide pattern.** Keep the action real and small enough
to actually do; the learning is in the doing and the honest look at effects, not in the scale.
Emphasize cooperation over competition (S-147, S-233): success is collective.

The **ethics** lens: acting for the common good is the strand's central moral move — from judging
what is fair to *making* something fairer, and then checking honestly whether it worked. The
**egalitarianism** lens: mutual aid is not charity — it is people meeting needs *together*, as
equals, sharing both the giving and the deciding (S-345); the lesson designs actions that build
power together rather than handing it down. The **global** lens: the mutual-aid traditions named —
harambee, gotong royong, mink'a, barn-raising — are real and worldwide (S-164), and cooperatives
span the globe as shared ownership (S-486, S-487); the learner's action is one local instance of a
human universal. The **technology** lens: tools (a shared document, a group chat) can coordinate an
action across distance, but they can also exclude people who are not online — design must ask who
is left out of the tool. The **environment** lens: the most tangible common goods are often
environmental — a clean common space, a shared water supply, a green corner — where acting
collectively for fairness and for the Earth are the same act (S-416). Preview: Lesson 11 reflects
on the learner's own role in public life.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9, the three habits. Today we combine them into one designed
   action.
2. **Meet the pattern (8 min).** Write it: people everywhere improve the common good by acting
   together — *harambee* in Kenya, *gotong royong* in Indonesia, *mink'a* in the Andes,
   barn-raising, and cooperatives the world over (S-164, S-486). The common good is built by
   common hands.
3. **Name the goal (8 min).** On the design sheet, choose one small, real improvement to a shared
   thing — a common space, a shared supply, a rule that could be fairer. Write the goal as a
   single clear sentence.
4. **List the allies (8 min).** Write who will do it with you — the people who use or care about
   that common thing, and any group already working there. Name the one shared thing everyone can
   agree on, even if they differ on much else.
5. **Plan the steps and the effects (12 min).** Write the concrete steps in order. Then look
   clearly at the *likely effects* — on people and on the place — including any effect you did not
   intend. Honesty about effects is part of the action, not a reason to skip it.
6. **Decide how you will know (8 min).** Write one simple way to tell whether it helped — what
   you will look at afterward, and when.
7. **Close (6 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one step you could
   actually take this week with at least one other person.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a design sheet with the five boxes and starters ("Our goal is…; our allies
  are…; our first step is…; it might also affect…; we will know it helped if…").
- **Extension:** Anticipate one unintended effect and write how you will check for it honestly,
  and what you would adjust if it appears.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. Ordering the steps is a
  sequence of words, not numerals; nothing must be counted or computed. The sheet is a word
  organizer, doable in speech, writing, sign, or gesture.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, planning, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step
  requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner design a small collective action — goal, allies,
  steps, likely effects, and a way to know if it helped — and name one step they can actually take
  with others (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed design sheet, added to the capstone ethics & civic
  journal.

## Home connection

With one or two people at home or nearby, do one small thing together for a shared space this
week, and write two lines afterward: what you did, and how you could tell whether it helped.
