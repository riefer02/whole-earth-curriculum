# Lesson 5 — Weighing a Contested Question of Justice and Rights

## Summary

Learners weigh a real, contested question of justice and rights — **the right to move and seek
safety, weighed against a state's control of its borders** — locally and globally. They map four
perspectives (the person seeking safety, the receiving community, the sending country, and
international law), name the value each appeals to, and separate **evidence** about displacement
from **values** about what should happen. No single verdict is asserted; the question is weighed,
and the conflicts are named honestly.

## Objectives

- Weigh a real, contested question of justice and rights, locally and globally, and defend a
  reasoned position that accounts for multiple perspectives. _(D09.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Almost every community faces the same tension in some form: people need to move — for safety, for
work, for a future — and the places they arrive already have people, rules, and scarce resources.
One voice says *every person deserves safety and a chance*; another says *a community must be able
to decide who comes in*. Both speak for something real. A contested question of justice is not
solved by declaring one side wrong; it is weighed, so the real conflict — between dignity and
belonging, between openness and self-rule — can be seen and argued honestly.

## Materials

- Contested-justice weighing sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: four perspective cards; scratch paper
- Enriched: two short primary sources

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the weighing sheet; prepare four perspective cards in plain language.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, weighing and holding a question open; from Grade 11 (D09.S2.11.01),
  justice, power, and law. Today we weigh a *contested* question of rights across perspectives.
- Prepare the evidence side carefully: displacement is large and global, driven mainly by conflict
  and persecution (S-493); migration has been part of human life throughout history (S-086).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a contested question of justice
and rights — here, the right to move and seek safety weighed against a state's control of its
borders — is weighed, not settled; the work is to map the perspectives (the person seeking safety,
the receiving community, the sending country, international law), name the value each appeals to,
and separate evidence about displacement from values about what should be done. No single verdict
is asserted.** Hold the facts accurately: the Universal Declaration affirms a right to seek and
enjoy asylum (Articles 13–14) (S-149); forced displacement is large and global, driven mainly by
conflict and persecution, with climate a growing driver (S-493); people have always moved (S-086).
Present every perspective as genuine; the learner weighs and judges.

The **ethics** lens: the question is a genuine clash of *duties* — a duty to the stranger in
danger and a duty to one's own community — and ethical reasoning means holding both, not choosing
the easier one to dismiss. The **egalitarianism** lens: ask *who has the power to be heard* in this
debate and whose voice is loudest or quietest; a state speaks with institutions behind it, a
person fleeing often speaks with none — weighing fairly means noticing that imbalance (S-275).
The **global** lens: the right to move and seek asylum is framed in global documents (S-149), yet
the African Charter adds *duties* and *peoples'* rights to the picture (S-465), showing the idea
of rights is amended and extended across traditions, not simply accepted. The **technology** lens:
how movement is governed is now mediated by technology — borders tracked by data, identity by
documents and databases — and who is included or excluded is partly decided by systems, not only
by people. The **environment** lens: some movement is *driven* by a changing climate — drought,
flood, lost harvests — which makes the question one about shared responsibility for a shared
Earth, not only about borders (S-493). Preview: Lesson 6 asks the learner to *defend* a reasoned
position across these perspectives.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 1–2, how we weigh and hold a question open. Today we aim that
   skill at a contested question of justice and rights.
2. **Name the contested question (8 min).** Write it: *people need to move and seek safety; states
   claim the right to control their borders.* Both are claims about justice and rights. A
   **contested question** is one where reasonable people, appealing to real values, disagree.
3. **Meet the four perspectives (15 min).** Read the four cards: the **person seeking safety**
   (dignity, survival, a future); the **receiving community** (belonging, resources, self-rule);
   the **sending country** (loss of people, or relief of pressure, or duty to its own); and
   **international law** (the right to seek asylum, S-149; the duty of states to their own people).
   On your sheet, write what each perspective adds and the value it appeals to.
4. **Separate evidence from values (10 min).** Mark each claim as *evidence* (what the data show:
   displacement is large and driven mainly by conflict and persecution, S-493; people have always
   moved, S-086) or *value* (what should be done about it). They are different, and honest weighing
   keeps them distinct.
5. **Name the conflicts (10 min).** In the bottom row, write where the perspectives *conflict*
   directly and where they *overlap* (for example: everyone prefers safety; they disagree about
   whose duty it is to provide it).
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing weighing
   four perspectives adds that picking one side does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sheet with the four perspectives pre-summarized; the learner underlines the
  value each appeals to, then answers with a starter ("The person seeking safety appeals to…; the
  receiving community appeals to…").
- **Extension:** Name the strongest point made by the perspective you are *least* inclined to
  agree with, and write why it is genuinely strong — without surrendering your own position.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** Any figures about displacement are offloaded to a pre-printed
  organizer and read aloud; nothing must be computed, ordered, or compared numerically. The whole
  weighing can be done in words, spoken, written, or signed, with no numerals required.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. Cards may be provided in print, braille, or audio description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner weigh a contested question of justice and rights by
  mapping the perspectives, naming the value each appeals to, separating evidence from values, and
  stating where they conflict — without asserting one easy verdict?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed weighing sheet, opened in the capstone ethics &
  civic journal — the strand's "contested justice" artifact.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how your own community has weighed "who may come in" or "who belongs" — at a
border, a neighborhood, a school, or a family. Write two lines: what they appealed to, and what
you noticed.
