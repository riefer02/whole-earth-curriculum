# Lesson 2 — Weighing Frameworks and Defending a Nuanced Position

## Summary

Learners weigh three ethical frameworks — **consequentialism** (what happens), **deontology**
(what is owed), and **virtue ethics** (who I become) — against each other, seeing that these
lenses appear across world traditions, not only in one. They apply all three to a dilemma, notice
where the lenses agree and where they conflict, and write a **defended, nuanced position** that
credits more than one framework and names what each leaves out — reasoning toward a position,
never asserting a slogan.

## Objectives

- Reason through a complex, real-world moral dilemma with no clean answer, weighing multiple
  frameworks and consequences, and defend a nuanced position. _(D09.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

The same hard choice can look different depending on the question you ask. "What will cause the
least harm?" points one way; "what do I owe, no matter what?" points another; "what kind of
person am I becoming?" points a third. A person deciding whether to tell a difficult truth, keep
a promise, or break a rule is living all three at once. Weighing frameworks means holding more
than one question at the same time — and refusing to flatten a hard choice into a one-line slogan.

## Materials

- Frameworks weighing sheet
- Capstone ethics & civic journal
- Low-tech: three framework cards; scratch paper
- Enriched: short excerpts from three ethical traditions

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the weighing sheet; prepare three framework cards (one guiding question each).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the reasoning frame and the three recurring questions. Today we weigh
  the questions *against each other* and defend a position.
- Prepare the worked example to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the three lenses —
consequences, duties, and character — are not competitors to rank; they are questions to weigh
together. A nuanced position is one that reasons with more than one lens, credits what the others
see, and names what it cannot fully settle. The lenses appear across world traditions — they are
not the property of one culture — and none is offered as the correct answer.** Teach the weighing
explicitly (S-011); the defense is the learning. Do not rank the frameworks for the learner; a
position is the learner's own, defended with reasons.

The **ethics** lens: consequentialism judges by results (S-456, S-386); deontology by duty and
respect for persons (S-457, S-387); virtue ethics by the kind of person an act builds (S-458).
The **global** lens is essential here: consequentialist reasoning is as old as Chinese Mohism's
inclusive care (S-466) and Buddhist concern to reduce suffering for all beings (S-467); duty-based
reasoning runs through Confucian role ethics (S-468) and Hindu and Buddhist accounts of dharma
(S-469). Holding these side by side models that ethics is a shared human work, not one tradition's
invention. The **egalitarianism** lens: ask which lens is easiest to speak *from power* — a
"consequences" calculation can quietly discount the people who are hardest to see, which is why
the duty lens insists every person counts, and why the virtue lens asks who a habit of overlooking
them turns you into. The **technology** lens: new tools sharpen old dilemmas — a data platform can
calculate consequences at a scale no single conscience can check, so weighing frameworks is also a
guard against letting a machine settle a question that needs all three lenses. The
**environment** lens: environmental choices are exactly where consequences (for future people,
for other species), duties (to a river, a forest, a generation not yet born), and character (a
habit of care or of waste) must be weighed together, because no single lens is enough.
Preview: Lesson 3 turns inward — how the learner's own values formed.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, the three recurring questions: consequences, duties,
   character. Today we name them as *frameworks* and weigh them against each other.
2. **Meet the three frameworks (10 min).** Write them. **Consequentialism** judges an act by its
   results — what happens to everyone affected (S-456). **Deontology** judges by duty — what is
   owed regardless of results, treating persons as ends, never merely as means (S-457).
   **Virtue ethics** judges by character — what kind of person the act builds, practiced until it
   becomes habit (S-458). Note: these are questions, not verdicts, and they appear across
   traditions (S-466, S-467, S-468, S-469).
3. **Worked example — one dilemma, three lenses (15 min).** Return to the composite whistleblower
   case from Lesson 1. Run each lens on it: what would the consequences lens notice? the duty
   lens? the virtue lens? Write each on your sheet. Mark where two lenses *agree* and where they
   *conflict*.
4. **Write a nuanced position (15 min).** Write a defended position: a claim, supported by the
   strongest reasons from *more than one* lens, crediting what the other lens adds, and naming
   what your position still cannot settle. Do not assert a slogan; *argue* a position.
5. **Share and check (8 min).** With a partner, read your position. Ask: *does it use more than
   one lens, credit the other side, and name what stays unsettled?* Give one specific suggestion.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing weighing
   three frameworks adds that a single framework misses.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sheet pre-filled with one line per lens on the case; the learner circles
  where the lenses conflict, then writes the position with a starter ("Weighing consequences and
  duty together, I lean toward…, because…, though I cannot settle…").
- **Extension:** Name a place where two lenses *directly contradict* each other and argue, in a
  paragraph, which should carry more weight in *this* dilemma and why — without pretending the
  other lens stops mattering.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The three frameworks, the
  case, and the position are all in words; nothing must be counted, computed, or ordered. The
  sheet is pre-printed, and the whole weighing can be done in speech, writing, sign, or gesture.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, sharing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step
  requires producing or hearing sound. Excerpts may be provided in print, braille, or audio
  description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner weigh consequences, duties, and character on one
  dilemma, name where they conflict, and defend a nuanced position that credits more than one
  lens and states what stays unsettled (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed weighing sheet with the defended position, added to
  the capstone ethics & civic journal.

## Home connection

Take one current dilemma in the news or your neighborhood and ask it three questions out loud to
someone at home: what would happen, what is owed, and what kind of person does each choice make?
Write one line on where the three answers pull apart.
