# Lesson 9 — Who's In, Who's Out, and Why

## Summary

Learners analyze how belonging draws a line between "us" and "them" — often
unintentionally — and weigh loyalty to the in-group against obligation to those left
outside. They meet the **minimal-group finding** that even arbitrary groups trigger
in-group favoritism, analyze real cases of inclusion and exclusion, and argue how the
line could be redrawn.

## Objectives

- Weigh belonging, loyalty, and collective responsibility, analyzing who a community
  draws in and who it leaves out. _(D02.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

Every "we" has an edge. A nation has a border; a club has a membership; even a friend
group has an inside and an outside. Often the line is drawn without anyone deciding to be
cruel — it just forms, and people on the far side of it quietly pay a cost. Research
found something striking: even when people are sorted into groups by *nothing* — a coin
flip, a made-up label — they start favoring their own group within minutes. That means
the "us/them" line is not only built by bad intentions; it is built by habit. Naming the
line — and who pays it — is the first step toward drawing it more fairly.

## Materials

- Belonging-line organizer
- Community case cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- In-group and out-group page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the belonging-line organizer; prepare the case cards, kept factual.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 8, the nested rings; from Grade 6 (D02.S3.06.01), how belonging
  can include and exclude. Today we weigh the line.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): an
  **in-group** is the "us"; an **out-group** is the "them." **In-group favoritism** is
  the tendency to favor our own group even when the group is arbitrary — the minimal-group
  finding (Tajfel, S-232). **Solidarity** and **collective responsibility** are the
  question: what do I owe the people my "we" leaves out?

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **belonging has a line,
often drawn by habit rather than malice, and maturity means seeing the line and weighing
loyalty against obligation to those outside it.** The minimal-group finding is real and
well replicated (Tajfel, S-232); hold it as description of how minds work, not as a claim
that groups are bad or that belonging is wrong. Belonging is a human good; the question is
*whose* belonging is paid for by *whose* exclusion.

The **ethics** lens: loyalty to "us" is a real value — and so is the claim of the person
standing outside the line; ethics is the weighing. The **egalitarianism** lens: the line
is not neutral — it is drawn by the powerful and paid by the excluded; noticing who draws
and who pays is the egalitarian move. The **global** lens: every human group draws lines
differently — by kinship, language, faith, place, or state — and no single way is the
default. The **technology** lens: online communities draw lines too — algorithmic sorting,
moderation, and gatekeeping can make an "us" feel seamless while quietly excluding people
whose access, language, or ability differs (World Bank digital divide, S-281; Britannica,
S-481). The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *who drew this line, when, and for what
purpose* before you accept it as natural. Preview: Lesson 10 turns the weighing into a
decision about solidarity and the common good.

**Safeguarding:** cases are about groups, never a learner's identity; no one is asked to
defend exclusion of a group they or their loved ones belong to.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: your nested rings. Today we look at the *edges* of
   a ring — where "we" stops.
2. **Meet the minimal-group finding (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): people
   sorted into two arbitrary groups — by a coin flip — soon favor their own group in small
   choices (Tajfel, S-232). Name the takeaway: the us/them line forms by habit, not only
   by cruelty.
3. **Analyze a real line (15 min).** In a group, take a case card. On the organizer, name
   who the community draws *in*, who it leaves *out*, and what the line costs — in
   dignity, access, or opportunity — and who pays it.
4. **Weigh loyalty against obligation (12 min).** Argue — aloud or in writing: what does
   this "we" owe the people outside its line — nothing, something, or a great deal? Use
   one example from the case, and be ready to say where the obligation ends and why.
5. **Redraw the line (8 min).** In your care journal, draw the line as it is, then draw it
   as it *could* be — wider, lower, or gone — and write one sentence on what the change
   would cost and what it would buy.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture) why seeing the line is
   different from pretending it is not there — and which one lets you draw it more fairly.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one "we" and one person it leaves out, and one thing the "we" could
  do to include them.
- **Extension:** Investigate one real community that redrew its line (a country, a club, a
  platform), and write a short account of who gained and who lost.
- **Communication access:** Arguing from each side of the line can be spoken, written,
  signed, typed, gestured, or AAC; a written or signed position is complete. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users complete every step in their preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name in-group/out-group and the minimal
  finding, analyze who a real community includes and excludes, and weigh loyalty against
  obligation with reasons?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The belonging-line organizer with the redrawn line, in
  the care journal.

## Home connection

Notice one "we" in your own life — a team, a friend group, a household — and ask who sits
at its edge. Write one sentence on what you noticed and one on what could change.
