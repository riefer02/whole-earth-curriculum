# Lesson 6 — Many Views, One Problem

## Summary

Learners deliberately take the perspectives of people they disagree with about one
contested shared problem, and let that change their reasoning. They fill a **perspective
matrix** — what each stakeholder needs, fears, and is fairly asked — then **"steelman"**
the view they least agree with, and revise their own position in its light.

## Objectives

- Take multiple perspectives, including ones I disagree with, and integrate them into
  ethical reasoning about a shared problem. _(D02.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

When a problem has many sides — a dam that powers a city but floods a valley, a road that
connects markets but cuts a forest, a factory that brings jobs but fouls a river — our
first instinct is to line up behind the side that already matches our gut. But the
strongest reasoning does something harder: it tries on the views it does not like, and
says them as their best, not their worst, before answering. That is called *steelmanning*,
and it is how your conclusion earns the right to be trusted — because it survived
meeting its rivals fairly.

## Materials

- Perspective matrix
- Shared-problem briefs
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Perspective-taking and bias page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the perspective matrix; prepare the shared-problem briefs, each with five
  stakeholders who genuinely disagree.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, the two columns (inside/outside); from Grade 8
  (D02.S2.08.01), analyzing a conflict from multiple perspectives. Today we steelman.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011):
  **perspective-taking** is deliberately seeing a situation as another sees it (Decety &
  Cowell, S-231). A **steelman** is the strongest, fairest version of a view you reject —
  the opposite of a strawman. **Bias** here means the tendency to over-trust the view we
  already hold.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **taking a rival view at
its strongest — not its weakest — is a discipline that makes reasoning more honest, and it
is a learnable skill, not a gift.** Perspective-taking is well evidenced as a route to
better judgment and prosocial behavior (Decety & Cowell, S-231); our first views are
biased toward themselves (Banaji & Greenwald on implicit bias — see Resources). Hold this
as description of how minds work, not as a claim that every view is equally right.

The **ethics** lens: fairness to a view is not agreeing with it; it is the minimum respect
reasoning owes the people who hold it. The **egalitarianism** lens: which stakeholders we
are willing to steelman is itself a choice — the powerful are easy to imagine, the quiet
and the poor and the more-than-human are easy to skip. The **global** lens: the same
problem (a dam, a road, a land use) plays out differently in a river valley on each
continent; the method travels, the details differ. The **technology** lens: media and
feeds decide which rival views you even see, and algorithms can hand you only strawmen of
the other side (Britannica, S-481) — steelmanning is a counter to that. The
**critical-thinking** lens: distinguish *understanding* a view from *endorsing* it, and
show both in your writing. Preview: Lesson 7 extends perspective-taking beyond humans, to
the more-than-human world.

**Safeguarding:** keep disagreements about the problem (water, land, jobs), never about a
learner's identity or private life; no one is made to argue against their own safety or
dignity.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: the two columns of reasoning. Today we make the
   "inside" column *wide* — many views, not just the one we already hold.
2. **Meet steelmanning (8 min).** Work a model example (S-011): in a valley, a dam would
   power homes but flood farmland and a fish run. A strawman of the farmers is "they're
   just resisting progress." A steelman is "the river *is* their livelihood and their
   home; asking them to trade it for distant electricity is a real loss." Name the
   difference.
3. **Fill the perspective matrix (15 min).** In a group, take a shared-problem brief. For
   each of the five stakeholders, write what they *need*, what they *fear*, and what is
   *fair to ask of them* — including the stakeholder you would normally skip.
4. **Steelman the rival view (12 min).** Choose the view you least agree with. In first
   person, deliver its strongest case for two minutes — speak, write, sign, or type it as
   if it were your own. Then, without mocking it, write one sentence it gets *right*.
5. **Revise your position (8 min).** In your care journal, rewrite your own position,
   now naming the one thing the rival view changed in it. If nothing changed, say (or
   write/sign) why — honestly.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture) why a conclusion that has
   met its rivals fairly is stronger than one that has not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill "what they need" and "what they fear" for two stakeholders, and say
  (or write/sign) one thing the other side gets right.
- **Extension:** Write a two-paragraph essay that presents the rival view at full
  strength, then answers it — and have a partner check that your steelman is genuinely
  strong, not a strawman in disguise.
- **Communication access:** Steelmanning may be spoken, written, signed, typed,
  gestured, or AAC; a written or signed first-person steelman counts fully. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users deliver and receive each view in their
  preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner fill the matrix for all stakeholders,
  steelman a rival view in first person (aloud or in writing/sign), and show what it
  changed in their own position?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed matrix and the revised position, in the
  care journal.

## Home connection

Pick a disagreement you have had at home or with a friend, and write the strongest
version of the *other* person's view before you respond again. Write one sentence on what
it changed.
