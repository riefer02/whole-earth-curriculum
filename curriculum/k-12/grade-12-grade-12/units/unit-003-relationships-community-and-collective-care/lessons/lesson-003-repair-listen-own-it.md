# Lesson 3 — Repair: Listen, Own It, Make It Right

## Summary

Learners practice real repair after harm in a close relationship, using a restorative
**three-question frame — what happened, who was harmed and how, how do we make it right**
(IIRP, S-022). They distinguish repair from a quick apology or a forced "sorry," work a
model example, and draft a repair conversation for a fictional harm, then connect repair
to the value of mending rather than discarding.

## Objectives

- Sustain an intimate, trusting relationship through repair after harm, using
  accountability, listening, and making it right. _(D02.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Every close relationship will be damaged at some point — not because it is broken, but
because it is alive. What separates relationships that survive harm from those that
quietly end is not the absence of harm but the presence of repair. A real repair is more
than "sorry": it names what happened, hears how the other was hurt, and does something
to make it right. This lesson practices that, because the relationships worth keeping are
the ones we know how to mend.

## Materials

- Repair conversation frame
- Harm-and-repair scenario cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Restorative practice page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the repair conversation frame; prepare the scenario cards, kept fictional
  and gentle.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, the four moves of honest talk; from Grade 7 (D02.S1.07.02),
  apologizing, listening, and agreeing on next steps. Today we deepen it into full
  repair.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): **repair**
  is restoring a relationship after harm by taking **accountability** (owning what
  happened without excuses), **listening** (hearing how the other was harmed), and
  **restitution** (doing something concrete to make it right). This is the restorative
  frame (IIRP, S-022): what happened, who was harmed, how do we make it right.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **repair is not a word
but a set of actions — own it, hear the harm, make it right — and it restores rather than
humiliates.** The restorative three-question frame is a well-established practice (IIRP,
S-022). Hold repair as a description of what works, and hold the value that people can
change and relationships can mend as a commitment, not a fact.

The **ethics** lens: what we owe after harm — truth, hearing, and making right — is the
core of restorative ethics. The **egalitarianism** lens: repair treats both people as
equals with needs; it neither punishes downward nor lets harm stand unanswered. The
**global** lens: cultures worldwide practice repair — truth-telling circles, peacemaking
traditions, and restorative justice rooted in Indigenous practice; the frame is one
modern form, not the only one. The **technology** lens: digital life complicates repair —
a public apology can feel performative, an "unsent" message erases no harm, and a private
repair is often the honest one (Suler, S-343). The **environment** lens: a throwaway
culture throws away broken things and broken ties alike; the value of *mending* — a
repaired bowl, a rewoven net, a restored streambank — is the same value that sustains
relationships (and it echoes the circular-economy ideal of repairing rather than
discarding). Preview: Lesson 4 turns to the listener we each want to become.

**Safeguarding:** all scenarios are fictional. No learner is asked to narrate a real
private harm; they may always use a character.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what are the four moves of honest talk? Today we
   use honesty on the far side of harm — in repair.
2. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): one friend
   publicly mocks another's project. A quick "sorry, ok?" is not repair. Repair names
   what happened ("I mocked your work in front of everyone"), hears the harm ("you felt
   humiliated and small in front of people you respect"), and makes it right ("I will
   correct it publicly and check in with you first next time"). Name each move.
3. **Sort real responses (12 min).** In pairs, sort responses to a harm — "I'm sorry you
   felt that way," "I was tired, it's not a big deal," "I did that, it was wrong, what
   can I do to make it right?" —    into *real repair* and *not repair*, and say (or write/sign) why.
4. **Practice a repair conversation (12 min).** Choose a scenario card. Write your three
   answers, then deliver them to your partner as the harmed person — aloud, or in writing,
   signing, typing, or gesture — and they respond honestly (in their preferred mode).
   Revise once so your accountability has no excuses in it.
5. **Connect to mending (8 min).** In your care journal: name one thing in your life — an
   object, a place, a tie — that is worth mending rather than discarding, and one sentence
   on what mending it would take.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture) the three questions in
   order, and why "who was harmed?" comes before "how do we make it right?"

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Answer only "what happened" and "how do we make it right," and deliver
  one real apology sentence — aloud, in writing, in sign, or by AAC.
- **Extension:** Compare a repair that keeps the power with one that shares it — and
  write two sentences on which one actually restores the relationship.
- **Communication access:** A repair conversation can be spoken, written, signed, typed,
  gestured, or AAC; learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users complete the
  three answers and the exchange in their preferred mode, and a written or signed repair
  counts fully.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three questions, sort real repair
  from a quick apology, and practice a repair conversation with accountability and no
  excuses?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three answers plus the "worth mending" reflection,
  in the care journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time a relationship was repaired — or should have been. Write
one sentence on what the repair (or its absence) changed.
