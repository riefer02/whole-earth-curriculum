# Lesson 2 — The Hard Conversation

## Summary

Learners practice saying something hard honestly and without blame, using a **four-part
sentence — observation, feeling, need, request** — drawn from needs-based communication
(Rosenberg, S-148). They convert blame sentences into honest ones, work a model example,
and practice a fictional hard conversation, then notice their own habit of blaming or
softening.

## Objectives

- Use honest, needs-based communication to sustain an intimate, trusting relationship
  through a hard conversation. _(D02.S1.12.01)_
- Reflect on one's own habit of blaming or softening, and take one step toward speaking
  honestly without blame. _(D02.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Every close relationship hits a moment where the truth is uncomfortable to say: a
promise broken, a pattern that hurts, a need that keeps going unmet. The temptation is to
do one of two things — say it as blame ("You *always*…"), which puts the other person on
the defensive, or swallow it entirely, which lets the hurt fester. There is a third way:
name what happened, own what you felt, name what you need, and make a request. This
lesson practices that third way, because a relationship grows through honest words, not
around them.

## Materials

- Four-part sentence frame
- Hard-conversation scenario cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Nonviolent communication page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four-part sentence frame; prepare the scenario cards, kept fictional
  and gentle.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the four blocks of trust. Today we practice the **honesty**
  block in a hard moment.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011):
  **observation** (what happened, without judgment), **feeling** (your own emotion),
  **need** (what matters to you), **request** (a concrete, negotiable ask) — the four
  moves of needs-based communication (Rosenberg, S-148). **Blame** is naming the *other*
  as the problem ("you always…"); honest talk names the *situation and my own need*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **honest communication
names what happened, what I felt, what I need, and what I ask — without turning the other
person into the villain.** The four-part form is a widely used, needs-based approach
(Rosenberg, S-148); hold it as a useful tool, not the only way people speak honestly
across cultures.

The **ethics** lens: honesty has a shape — it aims at the truth *and* at the
relationship, not at wounding. The **egalitarianism** lens: blame puts one person above
the other as judge; the four-part form treats both as equals with needs of their own. The
**global** lens: how directly people speak hard truths differs across cultures — some
value directness, some indirectness, some third-party mediation; the frame is a tool to
adapt, not a universal script. The **technology** lens: a hard truth sent by text arrives
without tone, face, or a chance to repair in real time, so digital honesty needs extra
care — or a decision to speak in person (Suler, S-343). The **critical-thinking** lens:
separate the *observation* (what happened) from the *story* you are telling yourself
about it, and you are halfway to honest talk. Preview: Lesson 3 turns to repair after
harm.

**Safeguarding:** all scenarios are fictional. No learner is asked to script a real
private conflict; they may always use a character.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what are the four blocks of trust? Today we work on
   **honesty** in the moment it is hardest.
2. **Meet the four moves (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): a friend keeps
   canceling plans at the last minute. Blame sounds like "You're so selfish — you never
   show up." Honest talk sounds like: "When you canceled three times this month
   *(observation)*, I felt hurt and unimportant *(feeling)*, because I need to feel my
   time and my friendship are valued *(need)*. Would you be willing to let me know
   earlier, or pick a time you can keep *(request)*?" Name each move.
3. **Convert blame into honesty (12 min).** In pairs, take blame sentences ("You always
   ignore me," "You never listen") and rewrite each into the four-part form. Check each
   other's: is it an observation, or still a judgment?
4. **Practice a hard conversation (12 min).** Choose one scenario card. Write your
   four-part sentence, then deliver it to your partner as the other character — say it
   aloud, or write, sign, type, or gesture it. The listener responds (in their preferred
   mode), and you revise once to keep it honest and unblaming.
5. **Reflect on your own habit (8 min).** In your care journal: when you have to say
   something hard, do you tend to blame, or to soften and stay silent? Write one sentence
   on each, and one step you will try.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture) the four moves in order,
   and why the order matters.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Complete only the observation and feeling moves, and speak them aloud once
  — or write, sign, type, or gesture them.
- **Extension:** Take a real hard truth you *want* to say (privately) and draft it in the
  four-part form — then revise it for tone, and test it against the "is it still blame?"
  check.
- **Communication access:** "Saying" a hard truth here means *delivering* it — aloud, in
  writing, in sign, by typing, by AAC, or by gesture. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, or hard-of-hearing deliver and receive each part in their preferred mode; a
  written or signed four-part sentence is a complete four-part sentence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four moves, convert a blame
  sentence into honest talk, and practice delivering it with a partner — aloud, in
  writing, in sign, or by AAC?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The rewritten four-part sentence plus the written
  reflection on their own blaming/softening habit, in the care journal.

## Home connection

Notice one small moment at home where someone said something hard (or avoided it). Write
one sentence on how it was said and one on how it could have been said without blame.
