# Lesson 11 — A Personal Ethic of Care

## Summary

Learners articulate their own personal **ethic of care, cooperation, and mutual aid** in
words they can live by. They distinguish a **value** (what I hold matters) from a
**commitment** (what I will actually do), meet the ethic of reciprocity with people and
the living world, and write a three-line ethic statement — *I care about, because,
therefore I will* — then test it for honesty.

## Objectives

- Articulate a personal ethic of care, cooperation, and mutual aid for a just and
  thriving world. _(D02.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

An ethic is not a poster on a wall; it is a sentence you can actually steer by. Most of
us carry care as a loose feeling — we would help, we are not cruel — but a feeling that
is never put into words is easy to forget in the moment it matters. Putting your ethic
into three plain lines — what you care about, why, and what you will therefore do — turns
a vague warmth into a commitment you can keep or break, and therefore examine. This lesson
asks you to write yours.

## Materials

- Ethic-statement scaffold
- Care journal
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Care and reciprocity page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the ethic-statement scaffold.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–10, empathy into reasoning, dignity for the more-than-human,
  nested communities, solidarity, and the common good. Today we distill it into *one
  ethic*.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): an **ethic
  of care** is a stance that sees relationship and need as central to right action.
  **Cooperation** is acting together toward a shared good; **mutual aid** is meeting one
  another's needs as equals (Kropotkin, S-163; Spade, S-345). **Reciprocity** is the
  pattern of taking and giving back that keeps relationships alive (Kimmerer, S-427).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a personal ethic is a
value plus a commitment — what I hold matters, and what I will therefore do — and putting
it into words is what makes it liveable and examinable.** Mutual aid is a well-documented
human practice (Kropotkin, S-163), and the care-vs-charity distinction (where the power
sits) is a real one (Spade, S-345). Hold "care is good" as a value the curriculum
affirms, distinct from the *fact* that mutual aid traditions are widespread.

The **ethics** lens: this is the lesson where the learner states, rather than only
analyzes, what they hold right — the capstone of the unit's ethical strand. The
**egalitarianism** lens: an ethic of care that serves only people who look like me is
only half an ethic; test yours against the person least like you. The **global** lens:
ethics of care and reciprocity are not one culture's possession — they appear in
Indigenous reciprocity, in African harambee, in Indonesian gotong royong, in Andean
mink'a (S-164) — many names for one recognition. The **environment** lens: a just and
thriving world includes the living Earth; an ethic of care that stops at humans leaves
out the world that holds us (Kimmerer, S-427). The **technology** lens: technology is a
servant of the ethic — the question is whether the tools I use extend care or only
efficiency; name one tool you will use *for* your ethic, and one you will not let
override it. Preview: Lesson 12 turns the ethic into practice, distinguishing mutual aid
from charity.

**Safeguarding:** the ethic is personal and may be shared only in part; there is no
single correct ethic, only honesty and the test that it serves others, not only oneself.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–10, name three ideas you want your own ethic to
   carry. Today we write it.
2. **Meet value vs. commitment (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): "I value
   fairness" is a value; "I will divide shared work so no one carries more than their
   share" is a commitment. An ethic needs both. Name the difference, and the ethic of
   **reciprocity** — taking and giving back (Kimmerer, S-427).
3. **Write your ethic (15 min).** On the scaffold, write three lines: *I care about…*
   (name it), *because…* (say why it matters), *therefore I will…* (name a concrete
   commitment). Make the commitment real — small enough to keep, big enough to mean
   something.
4. **Test it against the least-like-you (8 min).** Does your ethic serve someone very
   unlike you — distant, poor, older, or not yet born? Does it serve the living world? If
   not, revise one line until it does.
5. **Share it (8 min).** In pairs, deliver your three lines to a partner — aloud, in
   writing, in sign, or by typing. The partner asks one question — "what would that
   actually look like this month?" — and you answer (in any mode), then revise once for
   honesty.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture), in one sentence, the ethic
   you will carry out of this lesson — and one way you will keep it from staying just
   words.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Complete two lines — what you care about and one thing you will do — and
  deliver them aloud, in writing, or in sign.
- **Extension:** Write a one-page statement that connects your ethic to the unit's ideas —
  trust, empathy, nested communities, solidarity, and the more-than-human — and name one
  place it will be tested.
- **Communication access:** An ethic statement is written here, and may be shared aloud,
  in sign, by typing, or by AAC — or kept written only. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, or hard-of-hearing write and share their ethic in their preferred mode; the
  written statement is the artifact, not the speaking of it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner distinguish value from commitment, write a
  three-line ethic that includes a concrete commitment, and test it against someone unlike
  themselves and the living world?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The ethic statement, in the care journal — the unit's
  central capstone artifact, returned to in Lesson 14.

## Home connection

Share your ethic statement with someone at home — read it aloud, or show them the written
or signed version — and ask them to name one place they think it will be hard to keep.
Write one sentence on their answer.
