# Lesson 12 — Mutual Aid, Not Just Charity

## Summary

Learners distinguish charity from mutual aid — the difference is where the power sits —
and practice care that shares both the giving and the deciding. They meet named
mutual-aid traditions worldwide (harambee, gotong royong, mink'a, barn raising), and
design one small mutual-aid plan in which the people helped also help decide.

## Objectives

- Articulate a personal ethic of care and mutual aid, distinguishing mutual aid from
  charity and practicing care that shares power. _(D02.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

There are two ways to help. In one, someone who has gives to someone who lacks, and the
giver keeps the power — deciding what to give, who deserves it, and when. That is charity,
and it can be real and good. In the other, people meet one another's needs *together*,
sharing both the giving and the deciding — no one is reduced to a passive receiver. That
is mutual aid, and it is old and worldwide: Kenya's *harambee*, Indonesia's *gotong
royong*, the Andes' *mink'a*, a North American barn raising. The difference is not the
goods; it is the power. This lesson asks which kind of care you want to practice.

## Materials

- Charity-vs-mutual-aid chart
- Mutual-aid tradition cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Charity vs. mutual aid page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the charity-vs-mutual-aid chart; prepare the tradition cards, kept
  factual, with "verify local details with community members" noted.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, your ethic of care; from Grade 8 (D02.S4.08.02), the
  charity/solidarity distinction. Today we make it concrete.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): **charity**
  is a one-way gift in which the giver keeps the power and the receiver stays passive;
  **mutual aid** is people meeting one another's needs as equals, sharing the giving and
  the deciding (Spade, S-345). **Reciprocity** keeps the giving two-way over time
  (Kropotkin, S-163).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the difference between
charity and mutual aid is not the goods but the power — and care that shares power
preserves dignity in a way a one-way gift cannot.** This is a real, well-documented
distinction (Spade, S-345), and mutual-aid traditions are widespread (Kropotkin, S-163;
S-164). Hold the claim "sharing power is better than only sharing goods" as a *value* the
curriculum affirms, and hold the descriptive difference as *fact*. Charity is not wrong;
it is simply not the same thing.

The **ethics** lens: does the way we help respect the dignity of the helped — and who
gets to decide what "help" is? The **egalitarianism** lens: this is the egalitarian lens
made practical — the question is whether the giving relationship is between equals or
across a gap of power. The **global** lens: harambee, gotong royong, mink'a, barn raising
— different continents, one recognition that neighbors meet needs together; verify local
details rather than generalizing (S-164). The **technology** lens: digital networks now
host mutual aid — fundraisers, skill-shares, neighborhood groups — but a platform can
slip back into one-way giving or gatekeeping, so the power question follows the tool
(Britannica, S-481). The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *who decides* in any act of
helping — that single question separates the two columns. Preview: Lesson 13 scales the
ethic from local action to global solidarity.

**Safeguarding:** no learner is asked to perform or receive charity in the room; the
design is a plan, not a demand to give resources now.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11: your ethic's three lines. Today we make the
   "therefore I will" concrete — and ask *who decides*.
2. **Meet charity vs. mutual aid (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): after a flood,
   one response is a donor handing out supplies to grateful recipients (charity); another
   is neighbors pooling what they have and deciding *together* what each household needs
   (mutual aid). The goods overlap; the power does not (Spade, S-345). Name the
   difference.
3. **Meet the traditions (10 min).** Read the cards: Kenya's *harambee* ("all pull
   together"), Indonesia's *gotong royong* (shared bearing of burdens), the Andes' *mink'a*
   (communal labor), a North American barn raising (neighbors raise a barn, the favor
   returns). Note what each shares: everyone gives, everyone decides, the favor returns
   (S-164).
4. **Chart a real example (12 min).** In pairs, choose one real situation (a sick
   neighbor, a student who lost their books, a community garden). Fill the chart: in the
   charity column, who gives and who decides; in the mutual-aid column, how would giving
   and deciding be shared?
5. **Design a mutual-aid plan (10 min).** In your care journal, design one small mutual-aid
   plan — the need, who shares it, and one *decision rule* that keeps power with everyone
   involved, not just the givers.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture), in one sentence, the
   difference between charity and mutual aid — and which one you intend to practice, and
   why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one way neighbors help each other and whether the helped also get to
  decide, in one sentence.
- **Extension:** Research one named mutual-aid tradition in depth, and write a short
  account of how it keeps power and reciprocity with the people involved — then compare it
  to a digital mutual-aid tool today.
- **Communication access:** The "act it out" step can be drawn, signed, described in writing, or expressed via AAC
  instead of performed; the two-column chart and the plan are written artifacts.
  Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users complete every step in their
  preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish charity from mutual aid,
  name a mutual-aid tradition, and design a small plan with a shared decision rule?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The charity-vs-mutual-aid chart and the mutual-aid plan,
  in the care journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time neighbors helped each other — and whether everyone
helped decide. Write one sentence on what you learned.
