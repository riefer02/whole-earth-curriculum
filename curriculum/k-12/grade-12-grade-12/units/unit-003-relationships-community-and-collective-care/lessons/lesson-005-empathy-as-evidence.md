# Lesson 5 — Empathy as Evidence

## Summary

Learners integrate empathy and perspective-taking into ethical reasoning about a complex
social problem, rather than treating feeling and principle as rivals. They learn the
difference between **empathic concern** (which informs) and **personal distress** (which
overwhelms), reason one real problem through both columns — inside experience and outside
principles — and defend a reasoned position that is more honest because it includes both.

## Objectives

- Integrate empathy and perspective-taking into ethical reasoning about a complex social
  problem, weighing what I understand from the inside against principles and consequences.
  _(D02.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Reasoning about a complex problem — who should get housing, who may cross a border, whose
water a dam may take — without ever asking what it *feels like* for the people inside it
is reasoning about a ghost. But feeling alone is not reasoning either; outrage without
weighing can do as much harm as coldness. The mature move is to hold both: let empathy
tell you what is at stake from the inside, and let principles and consequences tell you
what to do from the outside. This lesson practices holding both at once.

## Materials

- Empathy-into-reasoning organizer
- Complex-problem briefs
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Empathic concern vs. distress page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the empathy-into-reasoning organizer; prepare the complex-problem briefs,
  kept factual and non-graphic, with stakeholders named.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D02.S2.11.01), empathy for people we have never met; from
  Unit 1 this year (D01.S1.12.01), how feeling shapes judgment. Today we make empathy a
  *deliberate input* to reasoning.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): **empathic
  concern** is other-oriented feeling that moves us to help; **personal distress** is
  self-oriented feeling of being overwhelmed (Batson, S-230). **Perspective-taking** is
  deliberately seeing the situation as another sees it (Decety & Cowell, S-231). **Ethical
  reasoning** weighs principles, consequences, and duties — now fed by what we understand
  from the inside.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **empathy is not the
enemy of clear reasoning; it is one input, held alongside principles and consequences —
and mature reasoning refuses to drop either.** The concern-versus-distress distinction is
well evidenced (Batson, S-230), and perspective-taking is a well-studied route to
prosocial judgment, not automatic (Decety & Cowell, S-231). Hold empathy as description
of how we respond, not as a demand to feel a prescribed way.

The **ethics** lens: what is right here is decided by both — the inner facts empathy
reveals and the outer principles that bind everyone. The **egalitarianism** lens: whose
experience we are *willing* to imagine is uneven; reasoning is skewed when we only
imagine the people who resemble us. The **global** lens: the same problem — housing,
migration, water — looks different in a megacity, a village, a refugee camp, and a
drought region; none is the default. The **technology** lens: media curates *whose* inner
world we are shown, and feeds can narrow empathy into an echo of people like us
(Britannica, S-481), so perspective-taking must be sought, not received. The
**critical-thinking** lens: mark clearly which parts of your conclusion are *evidence*
(what is at stake) and which are *values* (what you believe matters) — and do not let one
wear the other's clothes. Preview: Lesson 6 turns to deliberately taking many views of
one problem.

**Safeguarding:** problems are described factually and gently, no graphic detail; a
learner with a lived connection to a problem speaks only if they choose, and is honored,
not turned into a case study.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: the three moves of active listening. Today we
   extend listening into *imagining another's inner world* — and feed it into reasoning.
2. **Meet concern vs. distress (8 min).** Work a model example (S-011): hearing about
   families losing their homes can move you to help (concern) or swamp you into looking
   away (distress). Only concern is *usable* in reasoning; distress is the fog. Name the
   difference.
3. **Fill the two columns (15 min).** In a group, take one complex-problem brief. First,
   the **inside** column: for each stakeholder, one sentence on what the situation is like
   for them (perspective-taking). Then the **outside** column: the principles, duties, and
   consequences that apply to everyone.
4. **Argue a reasoned position (12 min).** Weigh the columns and defend one position —
   aloud or in writing — but you must cite at least one thing from *each* column in your
   defense. Notice where empathy changed your principle, and where a principle restrained
   your empathy.
5. **Mark evidence vs. values (8 min).** In your care journal, rewrite your position in
   three sentences, underlining what is *evidence* (what is at stake) and circling what is
   *value* (what you hold matters).
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture) why dropping either
   column makes your reasoning worse — not more honest.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one feeling for one stakeholder and one principle, and write a
  one-sentence position using both.
- **Extension:** Find a case where empathy and a principle genuinely conflict, argue both
  sides in writing, and say which should yield and why.
- **Communication access:** "Arguing" and "defending" a position may be done aloud or in
  writing, signing, typing, or AAC; a written or signed defense with one cite from each
  column is complete. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, or hard-of-hearing complete
  every reasoning step in their preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish concern from distress, fill
  both columns for a real problem, and defend (aloud or in writing) a position that cites
  both empathy and principle, marking evidence from value?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column organizer plus the three-sentence
  position with evidence/value marked, in the care journal.

## Home connection

With someone at home, take one decision your household faces and name both columns: what
each person feels from the inside, and what principles or consequences apply from the
outside. Write one sentence on what changed.
