# Lesson 8 — My Place in the Nest

## Summary

Learners articulate their own place within **nested communities**, from family to the
**global community of all life**. They draw a concentric map and name one real belonging
in each ring — noticing the rings they usually forget, how online communities cut across
the rings, and how the outermost ring includes the whole living Earth.

## Objectives

- Articulate one's own place within nested communities, from family to the global
  community of all life. _(D02.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

You belong to more than one "we." There is the "we" of your household, the "we" of your
friends and your school, the "we" of your neighborhood and your region and your country —
and behind all of them, the quiet "we" of humanity, and the even larger "we" of every
living thing that shares this planet. Each is a ring in a nest, and you sit at the center
of your own. Naming each ring — including the ones you usually forget — is how you locate
yourself, because you cannot act well in a community you cannot see.

## Materials

- Nested-communities map
- Care journal
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Nested and imagined communities page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the nested-communities map.
- Retrieval: from Grade 3 (D02.S3.03.01), comparing one's community with another's; from
  Grade 10 (D02.S3.10.02), how local connects to global. Today we draw the whole nest.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): a
  **nested community** is a set of belonging rings, each holding the smaller ones. Some
  outer rings are **imagined communities** — groups whose members will never all meet,
  yet hold a shared sense of "we" (Anderson, S-415). The outermost ring is the
  **biosphere**, the community of all life.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **I am located inside
nested communities, and the outermost ring — the community of all life — is not
metaphorical; it is where my belonging actually ends.** The concept of the imagined
community (a nation whose members never meet, Anderson, S-415) is a real, well-established
idea; hold it as description, not as a claim that nations are unreal. Naming one's rings
is a descriptive map, not a ranking of which belonging matters most.

The **ethics** lens: each ring carries a different "we" with different obligations — and
the outermost ring quietly obliges me to all life, not only my own. The **egalitarianism**
lens: belonging is uneven — some people are pushed to the margins of rings, some are
told they do not belong at all; noticing that is part of mapping honestly. The **global**
lens: the rings are not the same everywhere — in one place "region" is a valley, in
another a language group, in another a diaspora — the map adapts, the nesting does not.
The **technology** lens: online communities cut across the rings — a group of people on
three continents who never meet, yet feel "we" — a new kind of imagined community made
possible by networks (Britannica, S-481; Anderson's frame applied to the digital age).
The **environment** lens: the outermost ring is the living Earth itself — soil, water,
atmosphere, and every species — and it is the one ring every other ring depends on.
Preview: Lesson 9 asks who is included in a ring and who is left out.

**Safeguarding:** learners name their rings at whatever depth they choose; no one is
required to name a family, a nation, or any identity they do not wish to claim.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: the community of all life. Today we locate
   *ourselves* inside it.
2. **Meet nested communities (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a student in a
   coastal city — their rings are themselves, their household, their friends and school,
   their neighborhood, their city and country, humanity, and all life. Name the rings and
   the idea that some outer rings are **imagined communities** (Anderson, S-415): you
   will never meet most of your country, yet you share a "we."
3. **Draw your own nest (15 min).** In your care journal, draw the concentric rings and
   name one real belonging in each — a person, a group, a place, a species, an ecosystem.
   Notice which rings you had to think hard about.
4. **Find the forgotten ring (8 min).** Which ring do you usually forget — the region?
   humanity? the living world? Write one sentence on why it is easy to forget, and one on
   what it would mean to remember it.
5. **Map a crossing community (8 min).** Is there a community you belong to that cuts
   *across* the rings — an online group, a faith, a movement, a diaspora? Draw it as a
   line crossing the rings, and note what changes when belonging is not nested but woven.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture), in one sentence, where
   you are — from the innermost ring to the outermost.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name three inner rings (self, family, friends) and one outer ring (all
  life) with one word each.
- **Extension:** Write a paragraph on how one ring's claims can conflict with another's
  (family vs. nation vs. all life), and how you would begin to weigh them.
- **Communication access:** Naming the rings is a written (or signed, typed, gestured, or AAC)
  task here, not a spoken one; the map itself is the answer. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users complete every step in their preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner draw the nested rings, name one real
  belonging in each, and identify which ring they usually forget and why?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The nested-communities map, in the care journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home which "ring" they think of themselves as belonging to first. Write one
sentence on whether their answer surprised you.

## Resources

- On online communities as a new, network-made form of belonging: Encyclopaedia
  Britannica, "Social media" (S-481).
- On worked examples for a new mapping skill: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011);
  on spaced retrieval: Dunlosky et al. (2013) (S-039).
