# Lesson 7 — Dignity for the More-Than-Human

## Summary

Learners extend empathy beyond humans to the **more-than-human world**, and act on it by
planning a concrete act of dignity for a real living being or place near them. They meet
the idea of **intrinsic value** and **reciprocity**, study documented acts of ecological
care (the Chipko tree-huggers, the Green Belt Movement), and design one small, local
action they can actually take.

## Objectives

- Act on empathy to reduce suffering and promote dignity for the more-than-human world,
  planning a concrete local act of care. _(D02.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

The empathy you have been practicing does not have to stop at the edge of the human. A
river carries life downstream; a tree shades a street for a century; a soil holds the
world's quietest work. People across the world have acted on that — villagers in the
Himalayas embraced trees to stop them being felled, and a movement in Kenya planted
millions of trees from a woman's backyard. This lesson asks what *you* can do, right
where you are, to treat a living thing as worthy of care for its own sake — not only for
what it gives you.

## Materials

- Dignity-in-action planner
- More-than-human example cards
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Reciprocity and the land page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the dignity-in-action planner; prepare the example cards, kept factual.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, steelmanning a rival view; from Grade 11 (D02.S2.11.01),
  empathy for people we have never met. Today we extend empathy past the human line.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011): the
  **more-than-human** world is all living beings and places beyond human persons.
  **Intrinsic value** is worth a thing holds for its own sake, not only its use to us.
  **Reciprocity** is taking and giving back — the ethic that "all flourishing is mutual"
  (Kimmerer, S-427). **Ecological empathy** is empathy turned to the living world.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the circle of moral
concern can extend beyond humans — a tree, a river, an animal can be a subject of care,
not only a resource — and empathy, enacted, becomes an act of dignity.** The examples are
documented: the Chipko movement's villagers embraced trees to prevent felling
(Britannica, S-328); Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement planted over thirty million
trees and spread across Africa (S-059). The ethic of reciprocity with the living world is
a real, honored tradition — Kimmerer names it in *Braiding Sweetgrass* (S-427). Hold the
claim "the living world deserves care for its own sake" as a *value* the curriculum
affirms, distinct from the *fact* that many peoples have practiced it.

The **ethics** lens: is a river's worth only what it does for us, or does it hold worth
on its own — and what follows for how we act? The **egalitarianism** lens: dignity has
been denied to some *humans* by treating them as less than fully persons; extending the
circle to the more-than-human is a widening of the same principle, not a distraction from
human justice. The **global** lens: these are not one culture's idea — tree-hugging in
the Himalayas, tree-planting in Kenya, river-restoration on many continents — different
places, one recognition. The **environment** lens: this is the environment lens made
moral — empathy as an ecological force. The **technology** lens: technology cuts both
ways — machines that clear a forest in a day, and tools (mapping, monitoring, organizing)
that let distant people defend a river; the question is whose flourishing technology
serves. Preview: Lesson 8 maps your own place among nested communities, from family to
this whole living world.

**Safeguarding:** the action is small, local, and optional to share; no one is graded on
grandiosity. A learner without easy outdoor access can choose a potted plant, a pet, a
street tree, or a place they remember.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: why does a conclusion get stronger by meeting its
   rivals fairly? Today we meet a *rival claim* — that the living world is only a
   resource — and weigh it.
2. **Meet intrinsic value and reciprocity (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a
   tree can be cut for timber (use), or kept as a neighbor that shades, breathes, and
   lives for its own sake (dignity). Name the two ways of seeing, and the ethic of
   **reciprocity** — what we take, we give back (Kimmerer, S-427).
3. **Study acts of ecological care (10 min).** In a group, read the example cards:
   villagers in the Indian Himalaya embracing trees to stop felling (Chipko, S-328);
   Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement planting over thirty million trees across Africa
   (S-059); a community restoring its river. Note what each *did*.
4. **Observe one living thing (8 min).** If possible, go and observe one nearby living
   being or place for five minutes. Otherwise, bring one to mind. Notice what it needs
   and what harms it.
5. **Plan one act of dignity (12 min).** In the planner, name the being or place, what
   harms it, and one concrete act of dignity you can actually do this week — feed, clean,
   plant, protect, speak for (or write, sign, or campaign for), or simply learn about it.
   Make it real and small.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture), in one sentence, why
   treating the living world as worthy of care *for its own sake* changes what you will do
   this week.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one living thing and one small way to help it, and take that one
  step.
- **Extension:** Research one documented act of ecological care in depth, and write a
  short account of what made it work — then connect it to your own local action.
- **Communication access:** Naming and sharing in this lesson can be written, signed,
  typed, gestured, or AAC; "speaking for" the living world may equally mean writing, signing,
  or campaigning for it. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users
  complete every step in their preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name intrinsic value and reciprocity,
  describe a documented act of ecological care, and plan one concrete local act of
  dignity?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The dignity-in-action planner, kept in the care journal,
  with a one-sentence note on the first step actually taken.

## Home connection

With someone at home, do one small act of care for a living thing or place together — and
write one sentence on what you each noticed.
