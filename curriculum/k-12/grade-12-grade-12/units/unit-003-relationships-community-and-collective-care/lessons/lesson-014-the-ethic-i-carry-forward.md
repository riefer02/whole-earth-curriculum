# Lesson 14 — The Ethic I Carry Forward

## Summary

Learners gather the unit into a single carried ethic. They retrieve each of the unit's
artifacts — trust, honest talk, repair, listening, empathy into reasoning, many views,
more-than-human dignity, nested belonging, solidarity, mutual aid, local-to-global action
— and assemble a capstone portfolio that answers one question: what will I actually carry
out of here?

## Objectives

- Articulate a personal ethic of care, cooperation, and mutual aid, and commit to acting
  on it for people and the Earth. _(D02.S4.12.01; D02.S4.12.02)_
- Articulate one's place within nested communities, from family to the global community
  of all life. _(D02.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

An ethic is only real if it survives the walk out the door. Everything this unit built —
the trust you can name, the hard conversation you can hold, the repair you know how to
make, the empathy you can reason with, the dignity you can extend to a tree or a river,
the rings you can locate yourself in, the solidarity you can weigh, the mutual aid you can
practice, the local act you can link to the world — was practice for one thing: carrying
a way of being into the rest of your life. This lesson is the act of picking it all up and
walking forward with it.

## Materials

- Capstone portfolio sheet
- Care journal
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Spaced retrieval page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the capstone portfolio sheet; have learners bring their care journals.
- Retrieval across the unit: this lesson is the spaced-retrieval payoff — recalling each
  idea *now*, after the spacing of fourteen lessons, strengthens it (Dunlosky, S-039).
- Prepare the one organizing question: **what will I actually carry out of this unit — as
  a person in relationships, in communities, and in the living world?**

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the unit's parts are
not separate lessons but one ethic, and the capstone's job is to integrate them — by
recall, not by re-reading.** Spaced retrieval across the unit is the evidence-backed way
to make the learning last (Dunlosky, S-039); this lesson is that technique enacted, not
just described. The capstone is a *promise*, not a grade; a learner's ethic is theirs to
carry, not yours to judge.

The **ethics** lens: the capstone is the learner's own statement of what they hold right —
the unit's ethical strand completed. The **egalitarianism** lens: the stress-test — "does
this ethic serve someone unlike me, and the living world?" — is the egalitarian check,
kept to the end. The **global** lens: the ethic travels — it is one person's, but it was
built from harambee and mink'a, from tree-hugging and tree-planting, from rivers on every
continent. The **technology** lens: name the tool that will serve the ethic and the one
that will tempt it — the carried ethic includes a decision about technology. The
**environment** lens: the ethic includes the more-than-human world, because a thriving
world is not only a human one (Kimmerer, S-427).

**Safeguarding:** the carried ethic is shared only in the part the learner chooses; there
is no single correct ethic, only honesty, and the invitation to keep revising it for life.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve the unit (10 min).** Without opening your journal, write down, from memory,
   the key idea of each lesson — trust's four blocks, the four moves of honest talk, the
   three questions of repair, the listening commitment, the two columns of reasoning,
   steelmanning, more-than-human dignity, the nested rings, the us/them line, the common
   good, the ethic statement, mutual aid, local-to-global. Then open your journal and
   check what you recalled and what you missed (retrieval: S-039).
2. **Assemble the portfolio (12 min).** Lay out your artifacts on the capstone sheet.
   Notice the thread that runs through them — what each one was really *for*.
3. **Write the carried ethic (12 min).** Write, in your own words, the ethic you will
   carry out of this unit — no more than five lines — answering: how I will be in
   relationships, in communities, and toward the living world.
4. **Stress-test it (8 min).** Ask yourself the hard question and write one honest
   answer: *what would make me abandon this ethic — and how will I not?* Then ask: does
   it serve someone unlike me, and the living world?
5. **Share the promise (8 min).** In a circle or with a partner, share one line of your
   carried ethic — the line you most want to keep — spoken, written, or signed. Receive
   others' lines (by ear, eye, or hand) without judging them.
6. **Close (5 min).** Write the date on your ethic and one word for what you hope it
   becomes: a habit, a promise, a compass. Close the journal.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Recall three ideas and write a two-line carried ethic; share one line —
  spoken, written, or signed.
- **Extension:** Write a one-page letter to your future self, naming where this ethic
  will be tested and what you will do then — and seal it to reopen in a year.
- **Communication access:** The capstone is written; sharing the carried ethic is
  available in speech, sign, writing, typing, or AAC, and the written statement is the
  graded artifact. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, or hard-of-hearing share and
  receive the circle's lines in their preferred mode; no step requires hearing or
  producing sound.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The assembled capstone portfolio — all unit artifacts, the
  retrieved-ideas check, and the written carried ethic — assessed for completeness,
  honesty, and the stress-test (does it serve others and the living world?), not for
  agreement with any single view.
- **Self + peer:** The shared line (spoken, written, or signed) and the retrieval check,
  shared without ranking.

## Home connection

Share your carried ethic with someone who matters to you — read it aloud, or show them the
written or signed version — and ask them to remind you of it in a year. Write one sentence
on what they said.
