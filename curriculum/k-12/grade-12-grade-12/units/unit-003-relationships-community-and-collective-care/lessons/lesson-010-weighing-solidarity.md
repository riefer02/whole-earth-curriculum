# Lesson 10 — Weighing Solidarity

## Summary

Learners weigh belonging, solidarity, and collective responsibility when they pull in
different directions, and decide how to act for the common good. They meet the
**free-rider problem** — when individual or in-group advantage pulls against the shared
good — and practice a disciplined weighing: name the loyalties, name the costs, then
decide with reasons.

## Objectives

- Weigh belonging, solidarity, and collective responsibility in deciding how to act for
  the common good. _(D02.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

Sometimes the thing that is good for *my* people and the thing that is good for
*everyone* are not the same. A village may need the river's water that a downstream city
also drinks. A nation may want to keep its wealth to itself while others starve. A
neighborhood may want a forest for timber while the region needs it for flood control.
In those moments, solidarity is not automatic — it is a *decision*, made by weighing
loyalties and costs, and it is exactly the decision this lesson practices. Because a
common good is not a thing that happens to you; it is a thing you help choose.

## Materials

- Solidarity weighing sheet
- Common-good dilemmas
- Paper and pencil (low-tech)
- Commons and free-riding page (enriched)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the solidarity weighing sheet; prepare the dilemmas, kept factual.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, the us/them line; from Grade 8 (D02.S4.08.02), the
  difference between charity and solidarity. Today we weigh solidarity as a decision.
- Prepare plain definitions (model once: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011):
  **solidarity** is acting with others across difference for a shared good. The
  **common good** is what benefits the whole, not only one part. A **free-rider** is
  someone who takes the benefit of a shared effort without paying its cost — the pull of
  individual advantage against the common good. **Collective responsibility** is what we
  owe because we share the outcome.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **solidarity is a
decision, made by honestly weighing loyalty to one's own against obligation to the whole
— and the common good is chosen, not automatic.** The free-rider problem is a
well-documented feature of shared resources (Ostrom, S-416); hold it as description, not
as a claim that people are selfish. Ostrom's work shows communities *can* govern commons
fairly by their own rules — so the dilemma is real but not hopeless.

The **ethics** lens: the weighing itself is the ethical act — naming the loyalties and
costs before deciding. The **egalitarianism** lens: the common good is often invoked to
hide who actually pays — so ask who bears the cost and who reaps the benefit. The
**global** lens: the same dilemma recurs worldwide — water, borders, forests, fisheries —
and communities answer it differently; none is the default. The **technology** lens:
technology changes the commons — a global commons like the atmosphere or the ocean is
harmed by actions in many places at once, and digital networks can either coordinate
solidarity across borders or let free-riding hide in plain sight (IPCC, S-005; Britannica,
S-481). The **environment** lens: the largest commons are environmental — clean air,
stable climate, shared water — where one place's gain is everyone's loss, the purest
case of the weighing. Preview: Lesson 11 turns the weighing into a personal ethic of
care.

**Safeguarding:** dilemmas are structural (water, borders, resources), never about a
learner's identity; no one is asked to argue against their own people's survival.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9: the us/them line and who pays it. Today we *decide*
   across that line.
2. **Meet the free-rider problem (10 min).** Work a model example (S-011): a shared
   fishery where each boat gains by taking more, but if all take more the fish vanish.
   Name the pull: individual gain vs. the common good — and that communities can and do
   make rules to meet it (Ostrom, S-416).
3. **Weigh a dilemma (15 min).** In a group, take a common-good dilemma. On the sheet,
   name the choice, the loyalties pulling each way, the cost to each side, and who bears
   it.
4. **Decide and defend (12 min).** Decide how to act for the common good, and defend it —
   aloud or in writing — but you must name the cost of your decision and who pays it. A
   decision that denies its cost is not a decision; it is a wish.
5. **Find the free-rider (8 min).** In your care journal, name where the free-rider pull
   shows up in the dilemma — who could take the benefit without paying the cost — and one
   fair rule that could meet it.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say (or write, sign, type, or gesture), in one sentence, what the
   common good is — and why it is chosen, not automatic.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name who is helped and who is hurt by one choice, and say (or write/sign)
  which choice you would make and why in one sentence.
- **Extension:** Write a short argument that applies the weighing to a *real* current
  common-good dilemma (a river, a border policy, a climate choice), naming loyalties,
  costs, and a reasoned decision.
- **Communication access:** Defending a decision may be spoken, written, signed, typed,
  gestured, or AAC; a written or signed defense that names its own cost is complete. Learners who
  are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or AAC users complete every step in their preferred mode.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name solidarity, the common good, and the
  free-rider problem, weigh a dilemma's loyalties and costs, and defend a decision that
  names its own cost?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The solidarity weighing sheet, in the care journal.

## Home connection

Notice one shared resource your household or neighborhood uses together — water,
electricity, a shared space — and ask who pays its cost and who free-rides. Write one
sentence on what you noticed.
