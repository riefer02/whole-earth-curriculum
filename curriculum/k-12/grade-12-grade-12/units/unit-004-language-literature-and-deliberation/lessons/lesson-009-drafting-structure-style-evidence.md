# Lesson 9 — Drafting: Structure, Style, and Evidence

## Summary

Learners turn a plan into a **draft**, building paragraphs as small arguments — **topic
sentence, evidence, warrant, transition** — and making deliberate choices about
**structure, style, and voice**. A draft is meant to be imperfect; drafting with a
scaffold makes that imperfection *usable* for revision.

## Objectives

- Draft a sustained piece with clear structure, deliberate style, and integrated evidence,
  paragraph by paragraph. _(D04.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

Every paragraph you have ever admired is doing a quiet job: it opens with a claim, brings
evidence, says *why the evidence matters*, and hands the reader to the next paragraph.
None of that is magic — it is a pattern you can learn and repeat. A draft is not the
finished piece; it is the first time the idea stands on its own feet. The trick is to
draft *with structure in hand*, so that when you revise, you are fixing a building that
already has beams, not sorting a pile of bricks.

## Materials

- Drafting scaffold
- Deliberation journal
- Sentence-starter bank (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the drafting scaffold; prepare the sentence-starter bank.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 8, the writing plan; from Lesson 2, claim/grounds/warrant. Today
  we add the capstone turn: *drafting* (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
  S-011).
- Prepare the paragraph-as-argument frame, an extension of Toulmin's model (S-236):
  **topic sentence** (the claim), **evidence** (the grounds), **warrant** (why the evidence
  supports the claim), **transition** (how the paragraph hands off to the next). Explicit
  strategy instruction and the writing process are evidence-supported (IES, S-171; Graham
  & Perin, S-102).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
a paragraph is a small argument — claim, evidence, warrant, transition — extending the
claim/grounds/warrant model (Toulmin, S-236), and drafting with explicit strategy
instruction is evidence-supported (IES, S-171; Graham & Perin, S-102). The scaffold is a
*servant*, not a master: a paragraph that breaks the pattern deliberately can be
stronger, and a learner whose home language or tradition structures prose differently
should be honored, not corrected into one mold.

The **ethics** lens: integrated evidence is quoted and cited honestly — a writer's
integrity lives in not passing another's words off as one's own. The **egalitarianism**
lens: the scaffold puts a *doable* pattern in every learner's hands, so clear structure
is not the property of the fluent. The **global** lens: rhetorical structures differ
across cultures — some traditions circle a point, others state it outright — and no
single shape is "correct." The **technology** lens: word processors, version history, and
voice dictation change *how* we draft and who can draft; the tool assists, but the
thinking is the writer's. The **environment** lens: drafting is a place to choose
deliberately — a screen draft needs no paper, and a voice-first draft lets a writer
think aloud while moving — small choices with real effects. The **critical-thinking**
lens: "does this evidence actually support this topic sentence?" is the check a writer
runs on every paragraph. Preview: Lesson 10 revises the draft into its finished form.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: what five parts made your plan? Today the plan
   becomes paragraphs.
2. **Meet the paragraph-as-argument (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Watch
   one paragraph take shape: *topic sentence* (the claim), *evidence* (a measurement or
   quotation, cited), *warrant* (why the evidence supports the claim), *transition* (the
   hand-off). Name each part and what it does for the reader.
3. **Draft the body (15 min).** Using your plan and the scaffold, draft two or three
   body paragraphs. Use the sentence-starter bank where it helps, but keep your own
   voice.
4. **Read and mark (10 min).** Read your paragraphs aloud to a partner — or read
   them silently, have a partner or screen reader read them back, or sign them — and mark
   where the argument moves cleanly and where it stalls; note any evidence that is asserted
   but not yet shown.
5. **Vary the style (8 min).** Revise one paragraph's *style only*: change sentence
   lengths, choose one more precise word, and read it again — aloud, silently, or via
   screen reader — to hear the difference.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why a draft is meant
   to be imperfect, and what "good enough to revise" means to you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Draft one paragraph (topic sentence + one evidence + one warrant), leaving
  transitions and style for later.
- **Extension:** Draft a section that deliberately breaks the pattern for effect — a
  one-sentence paragraph, a delayed claim — and explain what the break does.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading aloud, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC draft, mark, and share in their preferred mode; no
  step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Writing access (dyslexia, low-vision, spelling difficulty):** The draft may be produced
  by voice — dictated to a scribe or recorded with speech-to-text where a device exists — and
  read back by a partner or screen reader (low-tech fallback: a partner or facilitator reads
  the draft back, or the learner traces each sentence finger-by-finger). Spelling and decoding
  difficulty is distinct from composition and thinking: a learner who spells non-standardly
  may draft with power and precision, and no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is
  required — what is assessed is structure, style, and integrated evidence, not the letter
  forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner draft paragraphs with a topic sentence,
  cited evidence, a warrant, and a transition, and vary style deliberately?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The drafted body paragraphs, filed in the deliberation
  journal as the working draft.

## Home connection

Read one paragraph of your draft to someone at home — or share it in writing, sign, or
AAC — and ask them where they got lost or wanted to know more. Mark that spot and plan one
revision.
