# Lesson 2 — The Architecture of a Reasoned Position

## Summary

Learners name the parts of a reasoned position — **claim, grounds, warrant, backing,
qualifier, and rebuttal** — and assemble them on a contested issue. A strong position is
reframed as one that *admits doubt honestly*: it states its evidence, names the bridge
between evidence and claim, and answers the strongest objection rather than ignoring it.

## Objectives

- Build a reasoned position on a contested issue by naming its claim, evidence, warrant,
  backing, qualifier, and rebuttal. _(D04.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

When you say, "I think we should do this," the first question anyone should ask is
*why?* — and the second is *so what connects your reason to your conclusion?* A position
that rests only on strong feeling collapses the moment it is pressed. A position that
rests on visible evidence, a stated bridge between that evidence and the claim, and an
honest answer to the best objection can survive disagreement. Those parts are not
mysterious; they have names, and you can learn to assemble them.

## Materials

- Position-architecture organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Claim strips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the position-architecture organizer; prepare the claim strips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the four deliberation moves and the difference between
  arguing to win and deliberating. Today we build the *thing* deliberation produces — a
  reasoned position (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare a plain definition of each part, from the standard functional model of
  argument (Toulmin, S-236): **claim** (what I am asserting), **grounds** (the evidence),
  **warrant** (the bridge that says *why* the grounds support the claim), **backing**
  (support for the warrant), **qualifier** (how certain I am), and **rebuttal** (the
  strongest objection and my answer to it).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: a reasoned position has
a *structure*, and that structure is the standard functional model of argument — claim,
grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, and rebuttal — named by Stephen Toulmin (S-236).
Hold it as **one useful scaffold among many**, not the only way humans reason: other
cultures and traditions have their own arts of reasoning and persuasion, and the
scaffold serves those rather than replacing them. The Greek rhetorical tradition named
persuasion through character (*ethos*), emotion (*pathos*), and argument (*logos*)
(Aristotle, S-346); Toulmin's model is one precise way to build the *logos* half.

The **ethics** lens: a rebuttal is not a weakness to hide but a duty — answering the
strongest objection is a form of honesty toward those who disagree. The **egalitarianism**
lens: the parts are learnable, which means a clear, evidenced position is not reserved
for the fluent or the privileged. The **global** lens: what counts as convincing evidence
and how openly doubt is admitted differ across cultures — none is the default. The
**technology** lens: online, claims circulate detached from their grounds and warrants;
reassembling the parts is a defense against that flattening. The **environment** lens:
use a contested environmental-claim example (a wind farm, a dam, a forest road) so the
scaffold is practiced on questions where evidence about the living world matters. The
**critical-thinking** lens: naming a qualifier and a rebuttal is the opposite of
overclaiming — it is where careful thinking becomes visible. Preview: Lesson 3 turns a
reasoned position into public speech.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what four moves keep deliberation open? Today we
   build the *position* those moves lead toward.
2. **Meet the parts (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Claim: *a wind farm
   should be built on the ridge.* Grounds: *the wind there is strong and steady, and the
   region needs power.* Warrant: *steady wind plus need is a reason to build a wind farm
   there.* Backing: *wind measurements and energy-demand records.* Qualifier: *probably,
   if the local wildlife and people are protected.* Rebuttal: *some argue it would harm
   birds and views — my answer is that careful siting and community co-ownership address
   those.* Name each part — aloud or in writing, sign, or AAC — and what it does.
3. **Assemble a position (12 min).** With a partner, take a claim strip and fill the
   organizer: grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, and — hardest — a real rebuttal with
   an honest answer.
4. **Stress-test the warrant (8 min).** Swap with another pair. Attack only the
   *warrant*: is the bridge sound? Could the same grounds support the opposite claim?
   Revise the warrant until it holds.
5. **Admit doubt (8 min).** Rewrite the qualifier so it states exactly how sure you are,
   and state the rebuttal in one sentence a fair opponent would recognize as their own.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why a position with a
   qualifier and a rebuttal is *stronger*, not weaker, than one that admits no doubt.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fill only claim, grounds, and warrant; leave qualifier and rebuttal for a
  later pass.
- **Extension:** Take a public claim from the news (any language) and reconstruct its
  missing grounds, warrant, and rebuttal — or show that it has none.
- **Communication access:** Every step that asks a learner to *name*, *say*, or *argue*
  can be completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC assemble, stress-test, and present
  the position in their preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name all six parts, assemble them on a
  claim, and state a rebuttal a fair opponent would recognize?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed position-architecture organizer, filed in
  the deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Find one claim someone at home makes often ("the weather is changing," "this is worth
the cost"). Ask them their grounds and their warrant, and write the claim, the reason,
and the bridge in one line each.
