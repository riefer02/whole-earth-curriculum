# Lesson 11 — Writing Across Disciplines, Ethically and Precisely

## Summary

Learners restate one factual claim for four different audiences and disciplines —
**scientific, civic, technical, and everyday** — choosing words that are both *precise*
and *ethical*. Clarity is a form of care; precision without kindness can confuse, and
care without precision can mislead. Mature writing holds both at once.

## Objectives

- Write with clarity, precision, and ethical care across disciplines and registers,
  adapting to diverse audiences in a multilingual world. _(D04.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

The same fact must be said four ways to be understood by four different people: a
scientist needs the exact measurement, an official needs the rule and its reason, a
technician needs the steps, and a neighbor — who may read your language as a second
language — needs the plain truth. Say it only one way and you either confuse the
scientist or lose the neighbor. The skill is not dumbing down or dressing up; it is
choosing, each time, the words that are *accurate* and *fair* to the person reading.

## Materials

- Cross-discipline writing organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Claim cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the cross-discipline organizer; prepare the claim cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, revision as re-seeing; from Lesson 4, the three registers.
  Today we add the capstone turn: *precision and ethics across disciplines* (worked
  example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the frame: precision and register are teachable writing skills (IES, S-171;
  Graham & Perin, S-102); accessibility — writing so people with different languages,
  abilities, and backgrounds can read it — is a standard worth teaching (WCAG, S-280).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
register and precision are teachable writing skills (IES, S-171; Graham & Perin, S-102),
and accessibility — making text understandable to people with different languages,
abilities, and backgrounds — is a documented standard (WCAG, S-280). Hold "clarity is
care" and "using language ethically" as *values*; hold "register shifts with audience"
as a *skill*. In a multilingual classroom, honor that a learner may write most precisely
in their home language, and that writing in a second or third language is a strength, not
a deficit.

The **ethics** lens: language can confuse or harm — jargon excludes, euphemism hides, and
a careless word can mislead a reader who trusts you; ethical writing asks "who will read
this, and what do they need?" The **egalitarianism** lens: plain language is an
equalizer — it lets a neighbor, a non-specialist, and a second-language reader stand on
the same ground as an expert. The **global** lens: what is "clear" differs across
languages and cultures, so precision is re-negotiated in every language, not imported
from one. The **technology** lens: technical writing is the written form of technology,
and digital text can be made accessible — structured headings, captions, readable type —
so the tool itself is a site of care. The **environment** lens: environmental claims
(measurements, risks, predictions) are exactly where precision and honesty matter most,
because overstatement or vagueness can misdirect decisions about the living world. The
**critical-thinking** lens: "is this both accurate and fair?" is a two-part test run on
every sentence. Preview: Lesson 12 turns from writing to *advocacy* — language justice.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what does each register ask of a reader? Today we
   write *for* those readers, precisely and ethically.
2. **Meet the tension (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Take one claim —
   *the well water shows elevated nitrate* — and restate it four ways: **scientific**
   (the measured concentration), **civic** (the rule and what it means for families),
   **technical** (how to test and treat), and **everyday** (plain truth for a neighbor).
   Notice where *precision* risks *confusion*, and where *care* risks *vagueness*.
3. **Restate a claim (15 min).** Take a claim card and fill the organizer: restate the
   claim for the four audiences. Under each, mark the one word choice that is most
   *precise* and the one that is most *caring*.
4. **Test for understanding (10 min).** Read — or present in writing, sign, typing, or
   AAC — your "everyday" version to a partner as if they were the neighbor. Ask — aloud or
   in writing, sign, typing, or AAC: *did you understand it? is anything still jargon? does
   it say exactly what is true?*
5. **Rewrite the jargon (8 min).** Take one jargon-heavy sentence (your own or a given
   one) and rewrite it into plain language *without losing accuracy*. Mark what you kept
   and what you traded.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why "precise but cruel"
   and "kind but vague" are both failures, and what mature writing does instead.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Restate the claim for only two audiences (everyday and one other), and
  focus on the plain-language version.
- **Extension:** Rewrite a real technical or legal sentence into plain language, then
  check the rewrite with a reader and note any accuracy that was lost or kept.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, testing, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC restate, test, and share in their preferred mode;
  no step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Writing access (dyslexia, low-vision, spelling difficulty):** Restatements may be
  produced by voice — dictated to a scribe or recorded with speech-to-text where a device
  exists — and read back by a partner or screen reader (low-tech fallback: a partner or
  facilitator reads the claim and scribes the restatement, or the learner traces key words
  finger-by-finger). Spelling and decoding difficulty is distinct from precision and thinking:
  a learner who spells non-standardly may restate a claim with exact, ethical care, and no
  "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is required — what is assessed is the precision
  and care of the wording, not the letter forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner restate a claim across four audiences with
  precision and care, and rewrite jargon into plain language without losing accuracy?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed cross-discipline organizer, filed in the
  deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Find one sentence at home that is hard to understand — a label, a notice, a form — and
rewrite it in plain language. Read both versions to someone — or share them in writing,
sign, or AAC — and see which they understand.
