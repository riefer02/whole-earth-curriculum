# Lesson 5 — Integrating Texts Across Disciplines

## Summary

Learners build a **synthesis matrix** — sources as rows, key questions as columns — and
weave several texts from different **disciplines and registers** into one supported
position. They learn to **corroborate** across sources, name where sources agree and
disagree, and notice **whose voice is missing**, so that integration is honest rather
than a stack of quotations.

## Objectives

- Integrate several scholarly, technical, and literary texts across disciplines into one
  supported position, corroborating evidence and noting omission. _(D04.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

Any real question is answered by more than one kind of text: an economist counts the
cost, a technician measures the water, a poet names what the drought feels like, and a
reporter asks who is affected. Read alone, each is partial — true but thin. The mature
reader does not just read them all; she *integrates* them, noticing where they reinforce
each other, where they conflict, and whose voice never made it onto the page. That
integration is the difference between collecting quotes and understanding.

## Materials

- Synthesis matrix
- Deliberation journal
- Source card set (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the synthesis matrix; prepare the source card set.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, the three registers and what each asks of a reader. Today we
  add the capstone turn: *integration across disciplines* (worked example first:
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare a plain definition: **synthesis** is combining sources into a new, supported
  position; **corroboration** is checking one source against another; **omission** is
  what a set of sources leaves out. Source evaluation — asking who created a text and
  with what purpose — is a core media- and information-literacy habit (UNESCO MIL,
  S-217; Center for News Literacy, S-278; Common Sense Education, S-063).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
integrating across texts is a teachable comprehension-and-synthesis skill (IES, S-170;
Duke & Pearson, S-172), and honest integration requires asking where each source comes
from and what it is *for* (UNESCO MIL, S-217; Center for News Literacy, S-278; Common
Sense Education, S-063). Hold "weigh evidence from many sources" as a *method*, while
"every voice deserves to be heard" is a *value* — the two are distinct and both matter.

The **ethics** lens: fair integration means not letting the loudest or most convenient
source drown out the others, and naming your own position as *yours*. The
**egalitarianism** lens: ask whose voice is missing from the source set — often the
people most affected — and whether a "complete" picture can be built without them. The
**global** lens: sources come from specific places and positions; a source set that
draws from only one region or one tradition is not neutral. The **technology** lens:
search engines and databases shape *which* sources you find, so the tool itself
influences the synthesis — notice that. The **environment** lens: use a contested
environmental issue (a dam, a forest road, a drought) as the shared question, so
integration is practiced where ecological and human evidence must be weighed together.
The **critical-thinking** lens: a synthesis matrix is a machine for spotting agreement,
conflict, and absence rather than for smoothing them over. Preview: Lesson 6 turns from
integrating texts to how *writing systems themselves* encode meaning.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what does each register — scholarly, technical,
   literary — ask of a reader? Today we hold several such texts together.
2. **Meet the matrix (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Lay out a small
   matrix: sources as rows (an economic note, a technical measurement, a literary
   account, a news report), key questions as columns (what is the problem, what is the
   evidence, who is affected, what should be done). Fill one row together, naming how
   each source answers each question — or stays silent on it.
3. **Fill the matrix (15 min).** With a partner, read — or listen to read back, or hear
   via text-to-speech — the source card set and fill your matrix. Mark cells where sources
   **agree**, where they **conflict**, and where a cell is **empty** because no source
   speaks.
4. **Notice the omission (8 min).** Look at the empty cells and the missing rows. Write
   one sentence naming whose voice or what kind of evidence is absent, and how that
   shapes the picture.
5. **Synthesize (10 min).** Write a two-to-three-sentence synthesized position that
   names the agreement, the conflict, and the omission — and states your own position
   as "my position," not "the facts."
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — in one sentence, the
   difference between *collecting* sources and *integrating* them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only three sources and two questions, and write one sentence of
  synthesis that names agreement alone.
- **Extension:** Add two digital sources to the matrix and revise the synthesis to
  account for what they add or contradict.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — discussing, naming, or closing aloud — can
  be completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC read, sort, and synthesize in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Reading access (dyslexia, low-vision, decoding difficulty):** Sources may be read by
  listening — a partner or screen reader reads them, or the learner uses text-to-speech
  where a device exists — with sources available in the learner's home script or as audio
  when possible (low-tech fallback: a peer or facilitator reads aloud, or the text is
  traced finger-by-finger). Spelling and decoding difficulty is distinct from comprehension
  and thinking: a learner who decodes slowly may still weigh, compare, and synthesize
  sources fully, and no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is required — what is
  assessed is the synthesis and the noticing of omission, not the letter forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner fill a synthesis matrix, name agreement,
  conflict, and omission, and state a synthesized position marked as their own?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed synthesis matrix and the synthesized
  position, filed in the deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Ask two people at home the same question and notice where they agree, where they
conflict, and what neither mentions. Write one line for each of the three.
