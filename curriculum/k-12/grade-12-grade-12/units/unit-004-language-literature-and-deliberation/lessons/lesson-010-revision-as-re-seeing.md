# Lesson 10 — Revision as Re-Seeing

## Summary

Learners run three **revision passes** over their draft — **structure, style, and
evidence** — and give and use **peer feedback** that serves the work, not the writer's
feelings. Revision is **re-seeing** (changing what the piece says and how it is built),
distinct from editing (correcting surface errors), and feedback is one of the strongest
levers for improving writing.

## Objectives

- Revise a sustained draft for structure, style, and evidence, using and giving feedback
  that serves the work. _(D04.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

The first version of anything you write is a first guess at what you meant. Revision is
not punishment for a bad guess; it is the chance to make the piece say what you actually
intended. Skilled writers expect to revise, and they know the difference between
**re-seeing** — does this paragraph belong here? is this the right word? does the evidence
hold? — and **editing** — is this spelled right, is the punctuation clean? Both matter,
but re-seeing comes first, because there is no point polishing a sentence you are about
to cut.

## Materials

- Revision checklist
- Deliberation journal
- Colored marks (low-tech: three pens or three symbols)

## Preparation

- Copy the revision checklist; prepare the three-pass marks.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, the paragraph-as-argument; from Grade 11 (D04.S3.11.01),
  extended original writing. Today we add the capstone turn: *revision as re-seeing*
  (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the revision frame from the writing process (IES, S-171; Graham & Perin, S-102):
  **revision** (structure, style, evidence) precedes **editing** (surface correctness).
  Feedback is among the strongest documented influences on learning (Hattie, S-012).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
revision — changing structure, style, and evidence — is a distinct, teachable stage of
the writing process, preceding editing (IES, S-171; Graham & Perin, S-102), and feedback
is among the strongest documented influences on learning (Hattie, S-012). Hold "feedback
serves the work" as a *value* and a *skill*, not a personal verdict. Be explicit that a
peer's first duty is to describe what the piece does, then to suggest — and that the
writer keeps the final say.

The **ethics** lens: honest feedback is a form of respect — soft-pedaling a real problem
does the writer a quiet harm; so does cruelty. The **egalitarianism** lens: peer revision
means every learner is both author and editor, which distributes the power of the red pen
rather than concentrating it in one teacher. The **global** lens: what counts as "clear"
or "well-structured" differs across traditions — a peer should ask what the writer was
aiming for, not impose one shape. The **technology** lens: comments and track changes let
feedback happen asynchronously and across distance, and let the writer accept or reject
each suggestion — but the *judgment* of what to change remains human. The **environment**
lens: revising on a screen before printing is a low-waste choice, and precise language
matters most when a piece describes environmental evidence where overstatement misleads.
The **critical-thinking** lens: "would the evidence still support the claim if I removed
my weakest source?" is the rigor a reviser applies. Preview: Lesson 11 writes *across*
disciplines and registers with precision.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9: what four parts make a paragraph an argument?
   Today we re-see the whole draft, not just its parts.
2. **Meet the three passes (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). On a shared
   draft, run three passes with three marks: **structure** (does each section earn its
   place? does the order build?), **style** (is each sentence doing work? is any word
   imprecise?), **evidence** (does every claim carry support? is any source weak or
   overused?). Name what each pass changes.
3. **Peer review (15 min).** Trade drafts with a partner. Using the checklist, mark the
   three passes on *their* draft — describing first, then suggesting. Be specific: name
   the paragraph, the sentence, the claim.
4. **Revise your own (12 min).** Take back your draft and act on what serves the work.
   Make at least one real change in each pass, and note *why* you made it.
5. **Distinguish revision from editing (8 min).** List the changes you made as either
   "re-seeing" (structure, style, evidence) or "editing" (spelling, punctuation). Which
   changed the *meaning*, and which only cleaned the surface?
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — in one sentence, what
   your piece says *now* that it did not say before you revised.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Run only the evidence pass and one editing pass, leaving structure and
  style for later.
- **Extension:** Write a short "revision memo" stating what changed, why, and what you
  chose *not* to change despite feedback.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — giving feedback, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC give and receive feedback in written or signed
  form and revise in their preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Writing access (dyslexia, low-vision, spelling difficulty):** The draft may be read back
  by a partner or screen reader, and revisions made by voice — dictated to a scribe or
  recorded with speech-to-text where a device exists (low-tech fallback: a partner or
  facilitator reads the draft and scribes the changes, or the learner traces key lines
  finger-by-finger). Spelling and decoding difficulty is distinct from revision and thinking:
  a learner who spells non-standardly may re-see structure, style, and evidence superbly, and
  no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is required — what is assessed is the
  re-seeing of the work, not the surface of the letters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner run three revision passes, give specific
  feedback that serves the work, and act on feedback with reasons?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The marked draft and the revised version, filed in the
  deliberation journal to show the arc from draft to revision.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to read one paragraph of your revised piece and tell you the one
place it is still unclear. Decide whether to change it, and why.
