# Lesson 1 — The Deliberative Stance

## Summary

Learners separate two postures toward disagreement: **arguing to win** and
**deliberating to understand and decide together**. They adopt four **talk moves** that
keep a discussion open — *restate, question, add, weigh* — and practice them on a real
contested issue. Deliberation is presented as a skill with a long, global history, not
as one culture's invention.

## Objectives

- Engage in sustained deliberation on a contested issue, hearing many voices and moving
  toward a reasoned position rather than a scored victory. _(D04.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last argument you were in — online or in person. Chances are each side was
trying to *win*: to land the better line, to have the last word. Now think of a decision
a group has to make that affects everyone, and cannot be undone cheaply: how a shared
water source is divided in a dry season, whether a road or a forest should come first,
how a clinic should spend a thin budget. Winning that argument does not solve the
problem; hearing everyone and deciding *together* does. That different posture has a
name — **deliberation** — and it is a skill, not a temperament.

## Materials

- Deliberation journal
- Deliberation-circle diagram
- Issue slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the deliberation-circle diagram; prepare the issue slips.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D04.S1.11.01), listening across accents, dialects, and
  viewpoints and naming assumptions; from Grade 8 (D04.S1.08.02), structured debate.
  Today we add the capstone turn: *deliberation* — hearing many voices toward a reasoned
  position (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare a plain definition: **deliberation** is reasoning *together* on a contested
  issue — hearing many voices, weighing reasons, and reaching a position — as distinct
  from **debate**, which is scored by winning, and from **consensus**, one possible
  *outcome* of deliberation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
structured discussion of contested issues — with reasons and genuine engagement with
opposing views — is a core, learnable civic skill (Hess, S-349), and productive
discussion rests on named "talk moves" that build on others' ideas and disagree
respectfully (Michaels & O'Connor, S-238). Deliberation is **not** a single culture's
invention: consensus-based councils are a living, centuries-old way of deciding among
the Haudenosaunee, whose Grand Council deliberates until members agree (S-142). Offer
this as one tradition among many, never as "the original" or the only model.

The **ethics** lens: deliberation asks not only "who has the better case?" but "what
should we, together, do — and what are the consequences for others?" The
**egalitarianism** lens: deliberation is only honest if the *quieter* voices are heard,
not only the loudest or most fluent. The **global** lens: ways of deciding together
differ across cultures; no single one is the default. The **technology** lens: much of
today's disagreement happens in fast, flattened online threads, where the deliberation
moves are harder to practice — notice that. The **environment** lens: many of the
hardest contested issues (water, forests, energy) are about shared environmental goods,
so deliberation is an environmental skill too. The **critical-thinking** lens: "restate
before you answer" is a test you run on your *own* hearing, not a verdict on a person.
Preview: Lesson 2 builds the architecture of a reasoned position.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: what does it mean to listen *across* a difference
   — an accent, a dialect, a viewpoint — instead of listening only to confirm your own?
2. **Meet the two postures (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011): two people
   disagree about whether a village road or a wetland should come first. In *arguing to
   win*, each interrupts, exaggerates, and scores points. In *deliberating*, each first
   **restates** the other's point, then **questions**, then **adds**, then **weighs**
   reasons toward a decision both can live with. Name the four moves and what each one
   does to keep the discussion open.
3. **Practice the moves (12 min).** In a circle of four, take up one issue slip. Each
   speaker must begin by restating the previous speaker's point before adding their own
   — or restate that point in writing, sign, typing, or AAC, then add the next one. Use
   the moves deliberately; a recorder notes which moves open the discussion and which
   close it.
4. **Weigh toward a position (10 min).** After several rounds, stop and ask: *what
   reasoned position is emerging?* Try to state it — aloud, or in writing, sign, typing,
   or AAC — in one sentence that names the strongest reason on each side, then the
   position the group now leans toward.
5. **Log it (8 min).** In your deliberation journal, write the issue, the voices you
   heard, and the emerging reasoned position — marked clearly as "tentative," not final.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — one sentence stating
   the difference between *winning an argument* and *deliberating*, and which move you
   found hardest to do honestly.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the two simplest moves (restate, add) and deliberate on one
  low-stakes issue, leaving weighing for later.
- **Extension:** Analyze a recorded public consultation: which talk moves appear, whose
  voices are missing, and how that shapes the position reached.
- **Communication access:** Every step that asks a learner to *say*, *restate*, or
  *speak* can be completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead — including the
  turn-taking restate and the closing one-sentence summary. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC take every turn in their preferred mode; the
  deliberation circle runs fully in written or signed form, and no step requires producing
  or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four moves, restate before
  answering, and state a tentative reasoned position that names reasons on both sides?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The annotated deliberation-circle diagram and the first
  issue-log entry, opened in the deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a decision the family or community made together and how
everyone was heard. Write one sentence about how the decision was reached and one about
whose voice might have been missed.
