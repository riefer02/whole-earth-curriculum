# Lesson 4 — Reading Across Registers

## Summary

Learners read one idea expressed in three **registers** — **scholarly, technical, and
literary** — and name what each register is *for*, how each is *structured*, and what
each *asks of a reader*. They learn to shift lenses so that a scholarly claim, a
technical procedure, and a literary image are each read on their own terms, then held
together as one fuller understanding.

## Objectives

- Read, evaluate, and integrate scholarly, technical, and literary texts by recognizing
  each register's purpose, structure, and demands. _(D04.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

The same truth arrives in different clothes. "Water is scarce here" can come as a
scholar's measured claim with citations, as a technician's steps for testing a well and
a table of rainfall, and as a poet's image of cracked earth waiting. If you read all
three the same way — hunting only for a fact, or only for a feeling — you misread at
least one of them. Mature reading is knowing which lens each register calls for, and
then holding all three together to understand the thing fully.

## Materials

- Three-register reading organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Short text trio (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-register organizer; prepare the short text trio.
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D04.S2.11.01), comparing texts across languages and
  translations; from Grade 10 (D04.S2.10.02), reading extended complex texts. Today we
  add the capstone turn: *registers* across disciplines (worked example first: Kirschner,
  Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare a plain definition: a **register** is a variety of language tied to a setting
  and purpose — here **scholarly** (argues with evidence and citation), **technical**
  (instructs or specifies with precision), and **literary** (evokes and imagines through
  form and image). Explicit comprehension-strategy and text-structure instruction is
  evidence-supported for adolescent readers (IES, S-170; Duke & Pearson, S-172).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
texts differ by register, and reading each on its own terms — recognizing its structure
and purpose — is a teachable, evidence-supported comprehension skill (IES, S-170; Duke &
Pearson, S-172). The three registers here are **one useful map**, not the only one; other
traditions sort texts differently, and no single culture's categories are the default.
Emphasize that a learner may meet all three registers in **any** language — their home
language first.

The **ethics** lens: reading a text on its own terms, rather than forcing it into the
reader's, is a form of fairness to the author. The **egalitarianism** lens: scholarly
registers can exclude by jargon; a mature reader notices who a register lets in and who
it shuts out. The **global** lens: what counts as "scholarly" or "literary" differs
across cultures and times — none is the default. The **technology** lens: the *technical*
register is the language of manuals, specifications, and interfaces — the written form
of technology itself — and reading it is a technical skill. The **environment** lens:
use an environmental idea (water, disease, migration) as the shared topic, so the three
registers are practiced on questions where the living world is at stake. The
**critical-thinking** lens: for each register ask "what is asserted, what is shown, and
what is left out?" Preview: Lesson 5 integrates several such texts into one position.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what stays the same when a speaker adapts their
   voice? Reading has registers too — today we name and shift among three.
2. **Meet the three registers (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Read three
   short passages on one idea — water scarcity. Name each: **scholarly** (a claim with
   evidence and a citation), **technical** (steps to test a well, a rainfall table), and
   **literary** (a two-line image of cracked earth). For each, say — or write, sign, type,
   or use AAC for — its *purpose*, its *structure*, and what it *asks of you* as a reader.
3. **Read the trio (15 min).** With a partner, read your own text trio on one idea and
   fill the organizer: for each register, the purpose, structure, evidence, and the
   reader's task.
4. **Compare the lenses (10 min).** Discuss — aloud, or in writing, sign, typing, or AAC:
   what did the technical register make visible that the poem hid, and the reverse? Write
   one sentence on what you understand about the idea *only* because you read all three.
5. **Name the demand (8 min).** For one register, write what a reader must do to read it
   well — and what a reader who lacks that skill would miss or misunderstand.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why "reading" is not
   one skill but several, and which register you find hardest and why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each passage to its register and name one purpose each, leaving
  comparison for later.
- **Extension:** Bring one authentic scholarly, technical, and literary excerpt on the
  same topic (any language) and analyze the register of each in depth.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, discussing, or closing aloud — can
  be completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC read and respond in their preferred mode; no step
  requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Reading access (dyslexia, low-vision, decoding difficulty):** Reading may be done by
  listening — a partner or screen reader reads a passage, or the learner uses text-to-speech
  where a device exists — with the same texts available in the learner's home script or as
  audio when possible (low-tech fallback: a peer or facilitator reads aloud, or the text is
  traced finger-by-finger and heard syllable by syllable). Spelling and decoding difficulty
  is distinct from comprehension and thinking: a learner who decodes slowly may fully
  understand and evaluate a register, and no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is
  required — what is assessed is understanding of each register, not the letter forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three registers, state each one's
  purpose and structure, and describe what each asks of a reader?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed three-register organizer, filed in the
  deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Find three things at home about the same topic — a label or manual (technical), a story
or song (literary), and something informative (scholarly). Name which is which and what
each is for, in one line each.
