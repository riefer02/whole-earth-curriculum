# Lesson 8 — Planning a Sustained, Polished Piece

## Summary

Learners plan a **sustained, polished piece** before drafting a word: choosing an
**audience** and **purpose**, stating a **thesis**, mapping **sections**, and listing the
**evidence** they will need. Planning is framed not as delay but as care — the difference
between a pile of thoughts and a piece with a spine.

## Objectives

- Plan a sustained, polished piece of writing by choosing audience and purpose, stating a
  thesis, and mapping structure and evidence. _(D04.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

A long piece of writing is not one big act; it is a small act repeated many times, and
the hardest problem is not filling pages but keeping a *direction*. A writer who plans —
who knows the audience, the purpose, the one-sentence spine, and the evidence needed —
writes faster, revises better, and finishes more often. The plan is not the piece; it is
the scaffold the piece is built on, and it is a skill you can learn and reuse for a
lifetime of writing.

## Materials

- Writing plan organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Topic slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the writing plan organizer; prepare the topic slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, integrating sources into a synthesized position; from Lesson
  2, the parts of a reasoned position. Today we begin the writing strand with *planning*
  (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the writing-process frame: **plan → draft → revise → edit → publish**, with
  explicit strategy instruction, which is evidence-supported for adolescent writers
  (Graham & Perin, S-102; IES secondary writing guide, S-171).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
the writing process — plan, draft, revise, edit, publish — with explicit strategy
instruction is a well-supported pedagogy for adolescent writers (Graham & Perin, S-102;
IES, S-171). Hold the *process* as evidence-based practice, and the *audience-first
ethic* as a value. Emphasize that the plan serves the writer, not the reverse — a plan
that outlives its usefulness is to be revised, not obeyed.

The **ethics** lens: choosing an audience is a promise — the piece owes that audience
clarity and honesty, not just style. The **egalitarianism** lens: a plan makes a long
piece *doable* in steps, which means sustained writing is not reserved for the
naturally fluent. The **global** lens: what counts as a well-structured piece differs
across cultures and traditions — a plan can honor many structures, not one. The
**technology** lens: digital outlines and citation tools are scaffolds, but the *thinking*
(what to say, to whom, why) is not automatable — the tool serves the plan. The
**environment** lens: invite topics that weigh human and ecological goods, and note that
a piece's *form* (paper, screen, spoken) carries a small environmental footprint worth
choosing deliberately. The **critical-thinking** lens: "what evidence will I need, and
where will it come from?" is a question of rigor asked *before* the writing, where it is
cheap. Preview: Lesson 9 turns the plan into a draft.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what six parts hold a reasoned position together?
   A sustained piece is a longer version of that position — today we plan it.
2. **Meet the plan (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Watch a plan take
   shape for one topic: *audience* (a community deciding about a local resource),
   *purpose* (to inform a decision), *thesis* (one sentence stating the position),
   *sections* (three or four), and *evidence needed* (which sources, which kinds). Name
   each part and what it does.
3. **Choose and plan (15 min).** Choose a topic (or adapt one) and fill your own writing
   plan: audience, purpose, thesis, sections, evidence needed. Be specific — name the
   audience as real people, not "everyone."
4. **Peer-check the plan (10 min).** Trade plans with a partner. Ask — aloud, or in
   writing, sign, typing, or AAC: *is the thesis one sentence and arguable? do the sections
   follow from it? is the evidence listed enough, and does any part need a source you do
   not yet have?*
5. **Write the contract (8 min).** Write a one-paragraph "contract": this is my audience,
   this is my purpose, this is my thesis, this is my structure. Mark it as a plan you
   may revise.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why a plan makes a long
   piece more likely to be *finished*, not just started.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Plan with a single audience, a one-sentence purpose, and two sections,
  leaving thesis refinement for later.
- **Extension:** Write the scope — what the piece will deliberately *not* cover — and
  explain how that choice strengthens rather than weakens the piece.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — asking, checking, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC plan, peer-check, and present in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Writing access (dyslexia, low-vision, spelling difficulty):** The plan may be produced
  by voice — dictated to a scribe or recorded with speech-to-text where a device exists — or
  built with drawings and word cards instead of fluent sentences (low-tech fallback: plan
  aloud and have a partner or facilitator write it down, or trace key words finger-by-finger).
  Spelling and decoding difficulty is distinct from planning and thinking: a learner who
  spells non-standardly may plan superbly, and no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling
  is required — what is assessed is the audience, purpose, thesis, and structure, not the
  letter forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state an audience, purpose, and one-sentence
  thesis, map sections, and list the evidence needed?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed writing plan (the "contract"), filed in
  the deliberation journal as the seed of the sustained piece.

## Home connection

Tell — or write to, sign to, or type to — someone at home what you plan to write, and ask
them one question they would want your piece to answer. Add that question to your plan.
