# Lesson 7 — One Idea, Many Scripts

## Summary

Learners place one idea — **"mother,"** and a cup of **tea** — across many languages and
scripts, distinguishing a **shared root** (words that descend from one older word) from
**borrowing** (words that traveled along a route). They meet the moderate idea of
**linguistic relativity**: languages sort the world differently, so translation always
gains and loses — and a single word is a small map of history.

## Objectives

- Reason across languages and writing systems, tracing how one idea appears in many
  scripts through shared roots and borrowing, and how languages sort the world
  differently. _(D04.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

The word you use for your mother is not random. Across Europe and South Asia, "mother,"
"madre," "mère," "Mutter," and "mātā" all descend, through centuries, from one older
word — a word so old it was spoken before writing (S-103). And the word for that warm
drink in your cup split two ways: it spread *by land* as "chai" and *by sea* as "tea"
(S-169). Every word you speak is like this — a small, folded map of where your language
has been and whom its speakers met. Reading that map is a kind of literacy all its own.

## Materials

- Many-scripts organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Word family cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the many-scripts organizer; prepare the word family cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, the four writing-system types. Today we add the capstone
  turn: *one idea across many scripts and languages* (worked example first: Kirschner,
  Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the two core examples with their sources: the Proto-Indo-European root
  **\*méh₂tēr** "mother" and its reflexes — English *mother*, Spanish *madre*, French
  *mère*, German *Mutter*, Hindi *mātā* (via Sanskrit *mātár-*) — an *inferred* scientific
  reconstruction, held as a story not a revealed truth (Fortson, S-103). And the word for
  tea: Sinitic *cha* → *chai* (overland) versus Min Nan *te* → *tea/thé* (by sea) (WALS,
  S-169). The moderate idea that languages "sort the world differently" is linguistic
  relativity, held in its moderate, well-supported form (Sapir & Whorf, S-168; Everett,
  S-167).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
words descend from older words (shared roots) or travel between languages (borrowing).
The "mother" set descends from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root \*méh₂tēr —
an *inference*, marked honestly as such (Fortson, S-103). The tea/chai split is a real,
mapped isogloss: Sinitic *cha* traveled overland (→ chai), while Min Nan *te* traveled by
sea (→ tea/thé) (WALS, S-169). Linguistic relativity is held **only in its moderate,
well-supported form** — languages *sort* the world differently and shape habitual
thought — not the strong determinism form, which is largely rejected (Sapir & Whorf,
S-168). Treat "language shapes thought" as a *description to weigh*, and "every language
is a complete system, a wealth" as a *value*.

The **ethics** lens: describing a language as a "dialect" or "broken" version of another
is a value claim dressed as a fact; the honest description is that every variety is a
complete system. The **egalitarianism** lens: whose word for "mother" or "tea" is
treated as the "real" one is a question of power, not of linguistics. The **global**
lens: the tea/chai map is a miniature of world trade — overland caravans and sea routes
— and no single route is the default. The **technology** lens: digital type and Unicode
let all these scripts share one screen, and machine translation tries (imperfectly) to
carry the meaning across — both are technologies that shape how languages meet. The
**environment** lens: the tea plant (*Camellia sinensis*) is itself a botanical story —
one plant, domesticated in one region, spread by human trade until its name marks two
hemispheres; language here carries an environmental history. The **critical-thinking**
lens: "does this similarity show a shared ancestor or a borrowed word?" is a precise
historical question, not a guess. Preview: Lesson 8 turns from reading to *writing* —
planning a sustained piece.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: what does a mark stand for in an abugida? In a
   character script? Today we zoom out to whole *words* traveling across languages.
2. **Meet a shared root (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Read — or listen
   to read back, or hear via text-to-speech — the "mother" set — mother, madre, mère,
   Mutter, mātā. What they share is not chance: they descend from one older word, \*méh₂tēr,
   reconstructed by comparing them. Note the honesty: the older word is *inferred*, not found
   written anywhere.
3. **Meet a borrowed word (10 min).** Read — or listen to read back, or hear via
   text-to-speech — the "tea" set — tea, thé, chai, चाय (chāy), 茶 (chá). These did *not*
   descend from one ancestor; the drink and its word spread along two routes: overland as
   *cha* → chai, by sea as *te* → tea. Sort the words onto the two routes.
4. **Sort the cards (12 min).** With a partner, sort the word family cards into "shared
   root" and "borrowed along a route," arguing — aloud or in writing, sign, typing, or AAC
   — for each. What clue tells them apart?
5. **Name what languages sort differently (8 min).** Choose one idea — snow, rice, time,
   or kinship — and note how different languages cut it up differently. Write one
   sentence on what translation would *gain* and one on what it would *lose*.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — in one sentence, what
   a single borrowed word can tell you about the people who met and traded.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match the clearly similar "mother" words only, and trace one route (tea)
   on a map, leaving the relativity step for later.
- **Extension:** Trace a chosen word through an etymological reference and present its
   root or route to a partner, marking what is documented versus reconstructed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, arguing, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC sort, trace, and explain in their preferred mode;
  no step requires producing or hearing sound.
- **Reading access (dyslexia, low-vision, decoding difficulty):** Word sets may be read by
  listening — a partner or screen reader reads them, or the learner uses text-to-speech
  where a device exists — with words also shown in the learner's home script (low-tech
  fallback: a peer or facilitator reads the words aloud, or the learner traces each word
  finger-by-finger). Spelling and decoding difficulty is distinct from comprehension and
  thinking: a learner who finds written word forms hard to decode can still reason about
  shared roots and borrowing, and no "correct penmanship" or standard spelling is required
  — what is assessed is the historical reasoning, not the letter forms.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish a shared root from a borrowed
  word, sort examples with reasons, and state what translation gains and loses?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed many-scripts organizer, filed in the
  deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a word in their language and a similar word in a neighboring
language. Find out together whether it is a shared root or a borrowed word, and write
one sentence about what you found.
