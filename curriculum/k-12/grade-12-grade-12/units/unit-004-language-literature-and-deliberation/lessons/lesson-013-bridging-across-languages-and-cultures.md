# Lesson 13 — Bridging Across Languages and Cultures

## Summary

Learners distinguish **translation** (written, revisable) from **interpretation** (spoken,
in the moment), and build a personal **plurilingual toolkit** for bridging across
languages and cultures. Bridging carries *meaning* — not word-for-word — across a gap, and
is an act of respect: it honors both languages as complete, and the person between them
as a skilled mediator.

## Objectives

- Bridge between speakers of different languages and cultures, translating and
  interpreting with accuracy, confidence, and respect. _(D04.S4.12.02)_

## Connection

You have been a bridge without calling it that: explaining a form to an elder, ordering
for a friend, telling a joke that does not quite translate, and finding the version that
does. Bridging is not "knowing two languages perfectly"; it is the skill of carrying
*meaning* from one person to another — written or spoken — while keeping both people's
dignity intact. Some bridges are built in writing and can be revised; others are spoken
and happen only once, in the moment. Both are learnable.

## Materials

- Bridging organizer
- Deliberation journal
- Bridging scenario cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the bridging organizer; prepare the bridging scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 12, language justice; from Grade 11 (D04.S4.11.02), interpreting
  main ideas across a language difference. Today we add the capstone turn: *confident,
  respectful bridging* (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the frame: **plurilingual competence** is the ability to draw on more than one
  language to communicate, including **mediation** — the literature's term for bridging
  (Council of Europe CEFR, S-237). **Translation** is written and revisable;
  **interpretation** is spoken and in the moment; **machine translation** helps with gist
  but is uneven across languages and cannot carry nuance (Britannica, S-350).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held as **description**:
plurilingual competence — drawing on several languages, including mediating between
speakers — is the literature's term for the bridging skill (Council of Europe CEFR,
S-237); translation (written, revisable) and interpretation (spoken, in the moment) are
distinct practices; and machine translation is a real but uneven tool, not a substitute
for a person (Britannica, S-350). The *value* — that both languages are complete systems,
and the person between them a skilled mediator — is a commitment, not a fact, and should
be named as such. Honor home languages first: a learner who bridges in their own languages
is an expert in this room, not a novice.

The **ethics** lens: a bridge carries both meanings and *responsibility* — an inaccurate
interpretation can mislead, and a careless one can wound; accuracy and care are the
bridge's two pillars. The **egalitarianism** lens: the bridge's job is not to make the
"weaker" language conform, but to meet both on equal ground. The **global** lens: a
lingua franca is one historical solution to many languages, but it is not the only one,
and plurilingual mediation is another — no single strategy is the default. The
**technology** lens: translation tools are fast and useful for gist, but they flatten
tone, idiom, and nuance and serve low-resource languages poorly — so a human bridge
remains necessary where it matters (S-350). The **environment** lens: languages carry
knowledge of the living world — plants, weather, seasons, places — so when a language is
lost, environmental knowledge can be lost with it (Kimmerer, S-427); bridging helps keep
that knowledge in circulation. The **critical-thinking** lens: "what did the tool get
right, and what did it flatten?" is the test a careful bridge runs on any machine output.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12: why is language justice a matter of safety and
   dignity? Today we become the *bridge* that justice requires.
2. **Meet translation and interpretation (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011).
   Read one sentence, then see it handled two ways: **translated** (written, revised,
   exact) and **interpreted** (spoken once, carrying the meaning and tone). Name what
   each can do and what each cannot.
3. **Plan a bridge (12 min).** Take a bridging scenario card and fill the organizer: what
   needs to cross (a question, a warning, a feeling), whether it is written or spoken,
   and your bridging strategy — including every language you can draw on.
4. **Practice the bridge (12 min).** With a partner, role-play the scenario. One of you
   speaks in one language — or writes, signs, or types it, in a slowed or simplified form —
   and the other bridges the meaning, aloud or in writing, sign, or AAC. Then switch.
   Notice what you carried and what you adjusted.
5. **Test the tool (8 min).** Compare a machine translation of one sentence against your
   own human version. Mark what the tool got, missed, and flattened.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — why bridging is an act
   of *respect*, not just of skill — and what you now know you can do as a bridge.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Bridge a single greeting or question, and name one thing you carried
   across accurately.
- **Extension:** Interpret a short, live exchange in front of the group — aloud, or in
   writing, sign, or AAC — then reflect in writing on what you kept, adjusted, and could
   not fully carry.
- **Communication access:** Interpretation here is not only spoken. A learner may bridge
  by writing, typing (AAC), or signing, and a d/Deaf or hard-of-hearing learner bridges
  between a signed and a written form, or between two written forms, just as fully as a
  spoken bridge. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC
  plan, practice, and perform the bridge in their preferred mode; the performance
  assessment is the carrying of meaning, not the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self + performance):** Can the learner distinguish translation from
  interpretation, plan and perform a bridge, and name what a machine tool gets and
  misses? The bridge may be performed aloud, in writing, in sign, or via AAC; what is
  assessed is the bridging of meaning, not the voice that carries it.
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed bridging organizer and a one-paragraph
  reflection on the practiced bridge, filed in the deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a word or saying that does not translate cleanly, and together
find the best you can do. Write the word, the attempt, and what was lost.
