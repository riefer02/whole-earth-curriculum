# Lesson 14 — The Many-Voiced Capstone

## Summary

Learners assemble the unit's artifacts into a **capstone portfolio** organized under its
four strands — **deliberation, reading, writing, and language justice** — and write a
closing reflection naming one idea from each strand they will carry forward. The lesson
is a **spaced-retrieval synthesis**: recalling the whole unit's ideas at once strengthens
them, and shows the learner the many-voiced whole they now command.

## Objectives

- Synthesize the unit's four strands into one capstone: deliberating, reading, writing,
  and advocating across languages with clarity, care, and integrity. _(D04.S1.12.01,
  D04.S1.12.02, D04.S2.12.01, D04.S2.12.02, D04.S3.12.01, D04.S3.12.02, D04.S4.12.01,
  D04.S4.12.02)_

## Connection

Over these four weeks you have done eight distinct things: deliberated, spoken in public,
read across registers, reasoned across scripts, planned, drafted, revised, and advocated
for language justice. Looked at one by one they seem separate. But they are one skill —
the skill of a person who can *hear many voices and speak with care in a many-voiced
world*. This final lesson is not new content; it is the act of seeing the whole, and
deciding what of it you will carry.

## Materials

- Capstone portfolio map
- Deliberation journal

## Preparation

- Copy the capstone portfolio map; set out a surface to assemble artifacts.
- Retrieval (spaced, across the whole unit): this lesson is a retrieval-practice and
  spacing payoff — recalling and organizing the unit's ideas strengthens long-term
  memory (Dunlosky et al., S-039). Prepare a one-line reminder of each strand's core idea.
- Prepare the four-strand frame: **deliberation** (hear many voices, reach a reasoned
  position), **reading** (evaluate and integrate texts, reason across scripts),
  **writing** (sustained, polished, precise, ethical), **language justice** (advocate for
  access; bridge across languages).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: the capstone is a
**spaced-retrieval synthesis** — recalling the unit's ideas at once, which is
evidence-supported for durable learning (Dunlosky et al., S-039) — and a **portfolio**
that makes the learning visible to the learner. It is summative in the sense of *showing*
mastery, never of *ranking*: a gap in the portfolio is information ("not yet assembled"),
not a verdict. Honor home languages first: the reflection may be written in whichever
language the learner thinks and feels in most clearly.

The **ethics** lens: the reflection asks not just "what did I learn?" but "how will I use
this to treat others fairly?" The **egalitarianism** lens: the portfolio belongs to the
learner — it is evidence of *their* growth, not a competition with anyone else's. The
**global** lens: the unit's through-line is multilingualism as wealth; the reflection
should name that the many voices are the point, not a problem. The **technology** lens:
a digital portfolio can be made accessible (captions, readable type, alt text), which is
itself an act of the language justice the unit taught. The **environment** lens: among
the unit's voices are voices for the living world — a deliberation about water, a warning
about a storm, a language that carries plant knowledge — and the reflection may name that
language serves the Earth as well as people. The **critical-thinking** lens: "which
artifact is weakest, and what would I do next?" is a self-check run with honesty, not
shame.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (10 min).** Without looking, name the unit's four strands and one thing you
   did in each. Then check yourself against the capstone portfolio map. (This recall is
   the point — it strengthens what you learned, S-039.)
2. **Assemble the artifacts (15 min).** Gather your eight artifacts — the deliberation
   log, the reasoned-position organizer, the speech map, the three-register organizer,
   the synthesis matrix, the writing-systems sheet, the many-scripts organizer, the
   writing plan, the draft, the revision, the cross-discipline organizer, the
   language-justice audit, and the bridging reflection — and sort them under the four
   strands. Where an artifact is thin, note it as "to revisit," not "failed."
3. **See the whole (10 min).** Stand back. What do the eight artifacts, together, say
   about the kind of communicator you are becoming? Write that in one sentence.
4. **Write the closing reflection (15 min).** For each strand, write one idea you will
   carry forward, in your own words and your own language. Then one sentence on how the
   four strands are really one.
5. **Share and receive (10 min).** In a pair or small group, show your portfolio map and
   read one line of your reflection — aloud, or in writing, sign, typing, or AAC. Leave
   each person one specific, appreciative note — naming a real thing you saw in their
   work.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — in one sentence, what
   it means to be a person who can hear many voices and speak with clarity, care, and
   integrity.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Assemble four artifacts (one per strand) and write one carry-forward idea
   for two strands, leaving the rest for a later pass.
- **Extension:** Write a short "capstone statement" addressed to a future reader —
   an employer, a community, a younger learner — explaining what you can now do as a
   communicator.
- **Communication access:** Every shared step — reading a line, or closing aloud — can be
  completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC share, receive notes, and reflect in their
  preferred mode; the reflection may be written, signed, or typed in the learner's own
  language, and no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio + peer + self):** Does the portfolio show evidence across all
  four strands, and does the reflection name one carry-forward idea from each strand with
  honesty and care?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed capstone portfolio map and the closing
  reflection — the unit's capstone.

## Home connection

Show someone at home your portfolio map and read them your one-sentence whole — or share
it in writing, sign, or AAC. Ask them which strand they see strongest in you, and write
down what they say.
