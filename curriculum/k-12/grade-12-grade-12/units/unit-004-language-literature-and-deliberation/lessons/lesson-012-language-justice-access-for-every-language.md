# Lesson 12 — Language Justice: Access for Every Language

## Summary

Learners audit a public communication — a warning, a sign, a form — for **who it reaches
and who it leaves out**, and draft an advocacy plan for **language justice**: making
public communication accessible to people of every language. They ground the advocacy in
documented facts while holding "every person deserves to be understood" as a *value*.

## Objectives

- Advocate for language justice by auditing public communication and making it accessible
  to people of all languages. _(D04.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

Imagine the storm warning is posted only in a language you do not read, or the clinic's
hours only in a script you never learned. You are not less capable — you are *excluded*,
not by any fault of yours, but by a decision someone made about whose language counts.
Language justice names that: public life should be legible to the people it serves, in
*their* languages. It is not a courtesy; it is a matter of safety, dignity, and fairness
— and it is something a person can advocate for, concretely.

## Materials

- Language-justice audit sheet
- Deliberation journal
- Public-notice samples (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy the language-justice audit sheet; prepare the public-notice samples.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, writing with precision and care; from Grade 11
  (D04.S4.11.01), evaluating language policies for equity. Today we add the capstone
  turn: *advocacy* (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).
- Prepare the documented facts to ground advocacy: about 40% of people globally lack
  access to education in a language they speak or understand (UNESCO, S-019); there are
  about 7,000 living languages, many endangered (Ethnologue, S-426). Hold these as
  *facts*; hold "every person deserves to be understood" as a *value*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, held carefully: the
*facts* — about 40% of people lack education in a language they speak (UNESCO, S-019),
and there are roughly 7,000 living languages, many endangered (Ethnologue, S-426) — are
documented and verifiable. The *value* — that public life should be legible to every
person in their language, and that exclusion by language is unjust — is a commitment,
not a fact, and should be taught as such. The lesson asks learners to argue *for* a value
using *facts*, which is exactly the evidence-versus-values distinction the curriculum
holds throughout.

The **ethics** lens: whose language is used in law, medicine, and school is a question of
power, and advocacy asks "who is being left out, and what should be done?" The
**egalitarianism** lens: language justice treats multilingualism as the normal human
condition, and monolingual public services as the problem to fix, not the other way
around. The **global** lens: language exclusion happens everywhere, and the fixes differ
by place — a multilingual sign, an interpreter, a plain-language rewrite — none is the
default. The **technology** lens: translation tools help but are uneven across languages
and cannot carry nuance, so they are a *partial* fix, not a substitute for human
translation and interpretation (machine translation's limits are documented — S-350).
The **environment** lens: the highest-stakes public communication is often environmental
— disaster warnings, water-quality notices, evacuation orders — so language justice is
an *environmental* justice issue too. The **critical-thinking** lens: the audit asks
"who can read this, and who cannot?" — a factual question that precedes any moral claim.
Preview: Lesson 13 practices *bridging* across languages; Lesson 14 assembles the
capstone.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11: what does it mean to write both precisely and
   ethically? Today that care moves from the page into *public life*.
2. **Meet the facts and the value (10 min).** Work a model example first (S-011). Read —
   or listen to read back, or hear via text-to-speech — the facts: about 40% of people lack
   education in a language they speak (S-019); there are about 7,000 living languages
   (S-426). Then state the *value* plainly: every person deserves to be understood in
   public life. Mark which is which.
3. **Audit a notice (15 min).** Take a public-notice sample and fill the audit sheet:
   *what does it say? who can read it? who cannot? what would change if it were in one
   more language? what would change if it were in plain language?*
4. **Rewrite for access (10 min).** Rewrite the notice so it reaches at least two more
   audiences — add a language, or plain language, or a pictogram — and keep the meaning
   exact.
5. **Draft the advocacy (10 min).** Write a short advocacy note: name the notice, the
   people left out, the change you propose, and the facts that support it. Mark your
   value claim as "I believe," and your facts as "the evidence shows."
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, type, or use AAC for — in one sentence, why
   language justice is a question of *safety and dignity*, not just courtesy.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** List the languages spoken in your own community and audit one notice for
  a single missing language.
- **Extension:** Draft a full advocacy plan — audience, ask, evidence, and the person or
  body to approach — and a first message to them.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, auditing, or closing aloud — can
  be completed in writing, signing, typing, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking,
  d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC read (or have read back, or hear via
  text-to-speech) the notices and advocate in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner audit a public notice for who it reaches
  and leaves out, rewrite it for access, and state a fact-based, value-marked advocacy
  position?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The audit sheet and the advocacy note, filed in the
  deliberation journal.

## Home connection

Find one public sign or notice in your area and list the languages it is written in.
Ask someone what language *they* would need it in, and write one sentence on what you
found.
