# Lesson 4 — Past, Present, and Possible Futures

## Summary

Learners connect the long human story to their own **agency**. They name what they **inherit** from
the **past**, what is happening in their **present**, and two or three **possible futures** they
could help make, then write the link between them. The lesson frames the story as **unfinished** —
not a fate to accept but a next step that is partly theirs to take — holding hope and agency
against despair.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own place in the long, unfinished human story, connecting the past to the
  present and to possible futures. _(D08.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Every future you have ever imagined stands on something you inherited and something happening right
now. A farmer imagines next season's crop from last season's soil and this week's rain; a student
imagines a career from a family's skill and a present opportunity. "Possible futures" is not a
dreamy abstraction — it is the ordinary, human way of standing between what was and what could be,
and noticing that you get a hand in the "could be."

## Materials

- Past–present–futures sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a partner-listener
- Enriched: a shared document or digital portfolio

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the past–present–futures sheet (three linked boxes + linking line).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, where the learner sits on the long story. Today we ask what *follows*
  from that place.
- Prepare a model reflection to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the human story is unfinished,
and "possible futures" is a concrete, ordinary act of agency — naming what I inherit, what is
happening now, and what I could help make.** Teach the three boxes explicitly (S-011). This lesson
must carry hope, never nihilism: the "unfinished" framing is empowerment (there is still time, and
the next step is partly ours), not dread. Name feelings before facts where the present is heavy,
and always connect difficulty to agency (facilitation, holding difficult topics safely).

The **ethics** lens: imagining futures is a moral act — some futures are more just than others, and
we can ask *which futures are worth wanting.* The **egalitarianism** lens: "possible selves" are
not a privilege of the rich — people everywhere imagine and work toward futures (S-399); the point
is that agency belongs to everyone, even when it is unevenly resourced. The **global** lens: the
same inherited past can seed many different futures in different places — a coastline facing the
sea, a dry region facing drought — and no single future is the only one. The **technology** lens:
tools narrow and widen futures at once — a phone connects a village to the world while a new dam
floods a valley; naming futures is naming which tools serve whom. The **environment** lens: the
Earth itself is the biggest "present" — what we do now narrows or widens which futures remain
possible for the living world (S-005). Preview: Lesson 5 turns to the many knowledge traditions
that different futures draw on.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — where you
   sit on the long story. Today: what *follows* from that place?
2. **Meet the three boxes (8 min).** Write them: **(1) Past — what I inherit** (a family skill, a
   language, a place, a debt or a gift from history); **(2) Present — what is happening now** (in
   my life, my community, the world); **(3) Possible futures — two or three I could help make.** A
   possible future is not a prediction; it is a *direction you could act toward*.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Read or hear a model: a learner who inherits a farming family,
   lives through a changing climate now, and names three futures — keep the farm and adapt it,
   teach others the soil, leave and return with new tools. Notice each box is concrete, and the
   futures *follow from* the past and present.
4. **Fill your sheet (18 min).** Write your own three boxes. Then write the linking sentence: *what
   I inherit and what is happening now point toward futures I could help make, and here is the
   first small step.*
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, share one future and your linking sentence. Ask:
   *does the future actually follow from the past and present you named?* Give one specific
   suggestion.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — why "unfinished" is a
   word of hope, not of failure.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sheet with starters ("I inherit…; right now…; one future I could help make
  is…"); fill one future rather than three.
- **Extension:** Mark which future depends on a present choice you can actually make, and write one
  sentence on the lever — the specific thing you could do this year.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; "two or three futures" is a loose
  count a learner may meet with any number, spoken or shown without numerals. The sheet is
  pre-structured so no ordering or counting is needed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, sharing, or closing — can be done in
  writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing,
  or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires producing or
  hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name an inheritance, a present condition, and at
  least one future that genuinely follows from them, with a linking sentence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed past–present–futures sheet, opened in the capstone
  history journal — the strand's "place in the story" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what future they once imagined and what they did about it. Write one sentence
on how their future grew out of their past and present, and one sentence on what that shows you
about your own.
