# Lesson 10 — How Migration Shaped the Human Story

## Summary

Learners **synthesize** how migration shaped the human story. They build one reasoned claim and
support it with three kinds of evidence: the **spread** of crops, animals, and technologies; the
mixing of languages, ideas, and beliefs; and the carrying of human **dignity and trauma** through
forced movement. The synthesis holds both spread and loss — never reducing migration to only its
gifts or only its wounds.

## Objectives

- Trace global movements of peoples and power across deep time and synthesize how migration has
  shaped the human story. _(D08.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

The rice in one bowl, the word for tea in another language, a song that crossed an ocean, a family
name that traveled a thousand miles — these are migration's fingerprints. Migration shaped the
human story not once but continuously: it moved the food we eat, the words we speak, and the people
we come from. A full account must say both what movement *gave* the world and what forced movement
*took* from the people it moved.

## Materials

- Migration-synthesis sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: scratch paper and the Lesson 9 map; a partner-listener
- Enriched: a shared document

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the migration-synthesis frame (claim + three kinds of evidence + "what migration
  keeps doing" line).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, the traced routes and the three questions. Today we assemble them into
  one synthesis.
- Prepare a model synthesis to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **migration shaped the human story
by spreading crops, animals, technologies, languages, and ideas — and by carrying both dignity and
trauma through forced movement; a full synthesis holds the spread and the loss together.** Teach the
frame explicitly (S-011). Guard against the one-sided tellings: neither "migration is only a gift of
exchange" nor "migration is only a wound." The mature synthesis says both, with evidence, and
centers the experience of the people moved.

The **ethics** lens: the same movement that spread a crop also carried enslaved people — naming the
trauma alongside the gift is an ethical requirement, not a mood. The **egalitarianism** lens: the
Columbian Exchange is often told from the colonizer's side; told from the side of the peoples and
lands it transformed, it is a story of both new foods and devastation (S-273) — and the enslaved
who carried a crop's knowledge across the ocean are authors of the story, not background (S-322,
S-274). The **global** lens: no region is untouched — the potato traveled from the Andes to become
a staple on other continents (S-054), and the exchanges went in every direction, not one. The
**technology** lens: what moved was not only people but *know-how* — weaving, metalwork, navigation
— which is why a migration is also a transfer of technology. The **environment** lens: moving crops
and animals between continents remade whole landscapes, for good and ill (S-273) — the human story
and the Earth's story are one migration. Preview: Lesson 11 turns from the story of peoples to
*your own* place in the movement of people and ideas.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9, name the movements you traced. Today: what is the one big
   claim they add up to?
2. **Meet the synthesis (8 min).** Write the frame: **Claim** — migration shaped the human story;
   **Evidence 1** — it spread crops, animals, and technologies; **Evidence 2** — it mixed languages,
   ideas, and beliefs; **Evidence 3** — it carried dignity and trauma, especially through forced
   movement; **So** — migration keeps shaping the story, now and next.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Read or hear a model: the potato left the Andes and fed other
   continents (S-054); the exchanges of the Columbian era moved plants, animals, and people across
   the Atlantic in both directions, with gifts and devastation (S-273); the transatlantic slave
   trade carried at least 12 million people by force, and their knowledge and resilience with them
   (S-322). Mark the three kinds of evidence.
4. **Write your synthesis (18 min).** Using the frame, write your own synthesis from your Lesson 9
   map. Name the spread *and* the loss, and say one thing migration keeps doing today (S-493,
   S-383).
5. **Revise with a partner (8 min).** Read your synthesis to a partner. Ask: *does it hold spread
   and loss together, with evidence for each?* Revise one sentence.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — why a full account of
   migration must name both what it gave and what it took.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame with starters ("Migration shaped the human story by…; it spread…; it
  mixed…; it also carried…"); fill the blanks with one example each.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth kind of evidence — the *resistance and resilience* of people under
  forced movement — and name one specific example.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The synthesis is qualitative — no arithmetic; the frame is
  pre-structured so the learner fills phrases, and the few figures ("12 million") are offered as
  background and may be spoken or shown without numerals. No counting or ordering is required.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the synthesis state a claim, support it with the spread *and*
  the loss, and name what migration keeps doing — while centering the people moved, not just the
  goods?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written migration synthesis, paired with the Lesson 9 map, in
  the capstone history journal.

## Home connection

Name one thing in your home or neighborhood that came from somewhere else — a food, a word, a tool,
a person. Write one sentence on how it got there, and one sentence on what else came with it.
