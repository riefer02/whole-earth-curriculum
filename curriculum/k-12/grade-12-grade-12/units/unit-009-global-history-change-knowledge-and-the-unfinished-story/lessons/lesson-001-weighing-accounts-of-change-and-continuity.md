# Lesson 1 — Weighing Accounts of Change and Continuity

## Summary

Learners meet the unit's core skill: **weighing** competing **accounts** of the same change.
They learn that two honest people can describe one change differently because one notices the
**change** and the other notices the **continuity** — and that weighing means asking who is
telling the account, what it notices and leaves out, and whether accounts agree. They practice
on the global shift from village life to city life, told from two sides.

## Objectives

- Weigh competing accounts of change and continuity across global history and form a reasoned
  synthesis of the evidence. _(D08.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

Ask two people who were there what happened when the road came through, when the factory
opened, when the family moved. One says "everything changed"; the other says "underneath, we
stayed the same people." Both are telling the truth. History is like that: it is not one
voice describing one change, but many voices, each noticing something real — and the skill is
learning to hold two tellings at once and ask, fairly, which parts are best supported.

## Materials

- Weighing-an-account sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: two written accounts of one change; scratch paper
- Enriched: two contrasting news reports or oral-history clips

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the weighing sheet; prepare two short, genuine accounts of one change (or use
  the lesson's worked example).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D08.S1.11.01), weighing what a long transformation gained and lost;
  from Grade 11 (D08.S1.11.02), how the choice of what counts as significant change shapes an
  account. Today we add the skill of *weighing accounts against each other*.
- Prepare the worked example to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **two accounts of one change
can both be true and still differ, because each weighs change against continuity differently —
and weighing means asking who is telling, what they notice, what they leave out, and where the
accounts agree.** Teach the weighing move explicitly (S-011), then let learners work a pair.
This is the "history from many perspectives" commitment made concrete: we never crown one
telling as the only true one, and we never pretend the differences away either.

The **ethics** lens: weighing is a duty of fairness — we owe every account a careful hearing,
and we owe the reader honesty about what we cannot settle. The **egalitarianism** lens: the
account of an elder in a village and the account of a planner in a capital are both evidence;
neither is discounted because of who speaks (S-417). The **global** lens: the village-to-city
shift is not one country's story — for the first time in history, more than half of humanity
now lives in cities, a crossing that happened only within recent decades (S-089), and it is
told differently in Lagos, Jakarta, and La Paz than in older industrial cities. The
**technology** lens: the tools of a change — the road, the rail line, the phone — appear in
some accounts as progress and in others as loss; both are real readings of the same machine.
The **environment** lens: an account that says "the land stayed the same" and one that says
"the river changed" can both be true about different scales of time — weigh the scale, not
just the words. Preview: Lesson 2 turns a weighing into a reasoned synthesis.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11, name one transformation you analyzed and one thing it
   *gained* and one thing it *lost* — for different people. Today we weigh *accounts*: the
   different tellings people give of the same change.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: a **change** is what became different; a
   **continuity** is what stayed the same. One event is usually *both* — and two honest
   accounts can disagree simply because one notices the change and the other notices the
   continuity. **Weighing** is not deciding who is "right." It is asking: *who is telling this,
   what do they notice, what do they leave out, and where do the accounts agree?*
3. **Worked example — village to city (15 min).** Read two short accounts of the same shift:
   a young person who moved to the city for work and calls it opportunity — new skills, a wage,
   electricity (S-089, S-052); and an elder who stayed in the village and calls it loss — the
   fields quieter, the young gone, but the songs and the planting still kept. Weigh them on
   your sheet: what does each notice? what does each leave out? Where do they *agree*? (Both
   are true; they notice different things.) Mark the change *and* the continuity in each.
4. **Name the move (5 min).** The weighing questions are a tool you can carry to any
   disagreement: **(1) sourcing** — who says this, and what do they know? **(2) perspective** —
   what do they care about and leave out? **(3) corroboration** — do independent accounts agree?
   (S-316).
5. **Weigh your own pair (15 min).** With a partner, weigh two accounts of one change — the
   lesson's pair, a local pair, or a historical pair you both know. Fill the sheet. Say — or
   write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — one place the accounts genuinely differ and one
   place they agree.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the three
   questions you ask whenever you weigh two accounts.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sheet pre-filled with the two accounts already summarized; the learner
  underlines what each notices and circles what each leaves out, then answers the "agree"
  question aloud or in writing.
- **Extension:** Name a place where the two accounts *directly contradict* each other and write
  what further evidence would help settle it — without pretending the contradiction away.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. Dates and population
  figures (e.g., "more than half of humanity is now urban") are offered on a pre-printed line
  or read aloud, never as a computation; a learner may complete the whole weighing in spoken or
  signed words without any numerals.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. Accounts may be provided in print, braille, or audio description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name what each account notices and leaves out,
  identify one point of agreement and one of difference, and use the three weighing questions?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed weighing sheet, opened in the capstone history
  journal as the first of the unit's "weighing" artifacts.

## Home connection

Ask two people at home about one change they both lived through — a move, a new road, a change
of work. Write one line for each: what did they notice, and what did they leave out? Then one
line on where their tellings agree.
