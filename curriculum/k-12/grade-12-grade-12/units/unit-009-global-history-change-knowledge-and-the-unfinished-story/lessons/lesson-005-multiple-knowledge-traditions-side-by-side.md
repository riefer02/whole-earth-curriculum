# Lesson 5 — Multiple Knowledge Traditions, Side by Side

## Summary

Learners practice setting multiple **knowledge traditions** side by side without ranking one above
another. They meet the idea that a **knowledge tradition** is a coherent, living way of asking and
answering questions — herbal medicine, scientific biomedicine, and Indigenous ecological
knowledge each ask different questions and offer different strengths — and they chart what each
**asks** and **offers**, then name how they relate, with dignity for all.

## Objectives

- Weigh multiple knowledge traditions side by side and articulate a respectful, non-dogmatic
  relationship among them. _(D08.S2.12.01)_

## Connection

For a fever, one person brews a plant their grandmother taught them; another takes a medicine a
doctor prescribed; a third knows that the fever is one sign in a whole season of a river's health.
All three are *knowing* — differently. The mature skill is not to declare one "the real knowledge"
and the rest superstition, but to set them beside each other and ask, without ranking, what each
asks and what each offers.

## Materials

- Side-by-side traditions chart
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: two or three written descriptions of knowledge traditions; scratch paper
- Enriched: an excerpt from Kimmerer's *Braiding Sweetgrass* or a local elder's account

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the side-by-side chart; gather two or three short, respectful descriptions of
  knowledge traditions (herbal medicine, biomedicine, Indigenous ecological knowledge, or a local
  set).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D08.S2.11.01), the relationship between dominant and marginalized
  knowledge traditions. Today we set traditions *beside* each other, as equals.
- Prepare the worked example (charting one set) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a knowledge tradition is a
coherent, living way of asking and answering questions, and the skill is to set several beside each
other without ranking — seeing what each asks, what it offers, and how they relate.** Teach the
chart explicitly (S-011). This is the inquiry-over-creed commitment made concrete (philosophy §11):
we do not rank traditions, and we do not pretend they are identical. Frame as *wondering together*;
use written or anonymous questions if speaking up is not safe (contexts, cultural power dynamics).

The **ethics** lens: ranking one people's knowledge above another's has been used to justify
suppression; refusing the ranking is an ethical stance, not a claim that nothing can be checked.
The **egalitarianism** lens: traditional medicine is a real, valued, living tradition, recognized
worldwide, used alongside — not beneath — other systems (S-145); Indigenous fire stewardship is a
long-standing practice documented by national science agencies as knowledge, not folklore (S-320,
S-321). The **global** lens: every named tradition here is a *living* practice carried by real
communities, recognized through living-heritage frameworks (S-056), never a specimen. The
**technology** lens: scientific instruments answer "how" with unmatched power, but they are silent
on "what it means" and "how we should live with it" — questions other traditions take up (S-427).
The **environment** lens: Indigenous ecological knowledge — when to burn, when to plant, how to
read a landscape — complements scientific ecology rather than contradicting it (S-320, S-321,
S-427). Preview: Lesson 6 asks learners to articulate the *relationship* between traditions
explicitly and non-dogmatically.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11, name one knowledge tradition that was marginalized or
   suppressed, and one thing it knew. Today we set traditions *beside* each other, as equals.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: a **knowledge tradition** is a coherent way a people
   ask and answer questions — through plants, instruments, story, apprenticeship, or prayer. Each
   tradition asks its *own* questions and offers its *own* strengths. **Side by side** means we
   refuse to rank them: we do not call one "real" and another "lesser."
3. **Worked example — three ways of knowing health and land (15 min).** Chart three traditions:
   **herbal medicine** (asks "what does this plant heal, and how is it passed on?" — S-145);
   **scientific biomedicine** (asks "what mechanism causes this, and what does the trial show?");
   **Indigenous ecological knowledge** (asks "how is this land and its people healthy together?" —
   S-320, S-321, S-427). For each, write what it asks and what it offers. Then the relation: they
   answer different questions; they can correct, inform, and complete each other — and none is
   lesser.
4. **Chart your own set (18 min).** With a partner, choose two or three knowledge traditions you
   can name respectfully — the lesson's set, a local pair, or another you both know. Fill the
   chart. Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — one place they complement and one
   place they genuinely differ.
5. **Name the relation (7 min).** Write one sentence of relation: do they answer different
   questions? does one carry facts and another carry meaning and duty? Be precise — no ranking, but
   no pretending they are identical either.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — what it means to hold
   several knowledge traditions side by side with dignity for all.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Chart only "what each tradition offers" before adding "what each asks."
- **Extension:** Name a case where two traditions appear to conflict (e.g., about a treatment or a
  landscape's history) and write how you would hold both honestly without pretending the difference
  away.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The chart is qualitative — no arithmetic; provide it pre-printed
  with the columns labeled so the learner fills phrases and single words, and allow the relation
  sentence to be spoken or recorded rather than written.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing — can
  be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner chart two or three traditions — naming what each asks
  and offers — and write one precise sentence of relation that neither ranks them nor erases their
  difference?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed side-by-side chart with the relation sentence, opened
  in the capstone history journal — a "knowledge traditions" artifact.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one way they know something — a remedy, a planting rule, a way of reading
weather. Write it beside a scientific account you know, and one sentence on how the two stand
together.
