# Lesson 13 — Weighing Accounts of Power and Resistance

## Summary

Learners weigh competing accounts of a contested historical event — the **Salt March** — where one
side named an act "resistance for dignity" and the other named it "defiance of lawful order." They
apply the weighing skill from Lesson 1 to questions of power and resistance, asking who held power,
who resisted and why, who benefits and who is harmed in each telling — and they form a reasoned
**judgment** while naming what stays unsettled, never asserting a single verdict.

## Objectives

- Weigh competing accounts of power and resistance in a contested historical event and form a
  reasoned judgment. _(D08.S4.12.01)_

## Connection

When a law is broken on purpose, two tellings appear at once: "they broke the law" and "they refused
an unjust order." Both describe the same act. The difference is not in the facts but in where each
telling stands — with the power that made the law, or with the people the law harmed. Weighing a
contested event means listening to both, asking who benefits and who is harmed in each, and forming
a judgment that is reasoned — not a cheer, not a shrug.

## Materials

- Power-and-resistance weighing sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: two written accounts; scratch paper
- Enriched: two primary-source excerpts

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the weighing sheet; prepare two genuine accounts of the Salt March (the colonial
  authority's and the marchers'), or a local contested event.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the three weighing questions (sourcing, perspective, corroboration);
  from Grade 11 (D08.S4.11.01), comparing social movements. Today we weigh power and resistance
  *against each other* in one contested event.
- Prepare the worked example to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **in a contested event, the account
of those holding power and the account of those resisting describe the same act from different
places; weighing them means asking who holds power, who resists and why, who benefits and who is
harmed in each telling — and then forming a reasoned judgment while naming what stays unsettled.**
Teach the weighing explicitly (S-011). Hold the Salt March accurately and with care: in 1930,
Mahatma Gandhi led a march to the sea to make salt in defiance of a British colonial salt tax, an
act of nonviolent resistance against colonial rule (S-087, S-205); the colonial account saw
lawlessness and disruption, the marchers' account saw a refusal of an unjust order. Present both as
genuine accounts — do not assert one verdict; the learner weighs and judges.

The **ethics** lens: the judgment is a *reasoned position*, and the lesson models that a judgment
can be firm *and* admit what it cannot settle — firmness without humility is dogma. The
**egalitarianism** lens: power is not only force — it is also the ability to set the agenda and to
shape what counts as "lawful" (S-275), which is why the account of those holding power can sound
neutral while still serving power. The **global** lens: the Salt March is one of many such moments —
the Defiance Campaign in South Africa, the Montgomery bus boycott, the Aba Women's War — where
ordinary people resisted unjust rule across continents (S-146, S-324, S-209); each is weighed in its
own terms, not flattened into one template. The **technology** lens: how an event is recorded —
whose account gets printed, broadcast, or archived — shapes whose telling survives; weighing means
asking whose voice reached us and whose did not. The **environment** lens: many resistances are also
defenses of land and water — the Chipko movement held trees against felling (S-328); the salt of the
sea was, for the marchers, a resource the land and coast freely gave, taxed by a distant power.
Preview: Lesson 14 turns the judgment into a reflection on one's own role in the *unfinished*
struggle for freedom and dignity.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, name the three weighing questions. Today we aim them at power
   and resistance in one contested event.
2. **Meet the frame (8 min).** Write it: **power** is the ability to decide, for oneself and for
   others — and it includes the power to set the agenda and define what is "lawful" (S-275).
   **Resistance** is refusing to accept that power when it harms. A **contested event** is one where
   the two sides tell it differently. Weighing asks: *who holds power? who resists and why? who
   benefits, and who is harmed, in each telling?*
3. **Worked example — the Salt March (15 min).** Read two accounts of 1930: the colonial authority's
   (the march broke the salt law and threatened order) and the marchers' (the salt tax was an unjust
   burden, and making salt was a refusal of it — S-087, S-205). Weigh them on your sheet: who holds
   power in each? who resists and why? who benefits and who is harmed in each telling? Where do they
   *agree* on the facts? Where do they part on the meaning?
4. **Form your judgment (12 min).** Write a reasoned judgment — a position, with its strongest
   evidence, crediting what the other account adds, and naming what stays unsettled. Do not assert a
   verdict; *argue* one.
5. **Share and check (8 min).** With a partner, read your judgment. Ask: *is it reasoned (evidence,
   not just feeling), and does it name what stays unsettled?* Give one specific suggestion.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — what weighing adds to a
   contested event that picking a side does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sheet pre-filled with the two accounts summarized; the learner underlines who
  benefits and who is harmed in each, then answers the judgment with a starter ("I judge that…,
  because…, though I still cannot settle…").
- **Extension:** Name a place where the two accounts *directly contradict* each other and write what
  further evidence would weigh more heavily — without pretending the contradiction away.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic. The one date (1930) is offered
  on the pre-printed sheet and read aloud, never required to be computed or ordered; the whole
  weighing and judgment can be done in words, spoken or signed, without any numerals.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, discussing, or closing — can be
  done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound. Accounts may be provided in print, braille, or audio description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner weigh two accounts of power and resistance — naming
  who holds power, who resists and why, and who benefits and is harmed in each — and form a reasoned
  judgment that names what stays unsettled?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed weighing sheet with the reasoned judgment, opened in
  the capstone history journal — the strand's "power and resistance" artifact.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time a rule was contested in your community. Write one line for each
side's telling, and one sentence for your own reasoned judgment — naming what you still cannot
settle.
