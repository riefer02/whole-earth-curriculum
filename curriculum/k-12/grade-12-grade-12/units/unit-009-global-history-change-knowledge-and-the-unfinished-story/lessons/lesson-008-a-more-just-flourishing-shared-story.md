# Lesson 8 — A More Just, Flourishing Shared Story

## Summary

Learners argue the connection between honoring many knowledge traditions and a more **just**,
**flourishing** shared story. They learn that a shared story is impoverished when one tradition
crowds out the rest — knowledge is lost, and whole peoples are told their knowing does not count —
and enriched when many traditions are honored. They write one reasoned claim with an example, and
answer the honest counter that a single "most efficient" tradition might be enough.

## Objectives

- Reflect on how honoring many knowledge traditions contributes to a more just and flourishing
  shared human story. _(D08.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

A garden with one crop feeds you; a garden with many crops feeds you *and* survives a bad season.
Human knowledge is like that garden. When many traditions are kept alive — many ways to heal, to
farm, to read a river, to remember — the shared story is more just (no one is told their knowing
does not count) and more able to flourish (there is more than one answer when one answer fails).

## Materials

- Flourishing claim sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a partner-listener
- Enriched: a shared document

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the flourishing claim frame (claim + because + example + who is left out otherwise).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, the four honoring acts and the cases. Today we argue *why* honoring
  matters for the whole shared story.
- Prepare a model claim to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **honoring many knowledge
traditions makes the shared human story more just and more able to flourish — because knowledge is
diverse, because telling a people their knowing does not count is an injustice, and because one
tradition alone leaves everyone poorer.** Teach the claim frame explicitly (S-011). Welcome and
answer the honest counter ("wouldn't one best method be more efficient?") — the answer is not that
efficiency is worthless but that a single tradition is also a single point of failure, and that
justice is not measured only by efficiency (S-460). Keep this a reasoned claim, not a slogan: the
learner must support it, not merely assert it.

The **ethics** lens: the claim is a *values* claim (a shared story *should* honor many traditions)
supported by *evidence* (diverse knowledge is more resilient) — model the distinction between
evidence and values explicitly. The **egalitarianism** lens: development understood as freedom —
people able to live the lives they value, in their own terms — is exactly what honoring many
traditions protects (S-460); suppressing a tradition is taking a capability away. The **global**
lens: the claim is universal, not one culture's — everywhere, when one tradition dominates, others
are lost, and everywhere, revival brings them back (S-428, S-056). The **technology** lens: tools
can centralize knowledge (one platform, one standard) or pluralize it (many archives, many
voices); the design of tools is a choice about whether the story stays many-voiced. The
**environment** lens: biodiversity and knowledge-diversity rise and fall together — when one crop,
one language, one way of burning is lost, the land loses a caretaker too (S-437, S-320). Preview:
Lesson 9 turns to the movements of peoples and power that have carried these traditions — and their
keepers — across the Earth.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, name one tradition and the four acts that honor it. Today:
   why does honoring *many* traditions matter for everyone, not just for that tradition's people?
2. **Meet the claim (8 min).** Write the frame: **Claim** — a shared story flourishes when many
   knowledge traditions are honored; **Because** — diverse knowledge is more resilient, and telling
   a people their knowing does not count is an injustice; **Example** — one concrete tradition and
   what it adds; **Otherwise** — who is left out, and what is lost, when one tradition crowds out
   the rest.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Read or hear a model: honoring both fire science and cultural
   burning keeps a landscape healthier than either alone, and refusing the Indigenous knowledge
   would be an injustice *and* a practical loss (S-320, S-321). Mark the four parts of the frame.
4. **Answer the counter (8 min).** Someone says: "wouldn't one best method be simpler?" Write an
   honest answer: efficiency matters, but one tradition is also one point of failure, and justice is
   not only efficiency — a people whose knowing is erased loses the freedom to live by it (S-460).
5. **Write your claim (15 min).** Using the frame, write your own argued claim. Support it with a
   real example; do not merely assert it.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one way the shared
   story becomes more just when many traditions are honored.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame with starters ("The shared story is more just and more able to
  flourish when… because… for example… and otherwise…"); fill the blanks.
- **Extension:** Add a second example from a different region, and mark which part of your claim is
  a value and which part is evidence.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the frame is qualitative and
  pre-structured, so the learner fills phrases and single words, and may speak or record the claim
  rather than write it.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the claim state *why* honoring many traditions helps the shared
  story flourish, support it with an example, and answer the counter honestly — while separating
  value from evidence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written flourishing claim, opened in the capstone history
  journal — the strand's "many traditions" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what would be lost if everyone farmed, cooked, or healed the same single way.
Write one sentence on what diversity of knowledge protects, and one sentence on your own part in
keeping a tradition alive.
