# Lesson 11 — My Place in the Movement of People and Ideas

## Summary

Learners locate themselves in the ongoing movement of people and ideas. They fill a sheet with a
family move, a **borrowed word** they use, a food or idea that reached them from elsewhere, and what
they **carry** forward — and they name, for each, whether the movement was chosen or forced. The
lesson makes migration personal and ordinary without flattening its harder forms.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own place in the ongoing movement of people and ideas, and their responsibility
  to a shared and changing Earth. _(D08.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

You do not have to have moved to be inside the movement. The word you say for "tea," the dish on the
table, the song you hum, the faith or skill your family keeps — each arrived from somewhere, carried
by someone. To find your place in the movement of people and ideas is to notice that your everyday
life is already a crossroads, and to ask, honestly, which of those arrivals were chosen and which
were forced.

## Materials

- My-place-in-movement sheet
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a partner-listener
- Enriched: a digital map or family-history tool

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the my-place-in-movement sheet (family move / borrowed word / food or idea / what I
  carry).
- Retrieval: from Lessons 9–10, the movements and the synthesis. Today we find *our own* place in
  that movement.
- Prepare a model reflection to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **everyone is inside the movement
of people and ideas — through family, language, food, and what we carry — and noticing this is the
first step to a considered relationship to migration.** Teach the sheet explicitly (S-011). Hold
two things gently: migration is ordinary (a word, a dish, a move), *and* some movement is forced —
for learners whose families fled or were displaced, this lesson must be a place of dignity, not of
being put on display. Offer the option to write about a community rather than a family, and never
require anyone to share a painful story. Distinguish chosen from forced movement with care (S-086).

The **ethics** lens: asking "chosen or forced?" about one's own history is an ethical honesty — it
is how we remember accurately rather than comfortably. The **egalitarianism** lens: a migrant is
not a category of *other* people — anyone can be one, and nearly everyone descends from one (S-086);
the word is descriptive, never a lesser status. The **global** lens: borrowed words are everywhere —
"tea/chai" marks two ancient trade routes, overland and by sea (S-169); our languages are already
world maps. The **technology** lens: today ideas move faster than people — a video crosses a border
in an instant — so "the movement of ideas" is no longer tied to the movement of bodies, which changes
what belonging means. The **environment** lens: some movement is toward safety from a changing
climate (S-383), and "what I carry" can include a care for the place I came from as much as the
place I am. Preview: Lesson 12 turns "my place" into a *responsibility* to a shared, changing Earth.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 9–10, name one thing migration spread. Today: find *your own*
   place in that movement.
2. **Meet the sheet (8 min).** Write the four lines: **(1) a family move** I know of (or a
   community move); **(2) a borrowed word** I use; **(3) a food or idea** that reached me from
   elsewhere; **(4) what I carry** forward from all of it. For each, mark **chosen** or **forced** —
   or say honestly that you do not know.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Read or hear a model: a learner whose family moved for work (chosen);
   who says "tea" (a word that traveled by sea — S-169); who eats rice that crossed oceans; and who
   carries a grandparent's recipe and a language half-remembered. Notice each line names the
   movement *and* the chosen/forced mark.
4. **Fill your sheet (18 min).** Write your four lines. If a line is painful or private, write about
   a community instead, or leave it blank — dignity first. Then write one sentence: *what does my
   place in the movement make me notice about migration itself?*
5. **Share and check (5 min).** Share one line with a partner (only what you wish). Ask: *does it
   name the movement and the chosen/forced mark?* Give one gentle suggestion.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing you carry that
   someone else carried to reach you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sheet with starters ("My family (or community) moved because…; I use the word
  … which came from…; the food … reached me from…; I carry…"); fill one line at a time.
- **Extension:** Trace the route of one borrowed word or food back as far as you can, and write where
  the idea changed as it moved.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the sheet is a list of four lines, not
  a computation, and dates are not required. A learner may complete every line in spoken or signed
  words without any numerals.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, sharing, or closing — can be done in writing,
  sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use
  AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.
  Sharing is always optional.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name a family or community move, a borrowed word, a
  food or idea, and what they carry — marking each as chosen or forced (or honestly unknown)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed my-place-in-movement sheet, opened in the capstone
  history journal — the strand's "place in movement" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one word, food, or song in your household that came from somewhere else.
Write one sentence on where it came from, and one on what it carried with it.
