# Lesson 3 — My Place in the Long Human Story

## Summary

Learners locate themselves on the **long human story**. They see — proportionally — that
anatomically modern humans have walked the Earth for roughly 300,000 years, that **writing** is
only about 5,000 years old, and that cities and electricity are younger still, and they place
their own one life on that **span**. The point is not to feel small but to feel *placed*: each of
us is a thread in a story no single generation ever finished.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their own place in the long, unfinished human story, connecting the past to the
  present and to possible futures. _(D08.S1.12.02)_

## Connection

Most of what you call "history" — the books, the dates, the named rulers — covers only the last
sliver of the human story. Before writing, thousands of generations of people lived, loved,
planted, mourned, and made decisions whose consequences reached you, without leaving a single
written word. When you place your own life on that longer line, your life stops looking like an
endpoint and starts looking like a *place* — a point from which the story keeps going.

## Materials

- Long-story timeline (pre-printed, non-numeric)
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: a long strip of paper, cloth, or smoothed earth; pebbles, seeds, or chalk
- Enriched: a digital timeline tool (TimelineJS or a shared document)

## Preparation

- Prepare the proportional long-story strip with markers for human origins, first writing, and
  first cities (no dates required to be computed).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D08.S1.10.01), comparing how societies measured time; from Grade 7
  (D08.S1.07.01), why historians divide time into periods. Today we place *ourselves* on the line.
- Prepare the proportional example to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the human story is far longer
than written history, and locating yourself on it — in proportion — turns "the past" from a
finished list into an unfinished story you are inside.** Model the proportional strip explicitly
(S-011). Handle the "deep time" numbers with care: humans evolved in Africa roughly 300,000 years
ago (S-506); writing is about 5,000 years old (S-264); most people came to live in cities only
within recent decades (S-089); electric light within a few generations (S-052). Hold these as
*proportions*, not as a memorization test, and keep the number access scaffold in place.

The **ethics** lens: knowing the story is longer than the records is a caution against treating
the written past — and its winners — as the whole of what mattered. The **egalitarianism** lens:
every person alive is a descendant of that long story, and no line of descent is more "history"
than another; the unrecorded billions were not extras — they *are* the story (S-506). The
**global** lens: this is not any one nation's origin — all living people share ancestry rooted in
Africa, and the story fans out to every continent (S-506). The **technology** lens: writing,
printing, electricity, and the internet each arrived astonishingly recently, which is why "modern"
feels breakneck — and why we should not mistake recency for destiny. The **environment** lens:
for almost the whole of the story, humans lived *within* landscapes that outlived them; only in
the last sliver did our tools begin to reshape the whole planet — a change of scale worth sitting
with. Preview: Lesson 4 carries the story from the past through the present toward possible
futures.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10 or 7, name one way a society measured time — a calendar, a
   dynasty list, an oral genealogy. Today we put *you* on a longer line than any of them.
2. **Meet deep time (8 min).** Write it down: the human story is far longer than the part that
   got written. People like us have existed for roughly 300,000 years (S-506); writing for about
   5,000 (S-264); city life for most people only recently (S-089); electric light for a few
   generations (S-052). Those are **proportions**, not a quiz.
3. **Worked example — the proportional strip (12 min).** Look at the pre-printed strip. Notice
   where "first humans" sits, where "writing" sits, and how tiny the space between "writing" and
   "now" is. Mark where *you* sit. The move: **place yourself on the line, and see what came
   before you as living, not just listed.**
4. **Make it your own (18 min).** On your strip (paper, cloth, or ground), place four markers:
   human origins; first writing; first cities; and your own life. Add one marker for something in
   your family's story — a move, a trade, a name — wherever you think it falls. Then write or say
   one sentence: *where do I sit, and what does that placement make me notice?*
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, show your strip and read your sentence. Ask: *does
   the placement match the proportions we learned?* Give one specific correction or agreement.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — one thing that
   changes when you see your life as a *place* in a story, not an endpoint.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the strip pre-marked with all positions except "you"; the learner places only
  themselves and one family marker and answers the sentence with a starter ("I sit… and I notice…").
- **Extension:** Mark what arrived *after* writing — printing, electricity, the internet — and write
  one sentence on what "very recent" means for how we treat our own moment.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The strip is deliberately non-numeric and proportional; no
  dates are computed, and the few figures ("300,000 years," "5,000 years") are offered on the
  pre-printed strip and read aloud, never required to be reproduced or ordered from memory. All
  positions are marked by *place*, not by number.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, sharing, or closing — can be
  done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode. For learners with
  low or no vision, the strip can be built as a tactile line (a raised ridge, a string, pressed
  marks) and the sentence spoken or signed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner place human origins, writing, cities, and their own
  life in proportion, and say one thing their placement makes them notice?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The marked long-story strip with the one-sentence placement
  reflection, opened in the capstone history journal.

## Home connection

Ask an elder at home for one story from before they were born that reached them — a family move,
a craft, a name. Write one sentence placing that story on the long line, and one sentence on how
it reached you.
