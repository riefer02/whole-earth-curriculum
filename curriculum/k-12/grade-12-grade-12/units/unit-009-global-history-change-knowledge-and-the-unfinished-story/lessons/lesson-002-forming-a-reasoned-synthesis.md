# Lesson 2 — Forming a Reasoned Synthesis

## Summary

Learners turn a **weighing** into a **synthesis**: one honest account of their own that carries
the best-supported parts of each competing account, names what the other account adds, and states
plainly what remains unsettled. They use a four-line frame (**claim**, **strongest evidence**,
**what the other account adds**, **what stays unsettled**) and revise with a partner. The
synthesis is presented as a reasoned position, not a verdict.

## Objectives

- Weigh competing accounts of change and continuity across global history and form a reasoned
  synthesis of the evidence. _(D08.S1.12.01)_

## Connection

When two people you trust disagree about what happened, you do not just repeat one of them — you
form your own careful sentence: *"Here is what seems true, here is why, here is what the other
side rightly reminds me of, and here is what I still do not know."* That sentence is a synthesis.
It is how a fair person carries several voices forward into one honest account, without flattening
them into a tie.

## Materials

- Synthesis frame sheet
- Capstone history journal (with the Lesson 1 weighing)
- Low-tech: scratch paper and the weighing sheet; a partner-listener or voice recorder
- Enriched: a word processor or shared document

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the synthesis frame sheet (four labeled lines).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the weighing questions and the learner's own weighing sheet. Today we
  assemble the weighing into a synthesis.
- Prepare a model synthesis to show first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a synthesis is not a vote and
not a compromise-soup — it is one honest account that states a claim, gives its strongest
evidence, credits what the other account adds, and says what is still unsettled.** Teach the
four-line frame explicitly (S-011). Guard against two traps: a synthesis that picks one side and
drops the other, and one that "splits the difference" so nothing is actually claimed. A good
synthesis takes a position *and* stays honest about its limits.

The **ethics** lens: intellectual honesty is the core value — a synthesis that hides the
unsettled part is a small dishonesty. The **egalitarianism** lens: synthesis is how many voices
— elder and planner, villager and migrant — all enter the final account, none erased (S-417). The
**global** lens: this move is not one culture's method; weighing then synthesizing is what
historians, elders' councils, and judges do across traditions, each in their own idiom. The
**technology** lens: a shared document lets two learners co-write one synthesis and see each
other's edits — a tool for the *cooperative* version of the skill. The **environment** lens: a
synthesis about land or water must state what the evidence cannot yet settle (how much a river
changed, what caused a drought), because honest uncertainty is the first step to good care.
Preview: Lesson 3 turns from weighing history to locating yourself inside it.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Look at your Lesson 1 weighing sheet. In one sentence, say — or write,
   sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — the single strongest thing you learned from *each* account.
2. **Meet the frame (8 min).** Write the four lines: **(1) Claim** — my reasoned position;
   **(2) Strongest evidence** — the best-supported facts that back it; **(3) What the other
   account adds** — the part it rightly keeps me honest about; **(4) What stays unsettled** —
   what I still do not know or cannot settle. A synthesis is these four, in one paragraph.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Read or hear a model synthesis of the village-to-city weighing
   from Lesson 1 (S-089, S-052). Notice it takes a position, cites evidence, credits the other
   telling, and names the unsettled. Mark each of the four lines in the model as you find it.
4. **Draft your synthesis (15 min).** Using the frame sheet, write your own synthesis of your
   Lesson 1 pair (or the lesson's pair). Complete all four lines. Be honest: if something is
   unsettled, say so rather than asserting it.
5. **Revise with a partner (10 min).** Read your synthesis to a partner (or they read it). Ask:
   *does it state a real position, credit the other account, and admit what is unsettled?* Revise
   one sentence to be more precise or more honest.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four lines your
   synthesis always contains.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame sheet with sentence starters ("I think… because…; the other account
  rightly adds…; I still cannot settle…"); fill the blanks rather than composing free.
- **Extension:** Add a fifth line — a **warrant** — naming *why* the evidence supports the claim,
   and mark where your synthesis would change if one piece of evidence were overturned.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** The frame is qualitative — no arithmetic; provide it
  pre-printed with the four lines labeled so the learner fills phrases and single words, and
  allow the synthesis to be spoken or recorded rather than written.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — reading, drafting aloud, discussing, or closing —
  can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the synthesis state a real claim, give its strongest evidence,
  credit the other account, and admit what is unsettled?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written synthesis paragraph, paired with the Lesson 1
  weighing, in the capstone history journal.

## Home connection

Write a three-sentence synthesis of a disagreement at home — what seems true, why, and what you
still cannot settle. Read it to someone and ask whether it was fair to both tellings.
