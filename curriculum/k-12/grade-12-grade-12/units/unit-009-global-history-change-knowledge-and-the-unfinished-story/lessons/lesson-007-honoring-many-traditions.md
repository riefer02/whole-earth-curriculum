# Lesson 7 — Honoring Many Traditions

## Summary

Learners move from *weighing* traditions to **honoring** them. They study short, real cases — West
African **griot** oral history, the Andean **khipu**, the manuscripts of **Timbuktu**, and
**language revitalization** — and ask what honoring each actually requires: recognizing it as
knowledge, **crediting** its keepers, protecting it, and helping pass it on. Honoring is
distinguished from merely tolerating, and from taking without credit.

## Objectives

- Reflect on how honoring many knowledge traditions contributes to a more just and flourishing
  shared human story. _(D08.S2.12.02)_

## Connection

When a recipe is passed to you, honoring it is not just cooking it once — it is learning it right,
naming who taught you, and passing it on to the next person, not claiming you invented it. The same
is true of a people's knowledge: to honor a tradition is to recognize it as real knowledge, credit
its keepers, protect it from erasure, and help it live into the next generation.

## Materials

- Honoring case card set
- Capstone history journal
- Low-tech: hand-copied case cards; scratch paper
- Enriched: a short film or audio of a living tradition (a kōhanga reo, a griot performance)

## Preparation

- Copy the case cards (griot, khipu, Timbuktu, language revitalization) and the honoring organizer
  (recognize / credit / protect / pass on).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D08.S2.11.02), how peoples have maintained and revitalized languages
  and knowledge. Today we name *what honoring requires*.
- Prepare the worked example (one case) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **honoring a tradition is active —
recognize it as knowledge, credit its keepers, protect it, and pass it on — and it is not the same
as tolerating, and not the same as taking without credit.** Teach the four honoring acts explicitly
(S-011). Keep each case accurate and centered on its own people: the griot (jeli/jali) carries
genealogies and histories orally across generations in West Africa (S-446); the Andean khipu is a
knotted-cord record-keeping system suppressed under colonial rule and now studied and revived
(S-473); Timbuktu's manuscripts preserve centuries of scholarship in Arabic and African languages
(S-201, S-268); language revitalization — kōhanga reo, Pūnana Leo, and others — brings languages
home (S-428). None is a museum piece; each is living.

The **ethics** lens: the line between honoring and taking is credit — using a tradition's knowledge
while erasing its keepers is not honoring but extraction. The **egalitarianism** lens: these are not
"minor" traditions of "minor" peoples; they are whole systems of knowledge whose bearers have the
same dignity as any scholar (S-056). The **global** lens: honoring is recognized worldwide through
living-heritage and memory-of-the-world frameworks (S-056, S-201) — a shared, cross-cultural
practice, not one region's virtue. The **technology** lens: digital archives now photograph and
index the Timbuktu manuscripts and record endangered languages, so tools can *serve* honoring — when
the keepers, not the tools, decide what is shared. The **environment** lens: many traditions carry
ecological knowledge (when to burn, plant, harvest) whose loss would also harm the land — honoring
the tradition and caring for the Earth are one act. Preview: Lesson 8 turns honoring into a claim
about a more just, flourishing shared story.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11, name one tradition that was revived or kept alive under
   pressure. Today: what does *honoring* it require?
2. **Meet the four acts (8 min).** Write them down: to honor a knowledge tradition is to **(1)
   recognize** it as real knowledge; **(2) credit** its keepers by name and people; **(3) protect**
   it from erasure or misuse; **(4) pass it on** to the next generation. Honoring is *not* merely
   tolerating, and *not* taking without credit.
3. **Worked example — the griot (12 min).** Read the case: the West African griot carries a
   people's genealogies and histories in memory and song, across generations, without a written
   text (S-446). Walk the four acts: recognize (it *is* a historian's discipline), credit (name the
   griot and the lineage), protect (the right to tell, and to be heard), pass on (apprenticeship to
   the next griot). Write these on your organizer.
4. **Work your own case (18 min).** With a partner, take one case — the khipu, Timbuktu's
   manuscripts, or language revitalization — and fill the four acts. Say — or write, sign, gesture,
   or use AAC to show — one thing honoring it requires that merely "tolerating" it would miss.
5. **Share and check (5 min).** Report one act to another pair. Ask: *did we credit the keepers, or
   just describe the tradition?* Give one correction.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the four acts that make
   honoring more than tolerance.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the organizer pre-filled with one act already done for each case; the learner
  adds the remaining acts with sentence starters ("To honor this, people must…").
- **Extension:** Weigh the difference between honoring a tradition and *borrowing* from it, and
  write one sentence on where credit makes the difference.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** No arithmetic is required; the four acts are a list, not a
  computation, and the organizer is pre-numbered so the learner works one act at a time without
  counting. Dates on the cards are offered as background, never required to be computed or ordered.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — naming, reading aloud, sharing, or closing — can be
  done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are non-speaking, d/Deaf,
  hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred mode; no step requires
  producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner apply the four honoring acts to a case, and name what
  honoring adds beyond tolerance — especially crediting the keepers?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed honoring organizer for one case, opened in the
  capstone history journal.

## Home connection

Name one thing your family or community knows — a remedy, a craft, a saying — and write who keeps
it and how it will reach the next person. One sentence on how it could be honored, and by whom.
