# Lesson 12 — Designing Shared Spaces: Access and Sustainability

## Summary

Learners use geometric reasoning to lay out a **shared space** — a ramp, a path, a gathering
place — so that it is **accessible** to everyone and **sustainable** for its place. They
compute a ramp's **slope** as rise over run against an access standard, work out **areas**
and **footprints**, and learn that "access" and "sustainability" are not afterthoughts but
the geometry itself.

## Objectives

- Apply geometric reasoning to real-world design, considering access and sustainability.
  _(D05.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

A step that keeps a wheelchair or a tired elder out, a path that washes away with the first
rain, a gathering place with no shade — each is a *geometric* decision about slope, area,
and layout. When you design a shared space — a school yard, a market square, a bus stop —
you are writing in geometry who can enter and whether the place endures. Getting the slope
right is how a space says "everyone is welcome here."

## Materials

- Shared-space design sheet
- Math journal
- Low-tech: grid paper and ruler

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the blank site plan; prepare a real ramp or step to measure.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 9–11, coordinates, vectors, scale, and design geometry. Today
  geometry becomes a question of *access and endurance*.
- Prepare the slope worked example (rise over run) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **slope = rise ÷ run, and
an accessible ramp needs a gentle slope (a common standard is 1:12 — one unit of rise for
every twelve of run); area measures a space's footprint, and a sustainable layout fits its
place and materials.** Teach the slope computation explicitly with a worked example
(S-011), then let learners make design trade-offs and argue them with numbers. Keep the
value claim (everyone should be able to enter) explicit as a value, and the numbers as the
evidence for whether a design honors it.

The **ethics** lens: a ramp too steep to use is a design that quietly excludes — geometry
carries moral weight. The **egalitarianism** lens: **universal design** asks who is left
out and builds for the widest range of bodies from the start, not as an afterthought. The
**global** lens: access standards and building traditions differ across the world, but the
slope rule is the same physics everywhere — a 1:12 ramp in one place is a 1:12 ramp in
another. The **technology** lens: slope and area are exactly what design software computes,
but the reasoning must be human first. The **environment** lens: a sustainable space fits
its climate and materials — drainage, shade, local stone — and the people who bear
environmental burdens are often the same people a bad design excludes (S-483, S-484,
S-395). Preview: Lesson 13 turns to inference from data.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11, scale and proportion in design. Today the numbers
   decide who can enter.
2. **Meet slope as access (10 min).** A ramp rises 0.5 m over a run of 6 m: its **slope** is
   rise ÷ run = 0.5 ÷ 6 = 1/12. A gentle slope like 1:12 is usable by a wheelchair; a steep
   1:3 step is not. Write slope as **rise over run** and read it as "one up for every twelve
   along."
3. **Worked example — check a ramp (15 min).** A doorway is 1 m above the ground. How long
   a ramp meets a 1:12 standard? Solve run = rise × 12 = 12 m. If the space only allows 6 m,
   the slope doubles to 1:6 — too steep. So you must switchback the ramp or find another
   entry. Work the numbers; the geometry *forces* a fair design choice.
4. **Area and footprint (8 min).** The gathering area is a rectangle 8 m by 10 m: its
   **area** is 80 m². A garden of the same area can be laid out many ways; the **footprint**
   (what ground it covers) matters for drainage and shade. Compute area and compare two
   layouts.
5. **Design and argue (12 min).** With a partner, lay out the shared space: place the
   ramp (check its slope), the path (wide and gentle enough for all), and the gathering
   area (with shade and drainage). Compute the slope and the areas, and write two notes —
   one on *access* (who can enter) and one on *sustainability* (does it fit this place).
   Swap designs and check each other's numbers.
6. **Retrieve and connect (4 min).** Write one sentence on why a gentle slope is a matter
   of justice, and one on how a space can be both welcoming and durable.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — what slope
   has to do with fairness, in one sentence.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Measure a real ramp or step and compute its slope, then state whether it
  meets 1:12 before designing anything.
- **Extension:** Design a two-way switchback ramp for a fixed space, compute both slopes,
  and compare the total footprint to a single straight ramp.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** Provide a pre-filled slope-and-area table for steps 2–4
  (rise ÷ run written as a ratio, and the 8 m × 10 m = 80 m² area already worked) to copy and
  extend; offload the division (0.5 ÷ 6, run = 1 × 12) to a partner or calculator; and take
  the verbal route — compare a gentle ramp to a steep step by hand or by eye and say "a
  gentler slope serves more people; 1:12 means one up for every twelve along," reaching the
  fairness conclusion without number crunching.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing,
  or closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their
  preferred mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner compute a ramp's slope against an access
  standard, work out an area, and defend a layout on both access and sustainability
  grounds (in speech, writing, sign, or AAC)?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One shared-space design with slope and area computed and
  access/sustainability notes, in the math journal.

## Home connection

Measure a ramp, step, or doorway near home: its rise and its run. Compute the slope, write
it as a ratio, and say in one sentence who it would welcome and who it would exclude.
