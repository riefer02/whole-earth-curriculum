# Lesson 5 — Advocating for Health and Dignity for All

## Summary

Learners turn understanding into **advocacy**: they propose a change that widens access to
healthy, dignified food, drawing on real levers — **policy**, community **food programs**,
**cooperatives**, **school meals**, **gardens**. They hold the **facts** (hundreds of millions
face hunger; **access**, not just availability, is the gap) alongside the **value** (everyone
deserves enough to eat), and link individual wellbeing to everyone's. Hunger is framed as a
systems condition, never a personal failing.

## Objectives

- Advocate for food systems and policies that support health and dignity for all people,
  linking individual and collective wellbeing. _(D03.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

Hunger is not a personal failing — it is a systems condition. Whether you can reach healthy
food depends on where you live, what it costs, and how much money your family has — things no
person chose. Advocacy is the move from "I wish this were fair" to "here is a change that
would make it fairer." It is something ordinary people do all over the world, and it is
something you can already do.

## Materials

- Advocacy-lever sheet
- Health & care journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; voice, pen, or petition
- Enriched: a short local example of food advocacy

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the lever sheet (change / who it helps / what it costs / first step).
- Retrieval: from Grade 11 (D03.S3.11.01), proposing a change that widens access; from the
  unit's own Lessons 1–4, the map and levers we built. Today we *act* on that map.
- Prepare the worked example (one advocacy proposal) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **advocacy is a learnable
move — name a change, show who it helps and what it costs, and take a first step — and it links
my wellbeing to everyone's.** Teach one worked-example proposal explicitly, then let learners
build their own (S-011). Keep facts and values cleanly separated: it is a *fact* that roughly
733 million people faced hunger in 2023 and about 2.33 billion faced moderate or severe food
insecurity (S-409); it is a *value* that everyone deserves enough to eat. Both are true, and
the second is what moves us to act.

The **ethics** lens: advocacy is the discipline of turning a value (dignity, fairness) into a
concrete, accountable ask — and of listening to the people the change is *for*, not deciding
for them. The **egalitarianism** lens: the right to food and health belongs to every child,
every person, without exception (S-060); advocacy exists because that right is not yet honored
equally. The **global** lens: advocacy takes many forms worldwide — Wangari Maathai's Green
Belt Movement began with tree-planting and grew into a movement for land, food, and peace
(S-059); community gardens, food banks, and cooperatives are advocacy too. The **technology**
lens: who controls the seeds, the data, and the distribution shapes who eats — and tools from
open data to mobile markets can be turned either way (S-006). The **environment** lens: food
advocacy and Earth advocacy are one fight — the same systems that leave people hungry also
strain the land and water that feed us. Preview: Lesson 6 turns inward, to managing your own
health independently.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 11: name one change you proposed that would widen access to
   food. Today we make that proposal real: specific, evidence-backed, and actionable.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: **advocacy** is speaking or acting for a change
   that should happen, so that it does. It has four parts: the **change**, **who it helps**,
   **what it costs**, and a **first step**. It links your wellbeing to everyone's.
3. **Worked example — one proposal (15 min).** Model all four parts on a real lever. For
   example: **change** — a school or neighborhood garden plus a meal program; **who it helps** —
   families who cannot reliably afford fresh food; **what it costs** — land, time, and a small
   budget, which a cooperative or local program can share; **first step** — find one ally and
   one place, and ask. Write the four parts on your sheet as the model.
4. **Name the move — fact and value, kept straight (8 min).** Your proposal rests on a fact
   (food insecurity is real and uneven, S-409) and a value (everyone deserves enough, S-060).
   Name both, separately, so your advocacy is honest: the fact is *why it is true*, the value
   is *why it matters*.
5. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, build your own proposal using the four-part
   sheet. Exchange and check each other: is the change concrete, the "who it helps" real, the
   cost honest, the first step small enough to actually take? Say — or write, sign, gesture,
   or use AAC to show — your first step and who you would take it with.
6. **Make it shareable (5 min).** Write your proposal as one short paragraph — the kind you
   could read to a neighbor, a student council, or a local official. Keep it in your journal.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — why "my
   wellbeing" and "everyone's wellbeing" are not two separate things but one.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the worked-example proposal pre-filled; the learner changes one part (the
  "first step" or "who it helps") rather than building all four from scratch.
- **Extension:** Name the strongest counterargument to your proposal and answer it, keeping
  facts and values clearly labeled, then state the single most persuasive line of your case.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson's figures (millions who face hunger) are read as
  "a very large number of people," never computed; provide the four-part sheet pre-printed, and
  allow the proposal to be written entirely in words with no numerals at all.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound. A proposal may be drawn, signed, or shown
  rather than spoken.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner build a four-part proposal (change, who it
  helps, what it costs, first step), keep a fact and a value distinct, and link individual to
  collective wellbeing?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed advocacy-lever sheet plus the one-paragraph
  proposal, opened in the health & care journal.

## Home connection

At home, talk with someone about one change that would make healthy food easier to reach where
you live. Write one sentence naming the change and one naming the first step you could take
together.
