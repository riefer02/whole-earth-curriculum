# Lesson 9 — Caring for Others: Caregiving and When to Step In

## Summary

Learners explore **caregiving** — helping others with daily life (a sick friend, a sibling, a
child, an elder, a person with a disability) — with **dignity**, **consent**, and respect for
**autonomy**. They build a circle of supports (daily help, listening, safety, professional
help), learn to **"help with, not instead of,"** and name the moments to **step in**: immediate
danger, a child being harmed, or a need bigger than one person can meet. The framing is
empowering — you can already do this — never anxiety-inducing.

## Objectives

- Explain how to care for the health and safety of others, including caregiving and when to
  step in. _(D03.S4.12.02)_

## Connection

Caring for others is something you already do — you have helped a friend who was sick or sad,
watched a younger child, sat with someone who was hurting. Caregiving makes that a *skill*:
how to help without taking over, and how to know the moment to get more help than you can
give. Being able to care for another person is one of the most useful things a human can be.

## Materials

- Caregiving-circle sheet
- Health & care journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a remembered moment of helping
- Enriched: a short account of a caregiver or support worker

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the caregiving circle (person at center; rings: daily help / listening / safety
  / professional help; each with "help with" and "step in").
- Retrieval: from Grade 5 (D03.S4.05.01), responding in an emergency and calling for help;
  from Grade 8 (D03.S1.08.02), healthy ways to help a body return to calm. Today we broaden
  from "help in a crisis" to "care for a person over time."
- Prepare the worked example (one ring filled in) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **caregiving means helping
another person's health and safety with dignity, consent, and respect for their autonomy —
"help with, not instead of" — and knowing when to step in or get more help than you can give.**
Teach one ring as a worked example, then let learners build their own circle (S-011). Keep it
empowering and normal: learners already help people; we are naming and refining that. On the
"safeguarding" moments, stay calm and clear, and always point to the adult/professional path —
never leave a learner feeling they must carry a heavy secret alone (S-051).

The **ethics** lens: care respects the person — their dignity, their choices, their right to
be heard and to say no; consent and autonomy are the floor of good care (S-060). The
**egalitarianism** lens: people who need care — children, elders, people with disabilities —
are not "burdens"; they are people whose dignity is equal to anyone's, and caring for them is
honoring that equality. The **global** lens: caregiving is done everywhere, in families and
communities, in many arrangements — multigenerational households, mutual aid, formal
care-workers — and no one arrangement is "correct." The **technology** lens: tools from phones
to monitoring devices can support care and summon help, but they can never replace a human
who notices and stays. The **environment** lens: a safe, accessible environment is itself a
form of care — and many people's need for care is shaped by the places they must live in.
Preview: Lesson 10 adds the crisis skills — emergency response — that caregiving sometimes
requires.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of one time you helped someone who was sick, sad, or in need.
   Name — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — what you did and what made it help.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: **caregiving** is helping another person with
   their health and daily life — over hours, days, or longer. Its first rule is **dignity**:
   help *with*, not *instead of*. The person stays the author of their own life; you support.
3. **Worked example — the circle of supports (15 min).** Model the four rings. **Daily help** —
   food, comfort, medicine as needed; **Listening** — being present, not fixing; **Safety** —
   noticing danger and acting; **Professional help** — knowing when a trained person is
   needed. For each ring, write one thing you would do (*help with*) and one sign that you
   should do more (*step in*).
4. **Name the move — when to step in (8 min).** You step in — or get help — when there is
   **immediate danger**, when a **child is being harmed** (tell a trusted adult, and keep
   telling until the child is safe, S-051), or when the need is **bigger than you** (a health
   worker, an emergency number). Write those three triggers in your own words.
5. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, work two short scenarios: (a) a friend is
   deeply sad for weeks and says they will manage alone; (b) a younger child near you seems
   hurt and afraid of going home. For each, decide: help with, or step in? Say — or write,
   sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — your choice and your reason.
6. **Write your circle (5 min).** In your journal, draw your own caregiving circle with one
   "help with" and one "step in" per ring. Add the line: *I help with, not instead of.*
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the difference
   between *helping someone* and *taking over someone*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the circle pre-filled with the worked example; the learner adds one "help
  with" and one "step in" to a single ring rather than all four.
- **Extension:** Work the "help with, not instead of" dilemma — a person who wants to do
  something risky for themselves — and write where dignity and safety pull in different
  directions and how you would weigh them.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic; the four rings are
  handled by the pre-printed circle, and every ring may be completed in words, drawings, or
  gesture with no numerals at all.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four rings, state "help with, not
  instead of," and give the three "step in" triggers with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed caregiving circle with the three triggers and
  the "help with, not instead of" line, opened in the journal.

## Home connection

At home, notice one small way you already help someone (or someone helps you), and name it
without judgment. Write one sentence about what makes that help *respectful* rather than
*taking over*.
