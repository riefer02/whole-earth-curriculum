# Lesson 2 — Nutrition, Ethics, and the Environment

## Summary

Learners weigh the **ethics** and **environment** of food choices: every meal reaches beyond
the plate to **land**, **water**, **climate**, and the **people** who grow and move it. They
trace one meal outward, separate measurable **facts** (a food's environmental **footprint**,
how much food is **wasted**) from **values** (what we owe each other), and commit to choosing
with eyes open — never shaming what a person can afford or the food their family holds dear.

## Objectives

- Synthesize the science of nutrition with ethical and environmental considerations to make
  lifelong food decisions. _(D03.S3.12.01)_

## Connection

Every meal is a choice that reaches beyond the plate. A mango, a bowl of rice, a piece of
bread — each was grown somewhere, used water and soil, produced some greenhouse gas, and
passed through the hands of people who were paid for it. Knowing a food's footprint does not
make you guilty; it makes you *able* to choose with eyes open. And guilt is the wrong tool:
what a person eats is shaped by money, access, and culture long before it is shaped by
preference.

## Materials

- "What-a-meal-touches" footprint sheet
- Health & care journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a remembered or real meal
- Enriched: a food-footprint data source (S-006)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the plate-with-four-arrows sheet (land / water / climate-energy / people).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D03.S3.10.02), the environmental footprint of different foods;
  from Grade 9 (D03.S3.09.02), hunger, food security, and unequal access. Today we weigh
  ethics and environment together in one decision.
- Prepare the worked example (trace one meal across all four arrows) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **food choices carry
consequences for other people and the living Earth, and we can weigh those honestly — by
separating facts from values — without shaming anyone's plate.** Teach the trace-the-meal
worked example explicitly, then let learners trace their own (S-011). This is the unit's
highest-shame-risk lesson; name it out loud: a learner who is poor, food-insecure, or whose
family's food is not "green" must never be made to feel at fault. The footprint belongs to
*systems*, and the responsibility to change them is *shared*, never loaded onto one plate.

The **ethics** lens: a food choice has consequences for others — farm workers, animals, future
people — and ethics is simply the discipline of asking *who is affected and what do I owe
them.* The **egalitarianism** lens: the costs of food are unevenly carried — the people who
grow and pick food are often the people who can least afford it, and the places least
responsible for climate change often feel it worst (S-006, S-005). The **global** lens:
footprints differ by place and method — a food farmed one way in one region has a different
footprint than the same food farmed differently elsewhere; there is no single "good" food. The
**technology** lens: fertilizer, irrigation, refrigeration, and long-distance shipping changed
footprints dramatically — some raising them, some lowering them. The **environment** lens:
about one-fifth of the food available to consumers worldwide is wasted, wasting the water,
land, and energy that made it (S-410). Preview: Lesson 4 traces the whole system from farm to
table; Lesson 5 turns this understanding into advocacy.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10, name one way the same food can have a bigger or smaller
   environmental footprint. Today we add the *ethics* side: who and what a meal touches.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: a food's **environmental footprint** is the
   total effect of producing, moving, and disposing of it — on **land**, **water**,
   **climate**, and the **people** in the chain. Every meal has one; knowing it is information,
   not a verdict.
3. **Worked example — trace one meal (15 min).** Take an ordinary meal (rice and beans, bread
   and soup, a bowl of fruit). Trace it across the four arrows: **land** (what was cleared or
   used to grow it), **water** (what it took to grow and process it), **climate/energy** (the
   greenhouse gases and fuel in growing and moving it), **people** (who grew, picked, packed,
   and sold it, and whether they were paid fairly). Write one *cost* and one *choice* per
   arrow.
4. **Name the move — fact vs value (8 min).** Some of what you wrote is **fact** (measurable —
   how much water, how much greenhouse gas), some is **value** (what we *ought* to owe workers
   or the Earth). Write one example of each from your own sheet. Both are real; only the first
   is settled by evidence.
5. **Guided practice (12 min).** With a partner, trace a second meal — one you actually eat —
   across all four arrows. Then exchange sheets and check each other: did you each separate
   fact from value? Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — one honest choice you
   *could* make and one honest thing that is *not* in your control.
6. **Commit without shame (5 min).** Write one sentence: *I will choose what I can, and I will
   not turn a system's cost into a person's guilt.* Keep it in your journal.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — the difference
   between a food's footprint (a fact) and a food's worth (a value).

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the worked-example meal pre-traced on the sheet; the learner adds one cost
  or choice to a single arrow rather than building the whole trace.
- **Extension:** Compare the relative footprints of two foods that could fill the same role
  (e.g., two protein sources) using real data (S-006), and write which difference is largest
  and why.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson's numbers (a fifth of food wasted, a footprint
  figure) are all read as *more, less, or about the same*, never as arithmetic; provide the
  plate-with-arrows sheet pre-printed, and allow the whole trace to be done in words and
  arrows with no numerals at all.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace a meal across the four arrows, separate a
  factual footprint claim from a value claim, and state why guilt is the wrong tool?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** One completed "what a meal touches" sheet with the
  fact-vs-value split marked, opened in the health & care journal.

## Home connection

At home, pick one food your family often buys and, with someone there, work out (roughly, no
numbers required) what it touches before it reaches you. Write one sentence naming a fact you
learned and one naming a choice you could make.
