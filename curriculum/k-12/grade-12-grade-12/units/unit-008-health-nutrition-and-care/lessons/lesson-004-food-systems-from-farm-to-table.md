# Lesson 4 — Food Systems: From Farm to Table

## Summary

Learners trace a **food system** from **farm to table to waste** — production, processing,
distribution, retail, consumption, disposal — and see who and what it touches: farmers,
workers, transport, land, water, and climate. They analyze a local food system for where
health, dignity, and the environment are helped or harmed, and locate the **leverage points**
where a change would matter most. This is the descriptive ground on which Lesson 5's advocacy
stands.

## Objectives

- Advocate for food systems and policies that support health and dignity for all, beginning
  by analyzing how a food system actually works. _(D03.S3.12.02)_

## Connection

The meal at your table is the end of a long chain: a seed in soil, water and sun, hands that
planted and picked, a truck or ship, a market or shop, a kitchen. When we see the whole chain,
we can see where health, dignity, and the Earth are helped or harmed — and where a person, a
community, or a policy could act. You cannot change a system you cannot see; today you learn
to see it.

## Materials

- Farm-to-table chain sheet
- Health & care journal
- Low-tech: paper and any writing tool; a single real food item
- Enriched: a local-food-system map or article

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the chain sheet (seed/soil → farm → processing → transport → market → table →
  waste, each with a "who" and a "what it touches" line).
- Retrieval: from Grade 9 (D03.S3.09.01), analyzing a local food system from farm to table to
  waste; from Grade 11 (D03.S3.11.01), how policy shapes population nutrition. Today we build
  the descriptive map that advocacy needs.
- Prepare the worked example (trace one food across the chain) to model first (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a food system is a chain of
stages — production, processing, distribution, retail, consumption, waste — and each stage
touches people and the Earth, so each stage is a place where health and dignity can be helped
or harmed.** Teach the trace-the-chain worked example explicitly, then let learners trace a
food of their own (S-011). Keep the frame descriptive and hopeful: we name harm in order to
find the place to act, never to despair.

The **ethics** lens: mapping a system means noticing who is affected at each stage — the
worker who is underpaid, the community whose water is used, the eater who cannot afford the
result. The **egalitarianism** lens: food security has four pillars — availability, access,
utilization, and stability — and "access" is where inequality bites: enough food can exist in
a region while some people still go hungry (S-409). The **global** lens: the same food moves
through very different systems in different places — a smallholder's field, a vast
export plantation, a neighborhood garden — and no one system is "the" system. The
**technology** lens: refrigeration, roads, and processing lengthened the chain and lowered
some prices while concentrating power in a few links; technology is a lever whose direction we
choose. The **environment** lens: the chain ends in waste — about a fifth of food available to
consumers is wasted, carrying the land, water, and energy that made it (S-410). Preview:
Lesson 5 turns this map into a proposal for change.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: name one stage in the journey of the last food you ate.
   Today we name them all and ask what each stage does to people and the Earth.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** Write it down: a **food system** is the whole chain that feeds
   people — **production** (growing and raising), **processing** (turning raw into edible),
   **distribution** (moving it), **retail** (selling it), **consumption** (eating it), and
   **waste** (what is thrown away). A food system is built by people and runs on land, water,
   and energy.
3. **Worked example — trace one food (15 min).** Take one familiar food. Walk it stage by
   stage on the chain sheet, writing **who** does the work and **what** the stage touches. For
   example: a tomato is seeded and grown (a farmer, soil and water), packed and moved (workers
   and a truck, fuel), sold (a shopkeeper), eaten (a family), and partly wasted (a bin). Note
   where a person could be treated unfairly and where the Earth is drawn down.
4. **Name the move — leverage points (8 min).** Some stages matter more than others. A
   **leverage point** is a place where a change would ripple through the whole system — the
   price a farmer is paid, the distance food travels, what a school or market chooses to buy.
   Circle one leverage point on your sheet.
5. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, trace a food from your own region across the
   chain. Exchange sheets and check each other: did you name a *who* and a *what it touches* at
   every stage? Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to show — the single stage where you
   think health or dignity is helped or harmed most, and why.
6. **Locate the lever (5 min).** In your journal, write one sentence: *The place I would
   change is ____, because ____.* This is the seed of Lesson 5's advocacy.
7. **Close (2 min).** Say — or write, sign, gesture, or use AAC to express — why "from farm to
   table" is really "from farm to table *and back*" (to the soil, the water, the waste).

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the chain sheet pre-filled with the worked-example food; the learner adds a
  single "who" or "what it touches" at one stage rather than tracing from scratch.
- **Extension:** Identify two leverage points and argue which would make the bigger difference
  for health and dignity, naming the trade-off between them.
- **Number access (dyscalculia):** This lesson requires no arithmetic; the stages are handled
  by the pre-printed chain sheet, and every stage may be completed in words, arrows, or
  drawings with no numerals at all. Any figures (a fifth of food wasted) are read as "a lot,"
  not computed.
- **Communication access:** Every spoken step — saying, naming, reading aloud, discussing, or
  closing — can be done in writing, sign, gesture, or AAC instead. Learners who are
  non-speaking, d/Deaf, hard-of-hearing, or who use AAC complete every task in their preferred
  mode; no step requires producing or hearing sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace a food across the chain, name a "who" and
  a "what it touches" at each stage, and locate a leverage point with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed farm-to-table chain sheet with one leverage
  point circled and the one-sentence "place I would change," opened in the journal.

## Home connection

Pick one food at home and, with someone there, retrace its journey backward from your table as
far as you can. Write one sentence naming the stage you could see least clearly and one naming
the stage where you think a change would matter most.
