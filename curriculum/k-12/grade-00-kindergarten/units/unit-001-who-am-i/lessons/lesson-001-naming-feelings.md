# Lesson 1 — Naming Our Feelings

## Summary

Learners learn to name five basic feelings and practice recognizing them in
themselves and others. They close by expressing one feeling through words, a drawing,
or a movement.

## Objectives

- Name and identify basic feelings (happy, sad, angry, scared, calm) in self and
  others. _(D01.S1.K.01)_
- Describe a feeling using words, a drawing, or a movement. _(D01.S1.K.02)_

## Connection

Everyone has feelings, every single day. When you know what you are feeling, you can
share it, ask for help, and understand the people around you — at home, in a
classroom, or anywhere people are together. Naming a feeling is the first step to
taking care of it.

## Materials

- Feeling face cards (happy, sad, angry, scared, calm)
- Crayons and paper
- A mirror (optional)

## Preparation

- Print or draw a set of feeling face cards.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and discussion.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Warm and matter-of-fact. Every feeling is allowed and none is wrong — say that
out loud, because a child who has just been told off needs to hear it.

**The facts to land.**

1. Five feelings have names: **happy, sad, angry, scared, calm**.
2. Feelings show up in the body — a fast heart, a hot face, a tight throat.
3. A feeling can be named with words, a drawing, or a movement; all three count.

**Don't overshoot.** Do not rank the feelings or imply some are good and some bad. Do
not correct a child's label — "you're not scared, you're fine" teaches a child not to
name what they feel. Offer another word alongside theirs.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Social-emotional:* a named feeling is easier to share and easier to soothe.
- *Ethics:* other people have feelings too, and we cannot see them from outside — so we
  ask.
- *Global:* families and cultures show feeling differently — loudly, quietly, with the
  whole body, not at all. None of these is a better way to feel.

**The skill.** Prepare the cards, mirror, and circle, then let the learner lead the
naming.

**Access.** If a learner cannot see the cards, describe each face aloud and let them
feel a face. If a learner does not speak, a point, a sign, or a drawn face is a complete
answer.

**Safety floor.** A child naming a big, frightening feeling is telling you something.
Thank them, stay calm, and follow your setting's safeguarding practice.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit comfortably. Notice: how does your body feel right now?
2. **Meet the feeling cards (10 min).** Look at each card and name the feeling. Make
   the face together. Describe each face aloud too — "a frown, eyebrows down" — so
   everyone can join, even without seeing the card. Point to the card that matches
   how you feel today.
3. **Body check (10 min).** Slowly scan your body: where do you feel happy? (A warm
   chest, a smile.) If you have a mirror, look in it to see your own face; if not,
   feel your face with your hands.
4. **Express it (15 min).** Choose one feeling and show it in one of three ways: a
   word, a drawing, or a movement. Share with a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: everyone has feelings, and feelings change. That is
   okay.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a smaller set of cards (two feelings); model a word and gesture.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to draw two feelings and describe a time they felt
  each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner point to or name a feeling that
  matches a scenario?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The feeling drawing, kept in the learner's portfolio.
