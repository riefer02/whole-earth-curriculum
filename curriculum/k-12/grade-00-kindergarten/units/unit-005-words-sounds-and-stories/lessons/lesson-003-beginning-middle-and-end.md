# Lesson 3 — Stories Have a Beginning, Middle, and End

## Summary

Learners notice that stories, like days and meals and journeys, have an order — a
beginning, a middle, and an end. They retell a story by walking or acting through the
three parts in sequence.

## Objectives

- Listen to a story and retell one important part in their own words.
  _(D04.S1.K.01)_

## Connection

Everything that happens has a before, a during, and an after — a day, a meal, a walk
home. Stories are the same: they start, something happens in the middle, and then they
end. Knowing the order helps you hold a whole story in your mind and tell it back so
others can follow along.

## Materials

- A short story with a clear order (a journey, a meal, a day)
- Three picture cards — beginning, middle, end (optional)

## Preparation

- Choose a story with an unmistakable three-part order.
- Prepare three picture cards or three found objects to stand in for first, next, last.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Keep the order concrete and physical — moving the body through
"first, next, last" helps children hold the idea before they can say it. Some stories
have more than three parts; at this age, three is plenty, and "middle" can be loose.
Accept any part a learner names as long as it is genuinely from the story. Acting out,
pointing to the three cards, or drawing the three parts all count as retelling — a
learner who does not speak aloud can still tell the story's order with their body, hands,
or pictures.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think about your morning so far. What happened first? What
   happened next? What happened last? You just told a story in order.
2. **Hear the story (10 min).** Listen to the story and look for its three parts: the
   **beginning** (how it starts), the **middle** (what happens), and the **end** (how
   it finishes).
3. **Find the three parts (10 min).** With a partner, say what happened first, next,
   and last. Use three fingers to count them.
4. **Walk the story (15 min).** Stand on a "first" spot, a "next" spot, and a "last"
   spot — three stones, three hoops, or three drawn boxes. Step to each spot and tell
   that part aloud. Your body helps your words remember the order.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: first, next, last. That order is the shape of a story,
   and it helps your listeners follow you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Tell the story in just two parts (first and last); offer the three
  picture cards and let the learner point to them in order.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to tell a story from their own day with a clear
  beginning, middle, and end.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place three parts of the story in order
  — first, next, last — while telling?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner retell at least one part and say whether it
  came first, next, or last — by saying, pointing, drawing, or acting?

## Home connection

At home, retell your day to a grown-up in three parts — first, next, last — and see if
they can guess which part was the middle.
