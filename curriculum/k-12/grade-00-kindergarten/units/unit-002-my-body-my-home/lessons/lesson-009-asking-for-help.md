# Lesson 9 — Asking for Help

## Summary

Learners learn to tell the difference between little things they can try themselves
and times to ask a trusted grown-up for help, and they rehearse asking clearly and
confidently. Asking for help is a strength, not a weakness.

## Objectives

- Tell when to ask a grown-up for help. _(D03.S4.K.01)_

## Connection

Everyone needs help sometimes — even grown-ups. You need help when you are hurt, when
you feel sick, when you are scared or unsafe, when something is too heavy or too high,
or when you feel lost. Knowing *when* to ask, and *who* to ask, keeps your body — your
home — safe.

## Materials

- Scenario cards (a cut, feeling sick, a broken toy, feeling scared or unsafe, a fire,
  a too-high shelf)
- A "help" sign or bell for practicing

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw simple scenario cards.
- Set a warm, safe tone — asking for help is brave, never silly.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). As facilitator, keep the tone warm and
reassuring. Introduce "trusted grown-ups" — the adults a child can safely turn to, such
as a caregiver, teacher, nurse, or neighbor. Keep safety talk age-appropriate: if a
child is hurt, scared, or made to feel unsafe, they should tell a trusted grown-up —
no details beyond that are needed at this age. Be ready for a child to share something
heavy, and respond with calm and follow your local safeguarding steps.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you asked for help. How did it feel? Asking for
   help is something strong people do.
2. **Myself or a grown-up? (15 min).** Look at each scenario card. Sort them: is this
   a little thing you can try yourself, or a time to ask a grown-up? Talk it through
   with a partner. A cut, feeling sick, feeling scared or unsafe, or a fire are all
   times to get a grown-up right away. A broken toy you might fix or leave.
3. **Who can help? (10 min).** Name the trusted grown-ups in your life — at home, at
   school, in your neighborhood. Point to a partner and say one grown-up you would ask.
4. **Practice asking (10 min).** Take turns: one person is the grown-up, one person
   asks clearly. "I need help — I hurt my knee," or "I feel scared." Speak in a calm,
   clear voice — or sign, point, tap a shoulder, or show a picture. Asking looks
   different for everyone, and every way of asking works.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: asking for help keeps your body safe. It is always
   okay to ask.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two or three scenarios and practice one help phrase many times;
  model the words and tone.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to add their own scenario to the sort and to role-play
  asking a specific trusted grown-up by name.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a scenario and say one trusted
  grown-up to ask, with guidance?
- **Self:** The learner names one situation where they would ask for help this week.

## Home connection

With a grown-up, name the trusted adults you could ask for help at home, at school, and
in your neighborhood. Practice saying "I need help" in a clear, calm voice.
