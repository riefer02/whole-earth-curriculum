# Lesson 3 — Balance and Control

## Summary

Learners practice staying steady — walking a line, standing still, and balancing a
small object — with growing control. They learn that wobbling is not failing; it is
the body's way of learning, and every body finds its own steady point.

## Objectives

- Demonstrate big movements (balance) with growing control. _(D03.S2.K.01)_

## Connection

Balance shows up everywhere in your life. You balance along a curb, a log, or the edge
of a boat; you carry a bowl of water without spilling; you stand on one foot to pull on
a shoe. People everywhere need balance — a fisher balancing in a boat, a dancer holding
a pose, a child crossing a stepping-stone path. Learning to balance helps you move
through the world with care and confidence.

## Materials

- A line or rope (chalk line, rope, or low curb)
- A beanbag or soft object to balance
- Music (optional)

## Preparation

- Lay a straight, low line or rope on the ground for balancing.
- Clear space so learners can move slowly without bumping each other.
- Remind learners that wobbling is normal and expected.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). As facilitator, frame **wobbling as
learning**, never as failure. **Honor every body:** a learner who cannot stand can
balance an object on a knee or open hand while seated; a learner who cannot walk a
line can hold a steady seated pose. Pair the "statue" voice with a visual signal —
a raised hand or a lifted scarf — so learners who cannot hear the cue know when to
freeze. Keep the tone calm and slow — balance grows from stillness and breath, not speed.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Stand or sit tall like a mountain. Take one slow breath in and
   out. Feel your body get still and ready.
2. **Warm up (5 min).** Rock gently side to side, then front to back, finding the
   middle point where you feel steady.
3. **Walk the line (10 min).** Walk slowly along the line, one careful foot in front
   of the other. If you wobble, stop, breathe, and begin again. If walking is hard,
   balance an object on your open hand while you sit or stay still.
4. **Balance an object (10 min).** Place a beanbag or soft object on your open hand,
   your knee, or your head. Hold it as still as you can, then try moving one slow step
   while it stays.
5. **Still as a statue (10 min).** When the music or a voice says "statue," hold your
   balance completely still. Count quietly to five before you relax. Notice which
   muscles help you stay steady.
6. **Close (5 min).** Shake out your arms and legs. What helped you stay steady today —
   slow breathing, watching a fixed point, or something else?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Allow seated or supported versions of every balance; use a wider line
  and a lighter object.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to balance on tiptoe or with eyes open and then
  closed, and to walk the line while balancing an object on their head.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner hold a balance with growing control
  and recover from a wobble, using an adapted version if needed?
- **Self:** The learner names one thing that helps them stay steady.

## Home connection

Practice balancing at home — walk a crack in the floor, stand on one foot while
brushing your teeth, or balance a folded cloth on your head. Tell a grown-up how you
found your steady point.
