# Lesson 5 — My Senses Notice the World

## Summary

Learners revisit the five senses, this time in motion and in connection with the
world around them. They name each sense, match it to the body part that does the
sensing, and explore safe objects to discover what each sense tells them.

## Objectives

- Name the five senses and identify a body part used for each. _(D03.S1.K.01)_

## Connection

Your senses are always on, telling you about the world. Your eyes see a bird fly
overhead, your ears hear a pot bubbling in the kitchen, your nose smells bread baking,
your tongue tastes a sweet fruit, and your hands feel the warmth of the sun on your
skin. Every person's senses work a little differently — some people hear less and feel
more, some see differently than you — and every way of sensing the world is a full way
to know it.

## Materials

- Five-sense picture cards (eye, ear, nose, tongue, hand)
- A mystery bag with safe objects to feel, smell, or hear
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw five-sense picture cards.
- Fill a mystery bag with safe objects (a smooth stone, a leaf, a bell, a lemon peel).
- Remind learners: nothing goes near the mouth or nose unless an adult says it is safe.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). As facilitator, describe each sense
aloud and name the body part so learners who cannot see the cards can still join.
**Honor every body:** a learner with low vision or hearing is not "missing" a sense —
they sense the world fully in their own way, often with stronger other senses. Frame
all five senses as doors to the world, and celebrate the different ways people use
them. Only taste should be limited for safety, and only with a safe, adult-approved
food.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit comfortably. Look around, listen, and take a sniff. Your
   senses are already working!
2. **Meet the five senses (10 min).** Point to each body part that senses the world:
   eyes (see), ears (hear), nose (smell), tongue (taste), and hands or skin (touch).
   Say each sense aloud and wiggle or point to the body part that does it.
3. **Mystery bag (15 min).** Reach into the bag without looking. Feel the object with
   your hands — is it rough, smooth, soft, or hard? Guess what it is, then look. If an
   adult says it is safe, you may also smell or listen to it. Name which sense told
   you the most.
4. **Draw a sense at work (10 min).** Choose one sense and draw a picture of you using
   it — your eyes seeing a rainbow, your nose smelling soup, your hands touching a
   kitten.
5. **Close (5 min).** Which sense do you think you use the most? Which one would you
   like to notice more today?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on two or three senses at a time; offer objects with one strong,
  clear quality (very smooth, very soft).
- **Extension:** Invite learners to describe an object using two senses at once and to
  name which sense gives the clearest clue.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the senses and point to the body
  part used for each?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's drawing of a sense at work.

## Home connection

At home, play "what do I sense?" with a grown-up — close your eyes and guess a food by
smell or a sound by ear. Tell them which sense gave you the best clue.
