# Lesson 2 — A Story About the Beginning

## Summary

Learners hear the story of the beginning that scientists tell — the "Everywhere
Stretch," sometimes called the Big Bang — told gently as a story of growth. They
learn that scientists tell it because of things they can see and measure, that nobody
has to believe it, and that it is one story among many. They retell it with their
bodies.

## Objectives

- Listen to an origin story about the universe and retell it in their own words,
  knowing it is one story among many. _(D06.S4.K.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever blown up a balloon and watched it grow bigger and bigger, or seen dough
rise and stretch as it bakes? Those things grow from something small into something
big, the way this story says everything once did. It is one way people have found to
answer the biggest question there is: where did everything come from?

## Materials

- A long stretchy cloth or scarf
- A balloon (optional)
- Small stickers or beads (optional)

## Preparation

- Blow up a balloon a little (if using) and keep it ready to show stretching.
- Clear a wide space where everyone can crouch and stretch without bumping.

## Facilitator note

This is the heart of the unit's "inquiry over creed" voice. Tell the story with
warmth and wonder, never as a fact a child must accept. Use the words "scientists
tell this story because…" and "nobody has to believe it." Do not say "this is how it
really happened." Keep it gentle: nothing explodes or breaks; everything stretches and
grows. If a child offers their family's own story of beginnings, receive it with
respect and say "what a beautiful story — there are many."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit together. Remember your questions from last time. Today we
   meet one story that tries to answer the very biggest question.
2. **Hear the story (10 min).** Listen: a long, long time ago — longer than you can
   count — everything we can see was packed into one tiny point, smaller than a grain
   of sand. Then it began to stretch and grow, like a balloon filling with air,
   getting bigger and bigger. As it grew, it cooled, and tiny pieces came together to
   make stars, and later planets, and our Earth. And it is still stretching today.
   Some people call this the "Big Bang," but "Everywhere Stretch" is a nicer name.
3. **Why scientists tell it (5 min).** People who study the sky with big telescopes
   can see faraway groups of stars — galaxies — moving apart, like dots on a balloon
   being blown up. That is their clue that everything was once close together. That
   clue is called *evidence*: something you can see or measure.
4. **Act it out (15 min).** Crouch into the smallest ball you can. Then stretch slowly
   — arms out, fingers wide, standing tall — while we say the story together: "It
   stretched… and stretched… and it is still stretching." If you have a balloon and
   stickers, put stickers on it and blow it bigger to watch the "galaxies" move apart.
5. **Retell it your way (5 min).** Tell the story in your own words to a partner, any
   way you like. Remember: this is one story among many, and nobody has to believe it.
   Everyone is invited to wonder.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Tell the story in very short beats and let the learner echo each beat
  with a movement ("small," then "stretch," then "stars," then "still growing").
- **Extension:** Invite learners to tell the story backward or to wonder what "before
  the beginning" might mean, and to name one question the story leaves open.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner retell the beginning story in their own
  words and say it is one story among many?
- **Performance:** The body retelling (crouch-and-stretch) shows the idea of growth.
