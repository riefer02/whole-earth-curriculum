# Lesson 1 — A Story About the Beginning

## Summary

Learners meet the oldest human question — where did everything come from? — as
*wonder*. The Big Bang is told gently as a story that scientists tell because of the
clues they have found, and it is held beside other peoples' origin stories. Nobody is
asked to believe; everyone is invited to wonder.

## Objectives

- Listen to an origin story about the universe and retell it in their own words,
  knowing it is one story among many. *(D06.S4.K.01)*
- Ask "why" and "how" questions about big things. *(D06.S1.K.01)*

## Connection

The things around you — the Sun, the Moon, the trees, your own body — all have a
beginning. When you look at the night sky or watch a seed grow, you are looking at a
small piece of the same big story. Asking where things come from is how every
scientist and every storyteller begins.

## Materials

- A picture sequence: a tiny, hot beginning; then stars; then galaxies; then Earth.
- Drawing paper and crayons.
- A "wonder box" (a decorated box for questions and drawings).

## Preparation

- Prepare the picture sequence of the universe's story.
- Recall one or two other origin stories from different cultures to mention briefly.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is *not* a religion lesson, and it is *not* a test of the Big Bang. The
Big Bang is offered as a story grounded in evidence — the story many scientists tell
because of what they have observed — while other peoples' origin stories are honored
as meaningful, too. The goal is curiosity and comfort with big questions, not belief.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit comfortably. Wonder: where did everything come from — the
   stars, the Earth, the people?
2. **Hear the story (10 min).** Listen to the story of the beginning, and follow the
   pictures: "Once, a very, very long time ago, everything we can see was packed into
   something unimaginably small and hot. And then — it began to grow and stretch, and
   slowly, slowly, it became stars, and galaxies, and our Earth." Scientists tell this
   story because they have looked at the sky and found clues that point to it.
3. **Many stories (10 min).** People all over the world, for a very long time, have
   told other beautiful stories about beginnings, too. We can listen to all of them.
   If you wish, share a story your family tells.
4. **Wonder box (15 min).** Draw your favorite part of the story — or draw a question
   you have about the beginning — and place it in the wonder box.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: some questions are so big that even grown-ups are
   still wondering. Wondering together is one of the best things humans do.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-picture version (before / after) and model one question.
- **Extension:** Invite a learner to "tell the story back" to a partner using the
  pictures.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner ask a question or retell part of the
  story?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The drawing placed in the wonder box.
