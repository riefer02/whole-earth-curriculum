# Lesson 4 — Sharing and Taking Turns

## Summary

Learners practice sharing and taking turns with a real object, then meet a deeper
idea: fair does not always mean *the same amount* — sometimes fair means everyone
gets *what they need*.

## Objectives

- Tell when something is fair or unfair in a simple situation like sharing or taking
  turns. _(D09.S2.K.01)_

## Connection

One swing, one ball, one snack to split — sharing and taking turns are how a group
makes one thing work for everyone. Sometimes fairness means each person gets the
same; other times fairness means each person gets what they need, which can look
different. A tall person and a small person may need different stools to see the
same window — that is fair, and it is not the same.

## Materials

- A shared object to pass (ball, shell, or "talking stick")
- A timer or a short song to mark turns
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Choose a shared object to pass and a way to mark turns (song, counting, or timer).
- Arrange a circle.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson introduces *equity* gently: fair is not
always equal. Use the stool-and-window or snack-and-hunger examples; keep it concrete
and never abstract. Some learners will insist "same amount" is the only fair way —
honor that as one real way, and hold the "what you need" idea alongside it as a
question to wonder about, not a correction. Turn-taking should feel cooperative, not
competitive; no one wins or loses. The passing object echoes talking circles used
by many Indigenous peoples around the world, where an object passes from speaker to
speaker so each voice is heard — we use it respectfully, not as decoration. A learner
who cannot hold or pass the object still takes a turn: a partner passes it for them,
or they point to a picture, sign, or nod their name or the thing they like. Sharing
your name or one thing you like can be spoken, signed, pointed to, or shown with a
gesture — no one is required to speak aloud.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** What is one thing in your home or class that everyone has to
   share?
2. **Take turns (15 min).** Pass the shared object around the circle (or have a
   partner pass it for you). When it is your turn, share your name or one thing you
   like — say it, sign it, or point to it. Everyone gets a turn — that is fair. Use
   the song or timer to mark each turn.
3. **Same or need? (10 min).** Think together: a big child and a small child both
   want to see out the window. The small child needs a stool; the big child does not.
   Is giving the stool to the small child fair? Yes — fair can mean *what each needs*,
   not *the same amount*.
4. **Draw it (10 min).** Draw one picture of sharing the same way, or one picture of
   sharing by what each person needs. Tell a partner which kind of fair your picture
   shows.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: sharing and taking turns are fair — and fair can
   sometimes mean each person gets what they need.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only "same amount" sharing with a clear, equal split; model
  counting out equal groups.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to invent their own "different needs" example (a
  coat for a cold friend, a bigger piece for a hungry friend) and share it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner tell a fair and an unfair way to
  share or take turns, and begin to name "what each needs" as fair?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "same or need" drawing, kept in the learner's
  portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, share something one of two ways — the same amount, or by what each person
needs — and tell a grown-up which way you chose and why it was fair.
