# Lesson 3 — How Many Are There?

## Summary

Learners deepen the idea of **cardinality** — that the last number spoken when
counting a group is the answer to "how many there are." They count several
collections of different sizes, say the total for each, and check by counting again.

## Objectives

- Count a group of objects and name the total number ("how many"). _(D05.S1.K.01)_

## Connection

Whenever someone asks "how many?" they are asking you to count — the fish in the
net, the eggs in the basket, the people at the table. The answer is the last number
you say when you count, and then you can count again to be sure. Counting and
checking is how people everywhere make sure nothing is lost and everyone gets their
fair share.

## Materials

- Several small collections of different sizes (3, 7, 12, 20 items), each in its own
  cup or pile
- A cloth or box for hiding a collection

## Preparation

- Prepare 3-5 collections of different sizes per learner or pair (for example 3
  shells, 7 beans, 12 pebbles, 20 leaves).
- Arrange them so each collection has a clear, separate spot.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The key idea is **cardinality**: the
last counting word is the number of things in the set — a concept young children
master at different times and must be allowed to arrive at gradually (Clements &
Sarama, 2014). Some learners will touch-and-count perfectly and still answer "five"
no matter how many there are; that is a normal step on the way, not a failure. When
that happens, ask gently, "What was the last number you said?" rather than correcting.
Never rank learners or praise only the fastest. Count in the home language. Learners
who are blind or low-vision can count by moving objects one at a time into a cup and
noticing the growing pile by feel; learners who are deaf or hard of hearing can sign
the numbers. Learners with limited hand or body movement name totals by voice or sign
while a partner moves each object, or indicate each object with eye-gaze or a mouth
stick for a partner to move and count. The "how many?" game should feel like play,
never like a test.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at your collections. Some look big, some look small. You
   cannot always tell how many just by looking — you have to count.
2. **Count and name (15 min).** Pick one collection. Count it, touching each thing
   and saying one number for it. The **last number you say is how many**. Say it
   clearly: "There are seven." Do this for each collection.
3. **Count again to check (10 min).** Count the same collection a second time. Does
   the last number match? Counting twice and getting the same answer is how you know
   your count is true.
4. **The how-many game (10 min).** With a friend, one of you hides a small pile
   under a cloth. The other peeks for only a blink, then must count carefully to say
   "how many." Switch turns. No rushing — slow counting is good counting.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the last number you say is your answer. Count once,
   then count again to check. That is how we know.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use very small collections (1-4) and count hand over hand, pausing on
  the last object to say the total together.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to line up their collections from fewest to most
  without counting first, then count to check their guess.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** When a learner finishes counting, do they state the
  last number as the total?
- **Self:** Ask the learner to count one collection twice and tell whether their two
  answers matched.

## Resources

- Clements, D. H., & Sarama, J. (2014). *Learning and Teaching Early Math: The
  Learning Trajectories Approach* (2nd ed.). Routledge. (Cardinality — the last
  counting word names the quantity.)

## Home connection

At home, find two or three small collections (beans, spoons, toys). Count each one,
say "how many," then count again to check. Ask a grown-up to hide a few things so
you can count and say how many.
