# Lesson 4 — Patterns All Around Us

## Summary

Learners learn that a pattern is something that repeats — again and again — and go on
a hunt for patterns in the world around them: striped cloth, woven baskets, tile
floors, music, dance, and nature. They discover that people everywhere make patterns,
and that finding patterns is a way of seeing.

## Objectives

- Recognize a simple repeating pattern in colors, shapes, or sounds. _(D05.S2.K.01)_

## Connection

Patterns are everywhere you look and listen. A striped cloth repeats red-blue-red-blue.
A woven basket crosses over-under-over-under. Music repeats a beat, and your footsteps
fall left-right-left-right. People in every land make patterns — in woven cloth, in
baskets, in songs and dances — because a pattern is simply something that happens
again and again. Today you will hunt for them.

## Materials

- A handful of objects in two colors or two kinds (red and green leaves, two kinds of
  seeds)
- Pictures or real examples of patterned things (a basket, striped cloth, a shell)

## Preparation

- Gather a few patterned examples (cloth, basket, tiled surface, feather) or pictures
  of them.
- Choose an indoor or outdoor area rich enough to hunt for patterns.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to plant is simply "a pattern
repeats." Do not yet demand that learners extend or build patterns — that is the next
lesson. Here, let them *see* patterns everywhere and feel the delight of recognition.
Center patterns from many cultures as equals: woven baskets, kente and other striped
and zigzag textiles, molas, beadwork, tile mosaics, and the repeating beats of drum
music are all examples of people making order through repetition — none is "better,"
each is beautiful in its own tradition. Be careful not to treat only printed or
European patterns as "real" patterns. Honor sound and movement patterns (clapping,
drumming, dance steps) as fully equal to visual ones, and invite learners who are
blind or low-vision to find patterns by touch and sound, and learners who are deaf or
hard of hearing to find them by sight and rhythm they can feel. Learners with limited
movement find and share patterns by voice or sign — saying what repeats, or choosing
examples with eye-gaze or a mouth stick — while a partner points or traces. There is
no "wrong" pattern to notice.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Say a little rhythm with me: clap-stomp, clap-stomp, clap-stomp.
   What kept happening? The same thing, again and again. That is a **pattern** — it
   **repeats**.
2. **Meet patterns (10 min).** Look at the patterned examples. Trace the stripes or
   the weave with your finger and say it: "over-under, over-under." Listen to a beat:
   "tap-tap-rest, tap-tap-rest." A pattern is anything that happens **again and
   again**.
3. **Pattern hunt (20 min).** Go on a hunt, indoors or outdoors. Use your eyes,
   ears, and hands. Find three patterns. They might be stripes, rows, waves, beats, or
   footsteps. Tell a friend where you found each one.
4. **Share (5 min).** Come back and share one pattern you found. Can the group say
   what repeats in it?
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: patterns are everywhere, in every land and every
   life. Finding them is a way of seeing the order and beauty around you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Hunt for one pattern together, then say its repeat aloud ("red-blue,
  red-blue") while tracing it, before hunting independently.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to find a pattern and say which part is the "repeat"
  — the little piece that happens over and over.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner point to a repeating pattern and name
  what repeats (color, shape, or sound)?

## Home connection

At home, hunt for three patterns with a grown-up — in cloth, on the floor, in music,
or in nature — and tell each other what repeats.
