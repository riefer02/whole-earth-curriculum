# Lesson 1 — Counting Real Things

## Summary

Learners learn the heart of counting: saying one number for each thing, without
skipping or double-counting. They practice "touch-and-count" on real objects they
can hold — pebbles, seeds, shells — and discover that the last number they say tells
how many there are.

## Objectives

- Count objects by matching one number to one object and say how many there are.
  _(D05.S1.K.01)_

## Connection

Counting happens all around you, every single day — a seller counts fruit at a
market, a child lines up river stones, a runner counts footsteps, you count your own
fingers. People everywhere count, but not with the same words or the same marks:
counting is done many ways, in many languages, and every way is real counting.
Today you will practice your own way of counting the real things in front of you.

## Materials

- About 10 small, safe objects per learner (pebbles, seeds, shells, bottle caps)
- A cloth or mat to count on

## Preparation

- Gather or help learners gather a collection of small objects (about 10 each).
- Give each learner a cloth, mat, or clear flat space to count on.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. The single most important idea here is **one-to-one
correspondence** — one number for one object — which is a well-documented first step
in early counting (Clements & Sarama, 2014). Model it slowly: touch an object, say
"one"; touch the next, say "two." Resist the urge to rush or to correct a learner who
is still practicing; accuracy grows with time and many gentle tries. Counting is a
skill everyone builds at their own pace, and **no one's speed is a measure of their
smartness**. Always count in the language the learner understands best — the home
language is welcome and just as "real" as any other way of counting. Learners who are
blind or low-vision can count by touch (moving each object into a cup) or by sound
(tapping, clicking); learners who are deaf or hard of hearing can count in sign, with
each signed number matched to one object. All of these are genuine counting. Learners
with limited hand or body movement count by voice or sign alone: a partner touches and
moves each object while the learner names each number, or the learner chooses each
object with eye-gaze or a mouth stick and the partner moves and counts it — the math
is in matching number to thing, not in the moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit with your collection of small things in front of you.
   Look at them. How many do you think there are? You do not need to know yet — you
   are about to find out.
2. **Watch touch-and-count (10 min).** Counting means giving each thing its own
   number. Touch one thing and say "one." Touch the next and say "two." Never skip a
   thing, and never give one thing two numbers. Try it with a friend on a few things.
3. **Count your own collection (15 min).** Now count your things slowly, touching
   each one and saying one number for it. When you finish, the last number you say is
   **how many** there are.
4. **Check it (10 min).** Count the same collection again, this time moving each
   thing onto a cloth or into a line as you count. Did you get the same number both
   times? If not, try once more, even slower. There is no prize for being fast — slow
   and careful is how we count true.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: counting is a way of paying attention to the things
   around you. One thing, one number. That is all counting ever is.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start with a very small collection (3-5 objects) and count together,
  hand over hand if helpful; name each number aloud and let the learner echo it.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to count a larger collection (up to 10) and to
  count objects they cannot touch, pointing carefully at each one instead.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner say one number for each object
  without skipping or double-counting?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner tell, after counting, how many there are?

## Home connection

At home, count something real: the spoons on the table, the steps to the door, the
socks in a pile. Touch each one as you say its number, and tell a grown-up how many
there are.
