# Lesson 10 — Counting, Shapes & Patterns

## Summary

The capstone of the unit: learners do all four things they have learned — count,
sort, make a pattern, and name shapes — with one collection of real objects, and
share their work in a class "math museum" where every piece is celebrated as it is.

## Objectives

- Count objects up to twenty and say how many there are. _(D05.S1.K.01)_
- Continue or copy a simple repeating pattern. _(D05.S2.K.01)_
- Name and match common shapes. _(D05.S3.K.01)_
- Sort objects into groups by one feature and tell which group has more.
  _(D05.S4.K.01)_

## Connection

All month you have been a mathematician — counting real things, finding patterns,
naming shapes, and sorting into groups. Today you put it all together, the way people
do every day all over the world: a weaver counts her threads and follows a pattern; a
builder names his shapes and sorts his stones; a gardener sorts her seeds and counts
how many. Math is how you see the world more clearly.

## Materials

- A mixed collection of objects (seeds, buttons, leaves, stones, blocks)
- Paper and drawing tools for a "museum card"

## Preparation

- Set up four small "stations" or jobs: count, sort, pattern, shape.
- Prepare a space (table, shelf, or cloth on the ground) to display finished work as
  a "math museum."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is a celebration, not a test — the
goal is for each learner to *show* what they can do and feel proud, never to be ranked
against others. Run the four jobs as flexible stations (or as a self-directed checklist
for one learner). Every child works at their own pace and may choose which job to
linger on; mastery is individual, and the "museum" belongs to everyone together
(philosophy §8). Keep the global lens alive: the same four moves — counting, sorting,
patterning, shaping — live in weaving, building, farming, and music across the world,
and no tradition is the model to copy. When learners share, praise the *thinking*
("you counted carefully," "your pattern repeats") rather than speed or size. For
learners who are blind or low-vision, counting, sorting, and patterning can all be
done by touch and sound, and shapes by feel; for learners who are deaf or hard of
hearing, the sharing can be signed or drawn. Learners with limited movement complete
each job by voice or sign, directing a partner to move, sort, or pattern objects, or
choosing with eye-gaze or a mouth stick — the thinking is theirs. The museum is for
everyone, and every piece has a place.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have learned four math superpowers. Name them: **counting**,
   **sorting**, **patterns**, and **shapes**. Today you will use them all.
2. **Count your collection (10 min).** Take your pile and count it, touching each
   thing. How many are there? Say the number.
3. **Sort it (10 min).** Now sort the same pile into groups by one feature — color,
   size, or kind. Which group has more?
4. **Make a pattern (10 min).** With some of your things, build a repeating pattern.
   Say it aloud and keep it going. Can a friend continue it?
5. **Find the shapes (5 min).** Look at your things and around you. Name one circle,
   one square, one triangle, and one rectangle. Where are they?
6. **Open the math museum (5 min).** Choose your favorite piece — your counted pile,
   your sorted groups, or your pattern — and put it in the museum. Tell a friend what
   you did, or draw a card for it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer one job at a time with a partner, and let a learner contribute a
  single piece (a counted pile or a simple pattern) to the museum.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to combine jobs — for example, sort, then count each
  group and make a pattern with the larger group — and to label their museum piece.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Across the four jobs, does the learner demonstrate
  counting to a total, sorting by one feature with a "more" comparison, a repeating
  pattern, and naming shapes?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's chosen "museum" piece, kept as a record of
  the unit.

## Home connection

At home, do a "math tour" with a grown-up: count one collection, sort it, make a
pattern with it, and name the shapes around you — then tell each other which job you
liked best.
