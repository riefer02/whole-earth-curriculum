# Lesson 6 — Meet the Shapes

## Summary

Learners meet four common shapes — circle, square, triangle, and rectangle — by
feeling their sides and corners and finding them in real objects: a round plate, a
square window, a triangular roof, a rectangular door. They discover that the same
shapes appear in buildings, baskets, and nature all over the world.

## Objectives

- Name common shapes (circle, square, triangle, rectangle). _(D05.S3.K.01)_

## Connection

Shapes are everywhere, in every place people live. The sun and the moon look round
like a **circle**; many windows and doors are **squares** and **rectangles**; roofs
and tents often rise to a **triangle**. Whether a house is made of brick, wood, cloth,
or stone, and whether it stands in a city or on a hillside, the same shapes hold it
up. Today you will learn their names and find them all around you.

## Materials

- A few real shape examples (plate, book, block, folded paper triangle)
- A set of paper shapes (circles, squares, triangles, rectangles)

## Preparation

- Gather real objects that are clear examples of each shape.
- Cut or prepare paper shapes in the four kinds, various sizes.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The goal is naming and recognizing
four shapes by their attributes — a circle is round with no corners; a square has four
equal sides and four corners; a triangle has three sides; a rectangle has four sides
with two long and two short. Teach the shapes through *touch and feel first* — sides
and corners are the real, physical facts of a shape, before names or marks. Use real
objects from the learners' world (a plate, a door, a tent, a woven mat). Draw on
global examples without ranking: roundhouses, pyramid shapes, triangular roofs, and
rectangular doorways exist in many traditions, and none is "the" right way to build.
Learners who are blind or low-vision should feel each shape's sides and corners and
count them by touch; learners who are deaf or hard of hearing can name shapes in sign.
Learners with limited movement can feel shapes hand-under-hand with a partner (the
partner's hand under theirs, guiding) or name and count sides and corners aloud while
a partner traces.
Never quiz or rush; naming shapes is a beginning, and every learner's pace is honored.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look around. The things near you are made of shapes. Today
   you will meet four of them.
2. **Meet the shapes (15 min).** Feel each shape with your hands and say its name. A
   **circle** is round with no corners. A **square** has four equal sides and four
   corners. A **triangle** has three sides and three corners. A **rectangle** has
   four sides — two long and two short. Trace each one with your finger.
3. **Shape hunt (15 min).** Find one real thing that is each shape. A plate is a
   circle. A door is a rectangle. Can you find a square? A triangle? Point and name
   them.
4. **Draw it (5 min).** With a finger, draw each shape in the air, or in sand or
   earth if you are outdoors. Say its name as you draw.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: shapes are the building blocks of everything you see.
   Once you know their names, you see them everywhere.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Introduce one shape at a time; give learners a shape to hold and
  match to the same shape, before asking them to name it.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to count the sides and corners of each shape and to
  notice that a square is a rectangle with all four sides equal.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner point to and name each of the four
  shapes in real objects?

## Home connection

At home, hunt with a grown-up for the four shapes — a circle, a square, a triangle,
and a rectangle — and name each one you find.
