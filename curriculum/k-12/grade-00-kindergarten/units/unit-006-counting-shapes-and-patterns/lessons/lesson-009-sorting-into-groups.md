# Lesson 9 — Sorting Into Groups

## Summary

Learners sort a mixed collection into groups by **one feature** — color, size, or
kind — and then compare the groups to tell which has **more**. They discover that
sorting is how people everywhere organize their world, and that "more" is a fact,
never a judgment about a group's worth.

## Objectives

- Sort objects into groups by one feature and tell which group has more.
  _(D05.S4.K.01)_

## Connection

Sorting is something people do everywhere, every day — a gardener separates big seeds
from small ones for planting, a market seller groups fruit by kind, a family sorts
laundry into piles. When things are sorted, you can find them, count them, and see
which group has more. Sorting is a way of caring for the things around you.

## Materials

- A mixed collection of objects (red and blue buttons, or big and small leaves)
- Two mats, plates, or hoops for the two groups

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair one mixed pile that differs by one clear feature.
- Provide two clear places (mats, plates, hoops) for the two groups.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The key skill is classification by one
attribute and a simple comparison (which group has more). Let learners choose their
own feature to sort by — color, size, or kind are all fine — and ask them to say the
rule ("all the red ones here, all the blue ones there"). Comparing "more" is best done
by matching one-to-one in two lines, or by counting each group; both are valid, and
neither should be rushed. A central value point: **"more" is a fact, not a worth.**
A smaller group is not "worse," and a bigger group is not "better" — we say "which has
more" without ranking, so sorting stays a tool of care, not competition. Sorting is a
human activity everywhere (seeds, laundry, market goods), so draw on many examples and
honor each. Learners who are blind or low-vision can sort by touch (smooth vs. rough,
big vs. small by feel); learners who are deaf or hard of hearing can sign the rule and
count in sign. Learners with limited movement sort by voice or sign — directing a
partner ("red here, blue there") or choosing each object with eye-gaze or a mouth
stick while a partner moves it. Every learner's pace is honored.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at your mixed pile. It is a jumble. What could you do to
   make sense of it? You could sort it into groups.
2. **Choose a rule (10 min).** Pick **one feature** to sort by — color, size, or kind.
   Say your rule: "All the red ones here, all the blue ones there." Sort your pile
   into the two places.
3. **Compare the groups (15 min).** Now find out which group has **more**. Line the
   two groups up side by side, one to one, or count each group. Which has more? Which
   has fewer? Remember: "more" just tells a fact — it does not make one group better.
4. **Sort a new way (10 min).** Mix everything up again. Can you sort by a different
   feature this time? Say your new rule and compare the groups again.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: sorting is how we organize the world so we can see
   it. One feature at a time — that is all it takes.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pile that differs in one obvious way (two colors) and sort it
  together first, saying the rule aloud, before the learner sorts alone.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to sort by two features in turn (first color, then
  size) and to say which group has more each time, and why "more" changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort by one feature and state the rule
  ("all the...")?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** A photo or drawing of the learner's two sorted groups,
  with a note of which had more.

## Home connection

At home, sort something real with a grown-up — spoons and forks, or socks by color —
say your sorting rule, and tell which group has more.
