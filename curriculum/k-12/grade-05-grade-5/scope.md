# Grade 5 Scope

## How to read this scope

Each `unit_id` in the frontmatter resolves to a unit file under `units/`.
`start_day`/`end_day` map the unit onto the 180-day school year; the list is a
continuous sequence from day 1 to day 180 with no gaps or overlaps. `domain` and
`pillar` name the primary domain(s) and pillar(s) each unit serves. `domain_weighting`
expresses the intended balance of instructional time across the ten domains, in
percent of the year. `lesson_count` counts the core lessons; the days between lessons
carry flex time, assessment, integration, and celebration. `assessment_plan`
describes how a child's growth is seen and recorded at this level.

## Year at a glance

The Grade 5 year is a spiral that deepens what Grade 4 began — and it opens with the
question at the heart of every science: *how do we know?* The opening unit, *How Do
We Know?*, establishes the year's through-line. Where Grade 4 learned to measure with
tools and compare repeated trials, Grade 5 now *designs* the test: learners form a
testable question, plan an investigation, and identify what stays the same and what
changes, and they learn to separate what they saw — evidence — from what they reason —
inference — from what they merely think — opinion — in a hands-on investigation such
as testing whether heavy things always sink. That one distinction will echo through
the whole year: through reading, history, media, and the arts alike.

Then the year turns inward. *My Feelings, My Values & My Growing Self* builds on
Grade 4's feeling-intensity and complex feelings: now learners explain how thoughts,
memories, and body sensations can shape a feeling, and compare how feelings are
expressed in different families, cultures, and situations. They name the values they
care about — fairness, honesty, kindness — and give an example of living by one, and
explain how a person's identity is shaped by family, culture, place, and history. They
monitor their own behavior and feelings during a task and name what helped or hurt
their focus, and take responsibility for a choice and its effects on others, naming a
better next step. *My Body, My Movement & My Growth* then reads the body with new
precision: the mind–body connection — how worry can make a stomach feel tight — and
how bodies grow and change at different rates, every pace normal. Learners refine a
specific motor skill through repeated practice and give a peer helpful feedback, and
explain how regular movement benefits the heart, muscles, and mood.

*Teams, Empathy & Belonging* turns outward into the practice of care. Where Grade 4
learned to paraphrase and negotiate fairly, Grade 5 learns to give and receive
feedback kindly and use it to improve shared work, and to cooperate in a team by
dividing tasks fairly and following through on one's part. They take the perspective
of a person from a different culture or place and describe their daily life, and
recognize when a first guess about someone's feelings might be wrong — and check with
them. They explain how communities are built through shared work, symbols, and
stories, investigate how people in a community meet shared needs such as food,
safety, and learning, describe examples of mutual aid from different cultures and
times, and practice fairness by dividing resources so everyone has enough. The heart
of the year is given to the two great tools of school, now working with real
independence. *Reading, Writing, Research & Many Languages* builds fluency and
comprehension: paraphrasing a speaker's points accurately and asking clarifying
questions when unsure, giving a short presentation with a clear main idea, supporting
reasons, and a conclusion, explaining how an author uses structure — cause and effect,
problem and solution — to organize ideas, and distinguishing fact from opinion and
identifying the author's purpose and point of view. They write for different purposes
and audiences, choosing an appropriate style and structure, and gather information
from several sources, note key facts, and write a short report in their own words.
They recognize that many writing systems exist — alphabets and character scripts —
and identify a few, and describe how language is tied to culture, such as words for
family, food, or respect that differ across languages — always in the learner's home
language first, with multilingualism held as wealth. *Decimals, Fractions, Shapes &
Data* grows from Grade 4's place value to 1,000,000 into decimals and the coordinate
plane: reading, writing, comparing, and rounding decimals to the thousandths and
connecting them to fractions with denominators of 10 and 100, adding, subtracting,
multiplying, and dividing fractions and decimals in real contexts such as sharing,
cooking, and money, plotting points and graphing relationships in the first quadrant
of a coordinate grid, writing and evaluating simple expressions, graphing points and
using them to solve problems about location and distance, finding the volume of right
rectangular prisms with unit cubes and formulas, converting among standard
measurement units, and representing and interpreting data on line plots with
fractions — mathematics as sense-making with real things, drawn from many traditions,
for everyone.

From there the year looks out at the world. *Food Webs, Forces, Earth & Sky* deepens
the science the year opened with: explaining how matter and energy move through a
food web and what can happen when one part changes, describing how traits pass from
parents to offspring and how some traits vary within a family, explaining that matter
is made of particles too small to see and using the idea to explain changes of state,
describing gravity and magnetism as forces that act at a distance, describing the
Earth–Moon–Sun system and explaining day length, moon phases, and eclipses, and
explaining how Earth's surface is changed by slow and fast processes, and where
earthquakes and volcanoes occur. *Making, Music & Movement* gives craft to the
imagination — planning and completing an artwork with a clear idea, composing a short
piece with a clear beginning, middle, and end, choreographing a short group dance or
scene, and designing and building something that solves a real problem — while
meeting the arts of other cultures as living traditions to honor with respectful
questions, citing what they observe and hear, never as specimens. *Healthy, Safe &
Strong* returns to the body with growing independence — how nutrients such as
carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, and water support different parts
of the body, how having too little or too much of certain foods affects the body over
time without judging any body, how to respond in an emergency and what information to
give, and the idea of consent for one's own body and respect for the boundaries of
others.

The final third steps into the wider human story. *People, Cultures & Long Ago* builds
a timeline that compares two cultures or civilizations and identifies what changed
and what stayed the same in each, explains how a tool or technology — writing, the
wheel, the plow — changed how people lived and worked, compares origin stories from
two or more peoples and explains how each reflects the people who tell it, describes
an achievement in art, building, or learning from a non-European civilization, traces
the migration of a specific people and the push and pull reasons for their movement,
explains how trade and travel connected peoples across land and sea and what moved
with them, describes a historical example of people treated unfairly who resisted or
helped one another, and explains what fairness and dignity mean using a concrete
example of when they were honored or denied — history told as a global story from many
perspectives, never one people's triumph. *Rights, Fairness & Shared Decisions*
explains how a person's values can shape their choices, compares two possible choices
and their consequences for others before deciding, explains what a right is, where
some rights come from — such as the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child — and why
they apply to all children, compares how fairness was or was not honored in a
historical or story example and who benefited, explains what government is and what
it does and compares how decisions are made in more than one country, plans and
carries out a small collective action for the common good and reflects on what made
it work, helps two peers solve a simple conflict by having each side listen and find a
fair solution, and explains the difference between conflict and violence — and why
disagreements can be healthy when handled well. *Tools, Media & Our Earth Home* closes
the year by gathering everything together: explaining how a technology people use
every day has changed daily life over time, comparing how people in two different
places use a similar technology differently, evaluating whether a claim is likely
true by checking more than one source, recognizing when a message favors one side and
what fair reporting would include, tracing the path of energy or matter through a
cycle or food web, explaining how living things in an ecosystem depend on one another
and what can happen when one part changes, describing a form of pollution or habitat
loss and something people can do to reduce it, and explaining what it means to use a
resource carefully so it lasts — then celebrating each whole person, the caring
community, and the living Earth, and looking ahead to Grade 6.

Throughout, the four pillars are interwoven. Intellectual awareness (P3) carries the
most weight — fact-and-opinion reading, research writing, decimals and fractions, and
designed, controlled tests are the year's central work — but emotional and social
awareness (P1) and physical and somatic awareness (P2) remain strong, for a known,
cared-for, and capable body-and-self is still the ground all formal learning stands
on. Contextual and ecological awareness (P4) keeps growing: history moves toward
comparing civilizations and tracing migration, civics toward rights, government, and
collective action, and ecology toward food webs, cycles, pollution, and stewardship.
No high-stakes tests anywhere: a child's growth is seen through reading aloud,
writing, number work with real things, designed fair tests, and a portfolio of what
they make, say, and do.
