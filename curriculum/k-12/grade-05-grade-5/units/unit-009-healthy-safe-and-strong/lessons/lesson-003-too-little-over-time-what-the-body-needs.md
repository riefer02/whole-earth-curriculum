# Lesson 3 — Too Little Over Time: What the Body Needs

## Summary

Learners discover what happens when the body gets **too little** of some nutrients **over
time**: too little **iron** brings tiredness and low energy, too little **calcium and vitamin D**
weakens bones, and too little **water** brings thirst and headache. The lesson is framed as
what **every body needs** — never as shame — and connects each shortage to foods that supply
what is missing, honoring the fact that some families have less because of unfair systems, not
because of anything a person did.

## Objectives

- Describe how having too little of certain foods affects the body over time, without judging
  any body. _(D03.S3.05.02)_

## Connection

Water a plant too little, day after day, and it wilts — not because the plant is "bad," but
because it is missing something it needs. Your body is like that. Go too long with **too
little** of a needed **nutrient** — over days, weeks, or months, not just one meal — and the
body signals that something is missing. Too little **iron** and you feel **tired** and low on
energy, because your blood has less of what carries oxygen. Too little **calcium and vitamin D**
and your **bones** grow weaker. Too little **water** and you feel **thirsty**, with a dry mouth
and sometimes a headache. None of this is about a person being "wrong" — it is simply what a
body needs, the way a plant needs water.

## Materials

- Too little over time poster
- What the body needs page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the too-little-over-time poster (three panels: iron → tiredness, calcium and
  vitamin D → weak bones, water → thirst and headache).
- Copy a what-the-body-needs page for each learner.
- Recall Lessons 1–2: the six nutrients and their jobs; today is about what happens when there
  is too little of one, over time.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a body needs enough
of each nutrient, and too little over time has real effects — described with care, never
shame**. Keep the tone warm and factual. Be sensitive: some learners experience hunger or food
shortage. Always frame a shortage of food or nutrients as a **fairness and systems issue**
(food not being available or affordable where a family lives), never as a child's or family's
fault. Do not link any nutrient shortage to body size or shape. Connect every shortage to
agency — foods that supply what is missing — and to hope. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding
difficulty with hope.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You now know the six nutrients and their jobs. Today's question: what
   happens when the body gets **too little** of one of them, **over time**?
2. **Meet the three signals (10 min).** Look at the poster. *Too little **iron**, over time:*
   your blood carries less oxygen, so you feel **tired** and low on energy. *Too little
   **calcium and vitamin D**, over time:* your **bones** grow **weaker** and can bend or break
   more easily. *Too little **water**:* you feel **thirsty**, your mouth is dry, and your head
   may ache. Notice the words: this is about what a **body needs**, never about a person being
   "wrong."
3. **Think together (10 min).** Why might a body get too little of a nutrient? Sometimes a
   person just needs to drink more water. Sometimes food that supplies a nutrient — beans and
   leafy greens for iron, milk or leafy greens and sesame for calcium — is **hard to get or too
   costly** where a family lives. That is a **fairness problem** in how food is shared, not
   anyone's fault. What foods around you supply iron, calcium, and water?
4. **Record (15 min).** On your page, answer: what happens with too little iron, too little
   calcium and vitamin D, and too little water, over time — and name one food or drink that
   supplies each. Write or draw.
5. **Close (5 min).** A body sends signals when it is missing something — tired, weak, thirsty.
   Listening to those signals, and having enough of what the body needs, is a right every
   person deserves. Next time: the other side — too much of some things, over time.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("Too little iron makes me feel ___. A food with iron
  is ___.") and a picture bank; scribe as needed.
- **Extension:** Explain the difference between one missed meal and a nutrient shortage over
  weeks or months, and why the phrase "over time" matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe one effect of too little iron, too
  little calcium/vitamin D, and too little water over time, and name a food that supplies each —
  without judging any body?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks that their page names the effect *and* the missing nutrient
  *and* a food remedy, and that no body is described with shame.

## Home connection

Ask at home about foods that are easy or hard to get where you live, and share one nutrient a
body needs enough of and a food that supplies it.

## Resources

- Too little iron over time leads to iron-deficiency anaemia, whose main symptom is fatigue;
  too little calcium and vitamin D over time weakens bones; and too little water causes
  dehydration (thirst, headache, dry mouth). See the World Health Organization's fact sheet on
  anaemia (<https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anaemia>) and its nutrition and
  hydration guidance. Food shortages are best understood as a **food security** issue — the UN
  Food and Agriculture Organization documents unequal access to food worldwide
  (<https://www.fao.org/>) — a fairness problem, not any person's fault.
