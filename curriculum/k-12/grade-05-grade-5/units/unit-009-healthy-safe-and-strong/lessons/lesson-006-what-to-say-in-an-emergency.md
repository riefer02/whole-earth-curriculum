# Lesson 6 — What to Say in an Emergency

## Summary

Learners learn exactly **what to say** when they call for help: their **name**, their
**location**, and **what happened** — then to **stay on the line** and answer the helper's
questions. A worked example models the call, pairs role-play it, and each learner completes a
take-home **emergency-information card** with their name, location, a trusted adult, and the
local emergency number.

## Objectives

- Describe how to respond in an emergency, including what information to give. _(D03.S4.05.01)_

## Connection

Imagine the phone is answered by a helper who wants to send aid right away — but they cannot see
you. They need to know three things, and you are the one who can tell them: **who** you are,
**where** you are, and **what** happened. Say your **name** clearly. Say your **location** — your
address, or a landmark you can see, like "beside the blue water pump" or "the shop by the big
mango tree." Then say **what happened**, in a few calm words: "someone fell and cannot get up,"
"there is a fire in the kitchen." Then **stay on the line** and answer their questions. Three
pieces of information — that is the whole skill, and it can bring help to the right place.

## Materials

- Give-three-things poster
- Emergency-information card
- Role-play scripts

## Preparation

- Print or draw the give-three-things poster (name, location, what happened).
- Copy an emergency-information card for each learner and a role-play script for each pair.
- Recall Lesson 5 (recognize, get safe, get help, call); today is what to say once you are on
  the call.
- Prepare the correct local emergency number and a sample nearby landmark for the location line.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is the **three pieces of
information — name, location, what happened — plus staying on the line**. Model the full call
once as a worked example (explicit instruction for a novice skill), then let pairs role-play
with a pretend phone. Keep every scenario mild and calm; never simulate graphic injury. Some
learners may not know a formal street address — that is normal and fine; teach them to describe
a **landmark** ("the well," "the blue house," "next to the school") as a valid location. Send
the card home so a family can confirm the number and address together. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned the four steps: recognize, get safe, get help, call
   the number. Today: **what do you say** when the call is answered?
2. **Meet the three things (10 min).** Look at the poster. A helper who cannot see you needs
   three things. **Your name** — say it slowly and clearly. **Your location** — an address, or a
   **landmark** you can see. **What happened** — a few calm words, nothing more. Then **stay on
   the line** and answer their questions; do not hang up until they say you can.
3. **Watch the worked example (5 min).** Your facilitator and a partner show one full call.
   *"Hello, what is your name?"* *"My name is Amina."* *"Where are you?"* *"The blue house by
   the water pump."* *"What happened?"* *"Someone fell and cannot get up."* *"Stay with me.
   Help is on the way."* Notice the order: name, location, what happened.
4. **Role-play with a partner (15 min).** Take turns — one of you is the caller, one the helper.
   Use the scripts or make your own mild example. Say all three things, then stay on the line for
   two more questions. Then switch roles.
5. **Fill your card (5 min).** On your emergency-information card, write your **name**, your
   **location** (address or landmark), one **trusted adult**, and the **local emergency number**.
   Take it home to check with your family.
6. **Close (5 min).** Three things — name, location, what happened — and help can find you. That
   is a small skill with a big result. Next time, we turn to a different kind of safety: the
   right to say **yes or no** about your own body.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the three-finger reminder and a sentence frame ("My name is ___. I am at ___.
   ___ happened."); practice with the facilitator before pairing.
- **Accessibility:** A learner who does not speak can point to the three things on their card or
   the poster, write them, or use a communication board, sign, or device — a partner can read
   them aloud for the role-play, or the "call" can be acted non-verbally. Learners who are seated
   or use a device take the caller or helper role the same way.
- **Extension:** Explain why to stay on the line and follow the helper's questions, and practice
   answering a follow-up ("Is the person awake?").

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** In role-play, can the learner give their name, location, and
  what happened calmly, and stay on the line for a follow-up question?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their completed card and checks all three pieces of
  information plus the emergency number are present and correct.

## Home connection

Take your emergency-information card home, confirm the number and location with your family, and
practice the three things — name, location, what happened — together.

## Resources

- Emergency-call guidance for children centers on giving the caller's name, a clear location
  (address or nearby landmark), and a brief description of what happened, then staying on the
  line and following the dispatcher's questions. This is standard practice in emergency-dispatch
  training worldwide (see national emergency-service and Red Cross / Red Crescent first-aid
  guidance). Practice should always be non-dialing, with a family confirming the local number.
