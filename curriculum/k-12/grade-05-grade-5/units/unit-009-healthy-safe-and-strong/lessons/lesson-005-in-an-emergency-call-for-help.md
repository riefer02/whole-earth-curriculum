# Lesson 5 — In an Emergency: Call for Help

## Summary

Learners learn to **recognize an emergency** — a time someone is badly hurt, very sick, or in
danger and needs help **fast** — and to respond with a simple, calm four-step flow:
**recognize, get safe, tell a trusted adult, call the local emergency number**. They sort
everyday situations into "emergency" or "not," learn that emergency numbers differ around the
world, and record their **own local number** and a **trusted adult**. The tone is calm and
empowering — this is about becoming a helper, not about fear.

## Objectives

- Describe how to respond in an emergency, including how to call for help. _(D03.S4.05.01)_

## Connection

Most days nothing goes wrong. But sometimes something happens **fast** and serious — someone
falls and is badly hurt, a fire starts, or a person is very sick and cannot wake. That is an
**emergency**: a time when someone needs **help right away**, and you — even as a child — can be
the person who gets it. It does not mean doing everything yourself. It means knowing a simple
four-step path: **recognize** that this is serious, **get safe**, **tell a trusted adult**, and
**call the number for help**. Every country has a number to call for emergencies — and knowing
*yours* by heart is one of the most useful things you will ever learn.

## Materials

- Emergency-steps poster
- Is it an emergency? cards
- Local emergency number card

## Preparation

- Print or draw the emergency-steps poster (four calm steps: recognize, get safe, tell a trusted
  adult, call the local emergency number).
- Prepare a set of "is it an emergency?" cards with everyday situations, including calm,
  non-frightening examples.
- Find out and prepare the correct **local emergency number(s)** for your area (it differs by
  country — see Resources) so you can give learners the right one.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is a **calm, empowering
four-step response** — never fear. Keep every example mild and non-graphic: a bad fall, a fire,
a person who cannot wake. Do **not** describe injuries in detail or scare learners. Emphasize
agency: a child's job is to **get safe and get an adult**, and to know the number — not to fix
the emergency alone. Emergency numbers are **not** the same everywhere: for example 112 (much of
Europe and many phones worldwide), 999 (United Kingdom and several countries), 000 (Australia),
911 (United States and Canada), and others (e.g., 119, 110, 10111) elsewhere. Give learners the
number for **their** place and let families confirm it. Never have learners dial a real
emergency number in practice. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have learned how to keep your body well. Now, keeping your body —
   and other people — **safe**. First question: what is an **emergency**?
2. **Meet an emergency (5 min).** An emergency is a time someone is **badly hurt, very sick, or
   in danger** and needs help **fast** — like a bad fall, a fire, or a person who cannot wake.
   It is rare, but when it happens, a calm helper is worth a lot.
3. **Meet the four steps (10 min).** Look at the poster. *One — **recognize**:* notice this is
   serious. *Two — **get safe**:* move away from danger if you can, without putting yourself at
   risk. *Three — **tell a trusted adult**:* find a grown-up you trust right away. *Four —
   **call the number for help**:* the adult, or you if you must, calls the **emergency number**.
   Say the four steps out loud: recognize, get safe, get help, call the number.
4. **Sort situations (15 min).** With your group, sort the cards. *A scraped knee, a lost toy, a
   dropped book* → **not an emergency** — an adult can help, but there is no rush. *A kitchen
   fire, someone badly hurt, a person who cannot wake* → **emergency — get help now**. Talk
   about why each belongs where it does.
5. **Record your number (5 min).** On your card, write your **local emergency number** and one
   **trusted adult's** name. (Your facilitator will tell you the right number for your place.)
6. **Close (5 min).** You do not have to be big to be a helper. Recognize, get safe, get help,
   call the number — and next time, you will learn exactly **what to say** when you call.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use picture cards and a simple two-corner sort; chant the steps with gestures
  (point, step, hold a hand, mime a phone).
- **Accessibility:** The corner sort can be done seated by holding up or tapping a card (or any
  two signs, e.g., a red/green paper) for "emergency" versus "not." The chant can be done with
  gestures alone — point (recognize), cross your arms or lean back (get safe), hold up a hand or
  point to an adult (get help), mime a phone (call) — so a non-speaking or seated learner joins
  fully.
- **Extension:** Explain why staying calm and keeping the phone line clear helps the helper on
  the other end, and practice a second scenario aloud.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/performance):** Can the learner recognize an emergency, state the four
  steps in order, and say the correct local emergency number and one trusted adult?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Do I know my local emergency number by heart, and can I say
  the four steps in order?"

## Home connection

At home, learn and confirm your local emergency number together, and agree on one trusted adult
to find in an emergency — then practice saying the four steps.

## Resources

- Emergency response taught to children centers on recognizing an emergency, getting to safety,
  getting a trusted adult, and calling for help. Emergency numbers are **country-specific** —
  for example 112 (much of Europe and the EU-wide number), 999 (United Kingdom), 000 (Australia),
  911 (United States and Canada), and others worldwide. Give learners their own local number; do
  not assume one universal number. See the International Telecommunication Union and national
  emergency-service guidance for the number in your country. Keep all practice non-dialing.
