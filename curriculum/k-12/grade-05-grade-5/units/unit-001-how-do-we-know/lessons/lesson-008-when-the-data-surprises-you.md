# Lesson 8 — When the Data Surprises You

## Summary

Two groups tested the same paper and got very different numbers. Learners treat the
disagreement as **information**, not a mistake: they check the measurement, then the
method, then the idea, and write an **anomaly report** that keeps the odd number in the
record until they know why it is there. A real 2011 physics surprise shows the same
habit working at the edge of what people knew.

## Objectives

- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_
- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

A weather app says no rain, and then you feel a drop. You do not throw away the app or
your hand — you check whether it is a leak from the roof, a spray from a passing cart, or
a cloud. Surprises are the most useful moments in an investigation, because they show
you exactly where your understanding is still thin.

## Materials

- The class data board from Lesson 7
- An anomaly report page per pair
- One set of materials for a re-check
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Leave the class data board up, and mark two groups' results for the same material that
  disagree — if the class data are uniform, add a written results slip with one odd
  number.
- Copy the anomaly report: **what surprised us | three checks | what we now think**.
- Keep one set of the absorbency materials available for a group to re-run a trial.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Interested, not alarmed. A surprise is the best thing that can happen in a
science lesson.

**The facts to land.**

1. An **anomaly** is a result that does not fit the pattern. It is information, not a
   failure.
2. Check in order: the **measurement**, then the **method**, then the **idea**. Errors
   in observation and measurement are common, so start there (S-242).
3. Keep the odd number in the record. Erasing it hides the one clue that could improve
   the test.
4. Science is self-correcting because results are shared and checked, not because
   scientists are never wrong (S-489).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not promise that every anomaly gets explained, or that checking
once settles it. Some surprises take years and better instruments.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* the order of checks matters; checking the idea first is how people
  defend a belief instead of testing it.
- *Intellectual:* changing your mind when the evidence asks you to is a strength, not a
  defeat.
- *Ethics:* publishing only the results that fit is a quiet form of dishonesty.
- *Technology:* instruments fail in ordinary ways — loose cables, dirty lenses, flat
  batteries.

**The skill.** Model one anomaly aloud, then pairs work through the three checks, then
write the report (S-011).

**Access.** Offer the three checks as three labelled cards to order physically, and read
the data board aloud.

## Procedure

1. **Find the surprise (5 min).** Look at the class data board. Which number does not fit
   the pattern? Point to it without explaining it. That number is your **anomaly**.
2. **List the possible causes (10 min).** With a partner, list every reason the same
   paper could give 12 mL in one group and 30 mL in another: a bigger square, a longer
   soak, a weaker squeeze, water left in the cup, a misread scale, a spill. Choose the
   cause you think is most likely, and say why.
3. **Check in order (10 min).** Use the three checks. **1 Measure again:** run one more
   trial with the same method. **2 Check the method:** compare the two groups' steps and
   constants side by side. **3 Check the idea:** only now ask whether the surprising
   result changes what you think about the papers. Step 2 is the cheapest check, so do it
   before you touch the idea (S-242).
4. **A real surprise (10 min).** In 2011, a physics team measured particles that seemed
   to travel faster than light. Instead of hiding it, they shared the result and asked
   others to check it. Months later they found a loose fibre-optic connection in their
   own equipment, and the result was withdrawn (S-488). Nobody was shamed; the checking
   worked. That is what peer review and replication are for (S-489).
5. **Write your anomaly report (10 min).** In three boxes: what surprised us; the three
   checks and what each showed; what we now think. Keep the odd number visible. If you
   still cannot explain it, write "not yet — we would check ___ next."
6. **Close (5 min).** A surprise is a question wearing a number. The class that checks it
   calmly learns more than the class that got a tidy result.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work through one supplied anomaly together, with the three checks as
  picture cards; scribe the report.
- **Extension:** Write two different explanations for the same anomaly and name the check
  that would separate them.
- **Language support:** Let the pair record the report as three labelled drawings with
  short captions.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner check the measurement and method before
  questioning the idea?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I keep the odd number, and can I say what I
  would check next?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed anomaly report.

## Home connection

Notice one surprise at home this week — a plant that grew somewhere unexpected, a pot
that boiled faster than usual. Write the surprise and the first check you would make.
