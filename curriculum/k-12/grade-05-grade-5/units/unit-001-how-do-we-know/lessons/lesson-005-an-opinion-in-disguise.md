# Lesson 5 — An Opinion in Disguise

## Summary

"The sweetest mangoes in the city." "Nine out of ten users prefer it." "My uncle has
used this oil for years." Some of these are opinions, some are evidence for a different
question, and some are claims nobody has tested. Learners sort statements into three
bins, learn five common disguises, and rewrite one claim as a question they could
actually investigate.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

A stall sign says "the best mangoes in town"; a phone advert says "twice the battery";
a neighbour says "this tea cures a cold". None is exactly a lie, but none is evidence
either. Learning to hear the difference protects your money, your time, and your health,
whether the claim comes from a screen, a shop, or someone you trust (S-278).

## Materials

- Six claim cards
- A three-bin sorting mat per pair
- A claim-rewrite page per learner
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Write the six claim cards (below), shuffling them so the order gives nothing away.
- Copy the sorting mat: **evidence | opinion | not enough evidence yet**.
- Copy the rewrite page: the claim, a testable question, and "what would count as
  evidence?"
- Have a trusted-adult or health-worker referral ready for any health claim a learner
  raises.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Sharp but friendly — you are teaching a shield, not a suspicion of everyone.

**The facts to land.**

1. **Evidence** is a measurement or observation someone could check. **Opinion** is a
   judgement or taste. **Not enough evidence yet** is a claim with no test behind it.
2. Five disguises: popularity ("everyone"), testimonial ("my cousin"), a number without
   a comparison ("50% more"), vague authority ("experts say"), and feeling ("it feels
   stronger").
3. "Nine out of ten liked it" is evidence about liking, not about working. Match the
   evidence to the exact claim.
4. A question is the way out: "twice as long as what, measured how?"

**Don't overshoot.** Do not teach that adverts always lie or that experts are worthless.
Most claims are partly true; find which part.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* separate the claim from the support offered for it.
- *Ethics:* repeating an untested claim about someone's health or work can cause real
  harm.
- *Technology:* adverts and algorithms repeat claims at scale, so a phrase can feel true
  because you have met it often.
- *Global:* the same disguises appear in every language and market; the questions work
  everywhere.

**The skill.** Sort one claim aloud as a worked example, then pairs sort, then learners
rewrite alone (S-011).

**Access.** Read cards aloud and accept oral sorting; keep sentences short and offer the
five disguises as picture cards.

**Safety floor.** Never test a health claim on yourself. Take it to a trusted adult or a
health worker.

## Procedure

1. **Three bins (5 min).** Look at the mat. **Evidence** can be checked. **Opinion** is a
   judgement or taste. **Not enough evidence yet** is a claim with no test behind it.
   Some statements will sit awkwardly between bins — say why.
2. **Meet the six claims (10 min).** Read each card:
   a) "Our soap is the best soap in the market."
   b) "In our test, soap A removed 8 of 10 marker marks; soap B removed 3."
   c) "Nine out of ten people who tried the tea said they liked it."
   d) "My uncle has used this oil for years and never gets sick."
   e) "This bag lasts twice as long as ordinary bags."
   f) "The tank water was 18 °C at 8 a.m. and 24 °C at 2 p.m."
3. **Sort and argue (10 min).** With a partner, place all six. For each one, say the
   sentence that justifies the bin: "This is evidence because ___" or "This is not
   enough because ___". Expect disagreement on (c) — that is the most useful card.
4. **Name the disguises (10 min).** Five tricks make an opinion sound like evidence:
   popularity, testimonial, a number without a comparison, vague authority, and feeling.
   Find one card for each. Then ask of card (c): evidence of what, exactly? Liking is not
   the same as working.
5. **Rewrite one claim (10 min).** Choose the claim that annoys you most. Write it at the
   top of your page, then rewrite it as a testable question — "Does bag A last twice as
   long as bag B when both carry the same weight?" — and name what would count as
   evidence. Use the key questions for analysing a message: who made this, and what is
   being left out (S-151).
6. **Close (5 min).** An opinion in disguise is not a villain. It is a claim waiting for
   a question. Next lesson you build the test that can answer one.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only four cards, with the five disguises displayed as labelled
  pictures; provide the rewrite frame "Does ___ change ___?"
- **Extension:** Find a claim where the evidence is real but does not support the
  sentence — then explain the gap in two sentences.
- **Language support:** Allow sorting in the home language first, then name the bin in
  the shared language.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place at least four claims correctly and
  give a reason that names the kind of support offered?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their rewritten question and asks, "Would the
  answer settle the original claim, or a different one?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The claim-rewrite page.

Find one claim on a packet, a poster, or a screen at home. Bring it back with the
question you would need to ask before believing it.
