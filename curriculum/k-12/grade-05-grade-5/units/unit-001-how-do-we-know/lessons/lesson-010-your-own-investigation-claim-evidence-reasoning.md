# Lesson 10 — Your Own Investigation: Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

## Summary

This is the unit's **capstone**. Learners choose a question they can test with the
materials in the room, run a **fair test** with three trials, and write a
**claim–evidence–reasoning** paragraph: one sentence that answers the question, the
numbers that support it, and the reasoning that connects them. They then defend the
investigation in an **evidence circle**, where peers ask the five claim questions from
Lesson 9.

## Objectives

- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_
- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

Every investigation in this unit has been practice for this one: a question you chose, a
plan you can defend, numbers you wrote down, and a sentence that says what they mean. A
market seller comparing two suppliers, a farmer testing a new seed, a nurse checking
whether a dressing stays dry — anyone who can say "here is what I found, here is how I
know, and here is why it matters" can be trusted with a real problem.

## Materials

- An investigation menu card per group
- An investigation page per learner
- Materials for the menu questions
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Choose four menu questions that your materials can actually support:
  which cloth dries fastest in sun or shade; which cup keeps water warmest; which paper
  plane stays in the air longest; which wet paper towel holds the most coins before it
  tears; or a learner's own question.
- Copy the investigation page with the variable rows, a three-trial table, and a CER box.
- Arrange the room so each group has a work surface and the evidence circle has space.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Celebratory but rigorous — this is a defence, not a show-and-tell.

**The facts to land.**

1. A **claim** answers your question in one sentence, and it says how sure you are.
2. **Evidence** is numbers with units, from three trials, with the spread visible.
3. **Reasoning** explains why *this* evidence supports *this* claim, and names one rival
   explanation you ruled out.
4. The chain is only as strong as its weakest part: a fair test with a vague claim, or a
   strong claim with one trial, both fail.

**Don't overshoot.** Not every investigation ends with a clean answer. "Not yet — here
is what I would check next" is a real result and should be marked as one.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* the CER is a structure for being checkable.
- *Intellectual:* a defended claim is one you have already tried to break yourself.
- *Ethics:* report the trials that did not fit; the audience is trusting your record.
- *Community:* an evidence circle makes the group smarter than any one investigator.

**The skill.** Model one short CER on the board, then guide the first sentence of each
part before learners write alone (S-011). Open with a no-notes recall of the unit's five
moves — retrieval and spacing are what make them stick (S-039).

**Access.** Provide sentence stems for each block, and let learners present orally or
with a labelled diagram instead of a written paragraph.

**Safety floor.** An adult pours any hot water; no open flames; wipe spills immediately.

## Procedure

1. **Recall without notes (5 min).** In two minutes, write the five moves this unit
   taught: a testable question, evidence, observation versus inference, opinion in
   disguise, and a fair test with repeated trials. Then check with a partner and fill
   any gap. Recalling is what makes it stay (S-039).
2. **Choose and plan (10 min).** Pick a question from the menu, or use the question you
   sharpened in Lesson 2 if the materials allow it. Fill the plan: what changes, what you
   measure, at least three things you keep the same. A fair test changes one thing
   (S-070).
3. **Run three trials (20 min).** Run the test three times, recording every result with
   its unit (S-071). Keep the odd numbers. If something goes wrong, write what happened
   next to the number instead of erasing it.
4. **Write the CER (15 min).** **Claim:** one sentence that answers your question, and
   how sure you are. **Evidence:** two or three numbers with units, and the spread across
   trials. **Reasoning:** why these numbers support this claim, and what else could explain
   them. Use the graphic as your frame.
5. **Evidence circle (5 min).** In groups of four, each person presents for one minute.
   Listeners ask one of the five claim questions: who says it, what exactly is claimed,
   what evidence is shown, was the test fair and repeated, what is left out (S-151). Give
   one useful question, not a score.
6. **Close (5 min).** How do we know? Because we asked something we could test, wrote
   down what we saw, repeated it, and showed our reasoning where others could check it.
   That is the answer the whole unit has been building — and it is a habit, not a fact to
   memorise.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a pre-filled CER frame with sentence stems and a two-trial table; allow
  the evidence circle to be a pair share.
- **Extension:** Add a paragraph on a rival explanation and the check that would rule it
  out, or design a second investigation that would make the claim stronger.
- **Language support:** Let learners write the CER in the home language and present it
  with a labelled diagram; the structure, not the language, is the assessment.

## Assessment

- **Summative (performance):** The investigation page and CER paragraph, judged on
  whether the claim answers the question, the evidence is fair and repeated, and the
  reasoning connects them.
- **Peer:** In the evidence circle, listeners ask one question that could strengthen or
  weaken the claim.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Which part of my chain is weakest, and what would I
  fix first?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The investigation page and CER, the unit's final portfolio
  piece.

## Home connection

Explain your investigation to someone at home in three sentences: what you asked, what
you found, and how you know. Ask them one question they would still want answered.
