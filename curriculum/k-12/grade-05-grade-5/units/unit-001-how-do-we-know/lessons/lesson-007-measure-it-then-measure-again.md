# Lesson 7 — Measure It, Then Measure Again

## Summary

Today the plan becomes numbers. Learners run the absorbency test they designed,
**repeating** each measurement three times, writing the **unit** every time, and
calculating a **mean**. Then they look at how far apart the three trials fall — the
**spread** — because a result is more trustworthy when you can say how steady it is.

## Objectives

- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

A tailor measures a sleeve twice before cutting; a shopkeeper weighs a bag of rice, then
checks the scale with a known weight; a cook tastes the soup, stirs, and tastes again.
One measurement can be a slip of the hand. Three measurements tell you how steady the
result is, and that steadiness is what makes a number worth trusting (S-105).

## Materials

- The materials and plan from Lesson 6
- A trials table per pair
- A class data board
- Pencils and a calculator if available

## Preparation

- Repost the class plans from Lesson 6, and choose which two materials each pair will
  test so every material is covered.
- Draw the trials table on the board: one row per material, columns for three trials and
  the mean.
- Check the measuring cup is readable at eye level, and that every group uses the same
  size of cup.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Calm and exact. Slow the room down; speed here produces numbers nobody trusts.

**The facts to land.**

1. A **trial** is one run of the test. One trial is a start; three show the spread.
2. Write the **unit** every time — 18 mL, not 18.
3. The **mean** is the three trials added and divided by three. It summarizes them; it
   does not make a wild trial disappear.
4. Every measurement carries uncertainty, so report the spread, not only the average
   (S-249).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not call the mean "the true answer". Three close trials are strong
evidence; three wild ones are a signal to check the method.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* a number without a spread hides how sure you can be.
- *Intellectual:* the mean is a tool, and knowing what it does is part of using it.
- *Technology:* a scale or spreadsheet is faster, but it does not decide what was
  measured.
- *Ethics:* report trials that did not fit; quietly dropping them is cheating, even when
  nobody notices.

**The skill.** Model one full trial aloud — soak, drip, squeeze, read at eye level,
record with the unit — then guide one trial before pairs run alone (S-011).

**Access.** Pre-print the table with units; a partner reads the cup aloud for a learner
with low vision, and a counted unit is fine if no marked cup exists.

**Safety floor.** Keep water off the floor, wipe spills at once, and use cold water only.

## Procedure

1. **Set up, say the plan (5 min).** Read your method from last lesson aloud to your
   partner. Check the four materials are the same size and that you have the same cup
   and the same soak time. If something does not match the plan, fix it before you start.
2. **Watch one full trial (10 min).** Watch the model: soak the square for 30 seconds,
   let it drip for 10 seconds, squeeze it into the measuring cup, and read the level at
   eye level. Record "18 mL" — number and unit (S-113). Notice that your number is not every drop
   in the paper; it is the same measure every time, which is what makes it comparable.
3. **Run three trials (20 min).** For each of your two materials, run three trials.
   Between trials, use a fresh dry square and the same cup, rinsed. Record every number
   in the table, even the surprising ones. Write the unit every time (S-071).
4. **Find the mean and the spread (10 min).** Add the three trials and divide by three.
   Then look at the three numbers: how far apart are they? Say it in words — "within 2
   mL of each other" or "one trial was much higher". Post your row on the class board.
   Comparing results across repeated trials is how a result becomes reliable (S-105).
5. **Close (5 min).** A good result is not one big number. It is a number, a unit, three
   trials, and an honest spread. Next lesson, the class data will surprise you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two trials only, with the mean described as "about the middle", and
  provide the table with units already written.
- **Extension:** Calculate the mean for all four materials from the class board and rank
  them; then say which ranking you trust least, and why.
- **Language support:** Provide a labelled diagram of the four method steps to point to
  while explaining the trial.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner record three trials with units and
  calculate a mean?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If I showed this table to another group, could they
  see how steady my result is?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed trials table with mean and a one-sentence
  statement of the spread.

## Home connection

Measure something three times with a grown-up — the width of a table, the time to walk
to the gate, the water in a bottle. Write the three numbers with their units and say how
close they are.
