# Lesson 4 — I Saw It, I Reasoned It

## Summary

You come home and the floor is wet, a pot is on its side, and there are footprints
across the puddle. "The pot fell" feels like something you saw — but you did not see it
fall. Learners practise separating **observation** from **inference**, attach each
inference to the observation that supports it, and name the check that would settle the
difference.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

You come home and the floor is wet, a pot is on its side, and there are footprints
across the puddle. "The pot fell" feels like something you saw — but you did not see it
fall. Reading a friend's face, listening to an engine, or looking at a rash works the
same way: you observe something and reason from it, and the skill is knowing which part
of your sentence is which.

## Materials

- The courtyard scene (a tray arrangement or the lesson graphic)
- A two-column page per learner
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Set up the scene on a tray: a small pot on its side, a shallow dish of water spilled
  beside it, paper paw prints crossing the wet patch, and a cat (or a picture of one)
  sitting nearby.
- Copy the two-column page: **I observed | I inferred**, with a line at the bottom for
  **what I would check**.
- Keep the scene covered until the lesson starts, so the first look is a real look.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Curious, like a quiet detective — not suspicious of anyone.

**The facts to land.**

1. An **observation** is what your senses or instruments pick up: "the ground is wet",
   "the pot is on its side".
2. An **inference** is a reasoned claim about what you did not see: "the pot tipped
   over", "someone walked through it".
3. Inferences are necessary, not shameful. Good science is full of them — the skill is
   labelling them and checking them.
4. The same observation can support more than one inference, which is exactly why we
   look for another check — and what we expect can shape what we notice (S-250).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not turn this into "never trust your thinking". The point is
precision about where a sentence comes from, not distrust of reasoning.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* labelling the source of a statement is the first move in checking
  it.
- *Intellectual:* inference uses prior knowledge, so two people can reasonably infer
  differently.
- *Global:* the same trace — a footprint, a smell, a sound — can mean different things
  in different places, which is why local knowledge matters.
- *Ethics:* an inference about a person's actions can harm them if it is treated as
  fact, so we say "I think" until we have checked.

**The skill.** Model two sentences aloud, one observation and one inference, then let
pairs sort before learners write alone (S-011).

**Access.** Describe the scene aloud for learners who cannot see it, and accept oral
answers recorded by a partner.

## Procedure

1. **Two sentences (5 min).** "The ground is wet." "Someone spilled the water." Which
   one could a camera have caught? Which one did you work out? Today you will sort a
   whole scene that way.
2. **Look first (10 min).** Uncover the scene. For two full minutes, write only what you
   can point to: colours, shapes, positions, wet or dry, how many prints, where the cat
   is sitting. No "because" yet. Being exact here is what makes the next step
   possible (S-242).
3. **Sort the statements (10 min).** Now write what you think happened. Put each
   sentence in one column. Then draw a line from every inference to the observation that
   supports it. If an inference has no line, it is a guess — keep it, but mark it.
4. **Two inferences, one observation (10 min).** In pairs, find one observation that
   could support two different inferences. For example, wet ground could mean a spilled
   pot or rain through an open window. What would you check to choose between them?
   Write it on the bottom line: "I would check ___."
5. **The honest sentence (10 min).** Rewrite three of your inferences in the form
   "I observed ___, so I infer ___, and I would check ___." Read one aloud. Notice that
   the sentence is longer, and much easier for someone else to check.
6. **Close (5 min).** Reasoning is not the enemy of evidence; it is what evidence is for.
   But an inference wearing the clothes of an observation can fool a whole room. Keep
   the two labels close.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a bank of six statements to sort rather than asking for free
  writing, and scribe the "I would check" line.
- **Extension:** Write a paragraph about the scene using only observations, then a second
  paragraph using inferences, and compare how each one sounds to a reader who was not
  there.
- **Language support:** Let learners sort picture cards into "saw" and "reasoned" before
  writing any sentences.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner correctly label at least three statements
  and give a reason for each label?
- **Peer check:** A partner reads one inference and asks, "What did you actually see
  that made you think that?"
- **Self-check:** The learner finds one sentence in their own writing that mixes the two
  and rewrites it as two sentences.

## Home connection

At home, find one small trace — a crumb trail, a wet towel, a moved chair — and write
one observation and two possible inferences. Ask someone else what they would check.
