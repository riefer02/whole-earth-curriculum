# Lesson 6 — Change One Thing: Designing a Fair Test

## Summary

You want to know which paper soaks up a spill best. If one sheet is bigger, or stays in
the water longer, you have not learned which paper is better — you have learned which
sheet got more help. Learners critique two flawed plans, name the **variables** in a
fair test, and write a method for the absorbency test they will run next lesson.

## Objectives

- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

A cook comparing two kinds of rice, a farmer comparing two seed varieties, and a clinic
comparing two ways of dressing a wound all face the same problem: if two things differ
at once, you cannot tell which one made the difference. So they change one thing on
purpose and keep everything else the same (S-070). That is a **fair test**, and it works
the same way whether you are testing paper in a classroom or a new pump in a village.

## Materials

- Four kinds of paper, cut to the same size
- Water, a bowl, and a measuring cup
- Ruler and scissors
- An investigation plan page per learner
- A timer, or a counted 30 seconds

## Preparation

- Cut the four materials to identical squares (10 cm × 10 cm) before the lesson, and keep
  the offcuts visible so learners can see they match.
- Set out water in a bowl deep enough to soak a square, and a measuring cup that reads
  millilitres.
- Copy the plan page with the four variable rows and space for numbered steps.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Engineer-at-the-bench: practical, unhurried, interested in the flaws.

**The facts to land.**

1. A **fair test** changes one thing and keeps everything else the same (S-070).
2. The three parts have names: the thing you **change**, the thing you **measure**, and
   the things you **keep the same**.
3. If two things change, the result cannot tell you which one mattered.
4. A method is fair only if someone else could follow it without asking you a question.

**Don't overshoot.** Do not say a fair test proves a cause beyond all doubt. It gives
good evidence for one explanation and rules out some others.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* naming the variables is what turns an activity into an
  investigation.
- *Intellectual:* a plan is a place to make your mistakes cheaply, before the water
  spills.
- *Egalitarianism:* a method anyone can repeat lets anyone check the result.
- *Environment:* the paper and water you use are real resources, so plan the smallest
  test that answers the question.

**The skill.** Model the variable table once with a different question, then let pairs
build theirs and check each other's plan (S-011).

**Access.** Provide the plan as a table with sentence stems, and let learners dictate
the method while a partner writes.

## Procedure

1. **The question (5 min).** "Which paper absorbs the most water?" Say what you would
   measure. Say what would count as "the most". Keep both answers; you will need them.
2. **Two plans, one flaw each (10 min).** Read these.
   *Plan A:* cut newspaper big and tissue small; soak the newspaper for a minute and the
   tissue for ten seconds; squeeze both.
   *Plan B:* cut all four materials to the same square; soak each for 30 seconds; let
   each drip for 10 seconds; squeeze each into the same cup.
   What exactly is unfair in Plan A? Name the two things that changed besides the paper.
3. **Name the variables (10 min).** Watch one worked example on the board, then fill your
   own table. **Change:** the kind of paper — one thing only. **Measure:** water absorbed,
   in millilitres (or grams on a scale). **Keep the same:** the size of each square, the
   amount of water, the soak time, the drip time, how you squeeze, and the cup you
   squeeze into. The more you hold constant, the more the result can be trusted (S-070).
4. **Write the method (15 min).** Number the steps so a group in another room could
   follow them without asking you anything. Include how you will know when a step is
   finished. A good method is boring on purpose.
5. **Peer check (5 min).** Swap plans. Your partner asks three questions: what changed?
   what did you measure? name three things you kept the same. If any answer is missing,
   fix it now — this plan is what you will run next lesson.
6. **Close (5 min).** A fair test is a promise: one change, one measurement, everything
   else held still. Next lesson you keep that promise with numbers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a half-completed plan with the change and measure already named, and
  ask the learner to add three constants.
- **Extension:** Design a second test where the *size* of the paper changes on purpose,
  and explain what different question that answers.
- **Language support:** Allow the method as a labelled diagram with arrows instead of
  numbered sentences.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Can the learner name one change, one measurement, and at least
  three constants in a partner's plan?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their method and asks, "Could someone else run this
  without me in the room?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed investigation plan, used in Lesson 7.
