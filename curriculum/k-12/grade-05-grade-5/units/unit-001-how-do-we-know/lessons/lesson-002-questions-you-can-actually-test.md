# Lesson 2 — Questions You Can Actually Test

## Summary

A question starts every investigation, but not every question can be tested with what
you have. Learners take six real wonders — a drying puddle, a leaning seedling, a
noisy tap — and sharpen them into **testable questions** that name one thing to change,
one thing to measure or observe, and the things to keep the same. Each learner leaves
with a question of their own that they could actually investigate.

## Objectives

- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

After rain, a puddle on the path is gone by afternoon. On a washing line, a shirt dries
faster than the same shirt left in a heap. Both begin as a wonder, and the move that
turns a wonder into an investigation is to ask what you would change, what you would
measure, and what you would keep the same — asking questions is where working
scientifically begins (S-041).

## Materials

- Six question cards
- A question-sharpener page per learner
- Chart paper and a marker
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Write six wonders on cards, using places learners know: a puddle, a seedling, a tap,
  a bicycle tyre, a bowl of rice left out, a wet cloth in shade.
- Draw the three-rung ladder large enough for the whole group to see.
- Check which materials you actually have, so the class knows what "testable here"
  means today.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Practical and a little playful — a question is a tool you can sharpen.

**The facts to land.**

1. A **testable question** names one thing you could change, one thing you could
   measure or observe, and what you would keep the same.
2. It has more than one possible answer, and you do not know which is right yet.
3. Sharpening does not throw away the wonder. "Why do seedlings lean to the window?"
   becomes "Do seedlings lean towards light in five days?" The wonder is still inside.
4. Some good questions cannot be tested here and now. They stay on the wonder wall.

**Don't overshoot.** Do not imply that only testable questions are good questions —
science also builds models and asks things no classroom can test.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* a question you cannot check is not yet an investigation.
- *Intellectual:* the sharpening is the thinking; the answer comes later.
- *Global:* questions come from kitchens, farms, clinics, and bus stops.
- *Egalitarianism:* every learner's wonder is worth writing down, not only the ones
  that already sound scientific.

**The skill.** Model one sharpening yourself, then guide practice in pairs before
independent work (S-011).

**Access.** Offer the frame "Does ___ change ___?" and let learners draw the test
instead of writing it. Pair learners who share no spoken language with picture cards.

## Procedure

1. **Remember where questions come from (5 min).** Last lesson you tested an idea you
   already believed: heavy things sink. Where did that question come from? Today you
   start one step earlier — with something you have noticed and cannot yet explain.
2. **Meet the ladder (10 min).** Watch one wonder get sharpened. *Wonder:* "Why do the
   seedlings on the windowsill lean?" Rung 1 — what could I change? Which side the
   light comes from. Rung 2 — what could I measure? How far each stem leans, in
   centimetres, after five days. Rung 3 — what would I keep the same? The same kind of
   seed, soil, water, and warmth. Now say it as a question: "Do bean seedlings lean
   towards the light within five days?" Yes or no — two possible answers. That is a
   testable question.
3. **Sharpen in pairs (15 min).** Take two question cards. For each one, fill the three
   rungs, then write the sharpened question on the line. Keep the original wonder
   above it so you can see what you kept.
4. **Partner check (10 min).** Swap pages. Check three marks: could we do this with
   what we have here? can we measure or observe something? is there more than one
   possible answer? If a mark is missing, say which rung needs work — not "wrong", just
   "not yet".
5. **Choose your own (5 min).** Write one question you genuinely wonder about. Fill its
   three rungs. This card goes in your portfolio and may become your investigation in
   Lesson 10.
6. **Close (5 min).** A testable question is a wonder with handles. You have not lost
   the wonder — you have found a way to hold it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give the sentence frame "Does changing ___ change ___?" and let the
  learner draw the two setups instead of describing them in words.
- **Extension:** Turn one "why" question into two different testable questions, then say
  which one would give a clearer answer and which would be easier to run here.
- **Language support:** Provide the three rungs as picture prompts — two objects that
  differ, a ruler, a padlock.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner's question name one change, one
  measurement or observation, and at least one thing kept the same?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their question and asks, "Could someone else run
  this and get an answer that is not just my opinion?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed question card with its three rungs.

Keep a wonder list for one week — things you notice and cannot explain. Bring one back
sharpened into a question with something to change and something to measure.
