# Lesson 3 — What Counts as Evidence?

## Summary

Once you have a testable question, the next question is harder: what would count as an
answer? Learners discover that **evidence** is an observation that is exact enough to
compare, **recorded** at the time, and **checkable** by someone else. They test their own
descriptions against those three checks, meet a real case where careful records changed
what people believed, and start an evidence log about a place they know.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

A nurse writes a temperature on a chart at eight in the morning; a farmer scratches the
rainfall on a doorframe; a bus conductor notes the minute the seven o'clock leaves. None
of them trusts memory alone, and none writes "a lot" or "very hot". When someone says
"this medicine worked", the useful question is what was written down, by whom, and
whether you could go and check it.

## Materials

- Leaves, stones, or seed pods (one per pair)
- An evidence log page per learner
- A ruler and a small measuring cup per group
- Pencils and something hard to write on

## Preparation

- Collect pairs of similar-looking leaves or stones, one per pair.
- Copy the evidence log page with three rows headed **time | place | what I noticed**.
- Choose a place in the school or neighbourhood with something to observe: a drain, a
  step that stays wet, a bench everyone uses, a tree with two sides.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Steady and concrete. This is a lesson about writing things down properly, not
about doubting each other.

**The facts to land.**

1. **Evidence** is an observation you can point to: exact, recorded, and checkable by
   someone else.
2. "It grew a lot" cannot be checked. "14 cm on Monday, 19 cm on Friday, same ruler"
   can.
3. A record made at the time is stronger than a memory made afterwards.
4. One careful record is a start, not a proof — evidence grows by adding records and by
   other people checking them (S-242).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not tell learners that memory is worthless or that only numbers
count. A clear drawing with a date and a place is evidence too.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* the three checks are a filter you can run on any claim.
- *Intellectual:* precision is a habit, and it is learned by doing it badly first.
- *Global:* tallies and knotted cords are old and widespread ways of keeping a record,
  including across African counting traditions (S-024).
- *Community:* a shared record lets a whole group know something one person saw.

**The skill.** Model one description and one record yourself, then guide a pair before
learners record alone (S-011).

**Access.** Accept a drawing, a photo, or a dictated note as a record; the checks are
about exactness and traceability, not handwriting.

## Procedure

1. **From question to answer (5 min).** Last lesson you sharpened a question. Suppose
   the question is "Which side of the wall stays wet longer?" What would you accept as
   an answer — a feeling, a guess, or something someone else could check?
2. **The description game (10 min).** Sit back to back with a partner. One of you holds
   a leaf or stone and describes it without naming it; the other draws. Compare the
   drawing with the object. What made a description useful? "It's green and big" almost
   never is. "It is 12 cm long, with a torn tip and five veins" usually is.
3. **Meet the three checks (10 min).** Evidence has to be **exact** enough to compare,
   **recorded** at the time, and **checkable** by someone else. Test these two
   statements. A: "The bean plant grew a lot this week." B: "The bean stem was 14 cm on
   Monday and 19 cm on Friday, measured with the same ruler." Which one passes all three
   checks, and which check does the other one fail? A record made at the time, with
   units, is what makes an observation usable (S-071).
4. **A real case (10 min).** In London in 1854, John Snow did not argue about bad air. He
   marked where people had died on a map and compared that with where they got their
   water. The map was a record others could check, and it pointed to a single pump
   (S-358). What made his map evidence, rather than just a strong opinion?
5. **Start your evidence log (10 min).** Go to your chosen place. Write three
   observations that pass the three checks. Each row needs a time, a place, and
   something exact — a number with a unit, a count, or a detail someone could find
   again. Do not explain yet; just record.
6. **Close (5 min).** Evidence is not what you remember. It is what you can show. Next
   lesson you will use records like these to separate what you saw from what you worked
   out.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a filled example row and a word bank of measurable features
  (length, width, number, colour, temperature, time).
- **Extension:** Record the same thing twice, an hour apart, and say which record would
  be more useful to a person who was not there — and why.
- **Language support:** Allow labels in the learner's home language alongside the shared
  language, and accept a labelled drawing as a full record.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner's record include a time, a place, and at
  least one exact detail?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks of each row, "Could a stranger use this to find and
  check the same thing?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The three-row evidence log, kept for the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Keep a three-line record of something at home for three days — the water level in a
bottle, the time the sun leaves the yard, the number of people at the tap. Bring it back
and check it against the three checks.
