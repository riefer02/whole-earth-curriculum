# Lesson 9 — Checking a Claim Someone Else Made

## Summary

A company says its towel "absorbs twice as much as ordinary paper" and that it was
"tested in our own laboratory". Learners apply five questions to the claim, compare it
with the class's own absorbency data, and reach a **verdict** — supported, not supported,
or not enough evidence yet — with a reason and one question they would send back to the
company.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between evidence, inference, and opinion using a simple
  example. _(D06.S1.05.02)_
- Form a testable question, design an investigation, and identify what stays the same
  and what changes. _(D06.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

A shop advertises "twice as absorbent"; a video says "the cleanest water in the region";
a neighbour says "that is the best school". You do not have to become a scientist to
check a claim — you need five questions, asked in order, and the patience to notice what
the claim does not say. The same questions work on a packet, a poster, a message from a
relative, or a number read out on the radio.

## Materials

- The manufacturer's claim card
- A five-question check sheet per pair
- The class data board
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Write the claim card exactly as given, including "tested in our own laboratory" — that
  phrase is part of what learners must examine.
- Copy the check sheet with the five questions and a verdict box.
- Keep the class data board up; learners will compare the claim with their own numbers.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Businesslike and fair. You are auditing a claim, not attacking a company.

**The facts to land.**

1. A **claim** is a statement someone wants you to accept. A **verdict** is your
   judgement about it, and it needs a reason.
2. Five questions: who says it, what exactly is claimed, what evidence is shown, was the
   test fair and repeated, what is left out (S-151).
3. "Twice as much" is meaningless until you know: twice as much as what, measured how, in
   whose test?
4. "Tested in our own laboratory" is not the same as "checked by someone independent".
   Independent checking is one of the safeguards of science (S-489).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not teach that a company's own test is worthless, or that all
advertising is false. A company test can be a start; it is stronger when others can
repeat it.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical thinking:* the five questions turn a reaction into a judgement.
- *Ethics:* a verdict should be as fair to the seller as to the buyer.
- *Global:* claims cross borders faster than evidence, so the questions travel with the
  product.
- *Technology:* a claim repeated by many accounts can look independently confirmed when
  it is only copied.

**The skill.** Work one question aloud with the class, then pairs complete the sheet
before writing the verdict alone (S-011).

**Access.** Provide the five questions as numbered cards and accept a spoken verdict
recorded by a partner.

## Procedure

1. **Remember the disguises (5 min).** Name the five disguises from Lesson 5 without
   looking: popularity, testimonial, a number without a comparison, vague authority, and
   feeling. Which one is hiding in "twice as much"?
2. **Read the claim closely (10 min).** The card says: *"AquaTowel absorbs twice as much
   as ordinary paper. Tested in our own laboratory."* Underline every word that would
   need a definition before you could test it. What is "ordinary paper"? Twice as much of
   what — water, oil, spilled tea?
3. **Ask the five questions (15 min).** With a partner, answer in order: **Who says it?**
   (the company). **What exactly is claimed?** (twice the absorbency, on some measure).
   **What evidence is shown?** (a statement that a test happened, no numbers). **Was the
   test fair and repeated?** (unknown — we cannot see the method or the trials; a fair
   test changes one thing, and repeats show whether a result is steady — S-070, S-105).
   **What is left out?** (the comparison material, the amount of water, the number of
   trials, who checked it).
4. **Check it against your own data (10 min).** Look at the class board. Our best paper
   absorbed about ___ mL and our weakest about ___ mL. Is that "twice"? What would the
   company need to show for its claim to match what we found? Remember that a test is
   stronger when someone independent can repeat it (S-489). History offers a warning
   too: Semmelweis's handwashing evidence sharply reduced deaths but was dismissed by the
   authorities of his time before it was accepted (S-357).
5. **Give your verdict (5 min).** Write **supported**, **not supported**, or **not
   enough evidence yet**, then one sentence of reason and one question you would send to
   the company. "Not enough evidence yet" is a legitimate verdict — often the most honest
   one.
6. **Close (5 min).** A claim is not a fact because it is printed, repeated, or confident.
   It becomes trustworthy when the evidence is shown, the test is fair, someone else can
   repeat it, and nothing important is missing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Answer only the first three questions, with the claim card and the data
  board side by side; provide verdict sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Write the method section the company would need to publish for an
  independent group to repeat its test.
- **Language support:** Allow the check sheet to be completed as five labelled drawings
  or in the home language, then summarised orally.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance):** Does the learner's verdict follow from the evidence they
  identified, rather than from whether they like the product?
- **Peer check:** A partner asks, "Which of the five questions decided your verdict?"
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed check sheet with verdict, reason, and question.

Choose one claim from a packet or poster at home and ask the five questions with someone
