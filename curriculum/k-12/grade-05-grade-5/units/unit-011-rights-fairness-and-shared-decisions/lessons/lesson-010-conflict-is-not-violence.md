# Lesson 10 — Conflict Is Not Violence

## Summary

In this **capstone** lesson, learners explain the **difference between conflict
(disagreement) and violence**, and **why disagreement can be healthy when handled
well**. They analyze a fork diagram — a disagreement can go two ways, respect or
force — sort examples, and revisit the unit's four strands in one reflection note:
values (S1), rights and fairness (S2), government and collective action (S3), and peace
(S4).

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between conflict (disagreement) and violence, and why
  disagreements can be healthy when handled well. _(D09.S4.05.02)_
- Revisit the unit's ideas: values shape choices (D09.S1.05.01), rights and fairness
  (D09.S2.05.01), and government and collective action (D09.S3.05.01).

## Connection

Two people disagree about a game rule — that is a **conflict**, and it is normal and
ordinary. One of them shoves or hurts the other to win — that is **violence**, and it
harms. The difference is not in the disagreement itself but in **how it is handled**.
Handled with respect, a disagreement can even be *good*: it surfaces real needs and
leads to better, fairer solutions. Handled with force, it breaks trust and hurts people.

## Materials

- Conflict-vs-violence fork poster
- Disagreement example cards
- Unit reflection page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the conflict-vs-violence fork poster.
- Copy example cards for each pair and a unit reflection page for each learner.
- Prepare one spoken disagreement for the whole-group opening.

## Facilitator note

This is the unit **capstone**, written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: (1)
**conflict (disagreement) is normal and can be healthy; violence (hurting a person's
body, dignity, or belongings to get one's way) is a choice that harms**; and (2) the
difference is *how we handle it*. Teach this **non-violently and non-shamingly** — no
child is labeled "violent"; the lesson names *behaviors and their consequences*, never
people. Distinguish **evidence from values** (`docs/philosophy.md` §5): *what conflict
and violence are, and what each path tends to lead to* are observable; *that we should
handle disagreement with respect and without violence* is a value this curriculum holds.
Close with retrieval across all four strands (a spaced, interleaved review — Dunlosky et
al., 2013). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** For nine lessons you have thought about values, choices, rights,
   fairness, government, collective action, and solving conflicts. Today you put it
   together with one clear line: **conflict is not violence.**
2. **Meet the difference (10 min).** A **conflict** is a disagreement — two people want
   different things. It is normal, it happens everywhere, and it is not a failure.
   **Violence** is different: it is *hurting someone's body, dignity, or belongings to
   get your way*. A disagreement can turn into violence, or it can stay peaceful — the
   difference is the *choice of how to handle it*.
3. **The fork (15 min).** Look at the poster. A disagreement sits at the top. It can go
   two ways. **Handled with respect** — each side speaks, each listens, and they find a
   fair solution — the disagreement becomes **healthy**: both are heard, and the bond
   can even grow. **Handled with force** — hurt or threaten to win — it becomes
   **violence**: someone is harmed, and trust breaks. Sort your example cards onto one
   path or the other, and say what each path leads to.
4. **Why disagreement can be healthy (10 min).** Disagreement is not the enemy. When we
   disagree and *listen*, we learn things we had not seen; we find solutions that work
   for more people. A group where no one ever disagrees can quietly miss what is unfair.
   Disagreement, handled well, makes decisions *better*.
5. **Capstone reflection (5 min).** On your page, write the conflict-vs-violence
   difference in your own words. Then, one line for each strand of the unit: a **value**
   that shapes choices; a **right** that belongs to every child; one thing **government
   and collective action** do for the common good; and the **fair-solution** step that
   keeps conflict peaceful.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the fork with the outcomes filled in and sentence starters for the
  four reflection lines.
- **Extension:** Take a real disagreement you have seen and write how each path (respect
  vs. force) would likely end, in one short paragraph.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The unit reflection page — can the learner state the
  conflict-vs-violence difference and recall one idea from each of the four strands? The
  page is kept as a unit artifact.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I tell conflict from violence? Can I name one
  way disagreement helps, and one way to handle it without hurting anyone?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home the difference between conflict and violence, and one way to
disagree without hurting anyone — then ask how they handle disagreements well.
