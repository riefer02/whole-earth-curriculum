# Lesson 7 — How Decisions Get Made: Comparing Countries

## Summary

Learners compare **how decisions are made in more than one country**. They meet three
real ways — **talking together until people agree (consensus)**, **direct votes**, and
**choosing representatives** — each with examples from different countries, and record
how two places are alike and different. The point is global comparison, not ranking one
way as "the best."

## Objectives

- Compare how decisions are made in more than one country. _(D09.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

Every family and class has a way to decide: one person decides, or everyone talks it
out, or people raise hands. Whole countries have ways too, and they are not all the
same. In one place, people gather and talk until they agree; in another, citizens vote
directly on a law; in another, they choose representatives to decide for them. Seeing
the different ways — and who gets a voice in each — is how we understand our own.

## Materials

- Three-ways comparison chart
- Decision-making example cards
- Compare page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-ways comparison chart (consensus / direct votes /
  representatives, with country examples).
- Copy example cards for each group and a compare page for each learner.
- Prepare one short spoken description per way if going low-tech.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is **comparing how
decisions are made in more than one country**, with three real, observable ways:
**consensus** (talking until people agree — for example, the *kgotla*, a community
assembly in Botswana where people gather and speak until a decision is reached);
**direct votes** (for example, Switzerland, where citizens vote on many laws several
times a year in referendums); and **choosing representatives** (as in many countries,
including India, Japan, Brazil, and others, where adults vote for representatives who
then decide in a parliament or congress). Present these **descriptively and
non-hierarchically** — no single model is "the" right one (the unit's commitment).
Distinguish **evidence from values**: *how decisions are made in each place* is
observable fact; *whether everyone should have a voice* is a value this curriculum
holds. Hold questions open. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned what government is. Now: *how do the
   decisions actually get made?* Try to recall one thing government does. (Pause.) Today
   you compare the ways different countries decide.
2. **Meet three ways (15 min).** Look at the chart. **Talking together (consensus):**
   people gather and discuss until they agree — for example, a *kgotla* in Botswana, a
   village assembly where anyone may speak. **Direct votes:** citizens vote on laws
   themselves — for example, Switzerland, where people vote on many questions several
   times a year. **Choosing representatives:** citizens vote for people who then decide
   for them — as in India, Japan, Brazil, and many other countries, whose parliaments or
   congresses pass laws. For each, ask the same question: *who decides? who has a voice?*
3. **Sort and compare (15 min).** Take your example cards. Each describes a decision
   being made somewhere. Sort each into one of the three ways. Then compare two of them:
   who decides in each? Who gets a voice? What is one way they are alike, and one way
   they differ?
4. **Weigh, but don't rank (10 min).** Each way has strengths and limits. Consensus lets
   everyone speak but can be slow; direct votes give people a direct say but not everyone
   always knows the details; representatives can work quickly but must be trusted to
   listen. Notice: there is no single "best" way — but the question *who has a voice?* is
   always worth asking.
5. **Record (5 min).** On your compare page, write two ways, one example of each, and
   one likeness and one difference.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the chart with a sentence starter ("In ___, decisions are made by
   ___; in ___, they are made by ___.").
- **Extension:** Name a strength and a limit of each way, and argue which gives more
  people a voice and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name two ways decisions are made in
  different countries and say who decides in each?
- **Peer check:** Partners check each other's compare page: "Did you name *who decides*
  in each place, not only the country name?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how decisions are made in your household or community — one person,
a talk, or a vote? — and compare it with one of the ways you learned today.
