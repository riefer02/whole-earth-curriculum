# Lesson 9 — Helping Two Peers Solve a Conflict

## Summary

Learners practice **helping two peers solve a simple conflict** by having **each side
listen** and **find a fair solution**. They learn a short mediation routine — each side
speaks one at a time, each listens and repeats back, name the needs, then find a fair
solution both accept — and role-play it in trios, producing a mediation record.

## Objectives

- Help two peers solve a simple conflict by having each side listen and find a fair
  solution. _(D09.S4.05.01)_

## Connection

Two friends both want the last turn, or the same spot, or the one borrowed pencil. Both
are upset, and neither is listening. A helper can change everything: not by choosing a
winner, but by helping **each side speak, and each side listen**, until they find a
**fair solution** they both accept. That is what you will practice today — in the
classroom, the yard, or anywhere people disagree.

## Materials

- Mediation-steps poster
- Conflict role-play cards
- Mediation record page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the mediation-steps poster.
- Copy role-play cards for each trio and a record page per trio.
- Arrange trios (two sides + one helper); prepare a talking object if going low-tech.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is a short **peer
mediation routine**: (1) each side speaks, one at a time; (2) the other side listens and
**repeats back** what they heard; (3) name each side's need; (4) **brainstorm fair
solutions**; (5) choose one both accept. This is a skill, so **model the full routine
once** as a worked example before trios try it (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep
it **non-shaming**: a conflict is not a moral failure, and no child is "the bad one."
The helper's job is dignity and listening, never judgment. Emphasize that the fair
solution must be accepted by *both* sides — not imposed. Draw a clear safety boundary:
if anyone is hurt, scared, threatened, or unsafe — or the conflict feels too big — the
helper's first move is to bring in a trusted adult; a peer helper never resolves a
serious conflict alone. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have practiced comparing choices and seeing fairness. Now a
   skill for real life: when two people disagree, you can **help them solve it** without
   anyone winning or losing. Try to recall: what does it mean to *really* listen?
   (Pause.)
2. **Meet the steps (10 min).** Look at the poster. The helper has five moves: **each
   side speaks, one at a time** (no interrupting); **each side listens and repeats back**
   what the other said; **name the needs** ("you need a turn; you need the pencil too");
   **brainstorm fair solutions** (two or three ideas); **choose one both accept.**
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch and help. Two volunteers are the sides ("I had the
   ball first!" / "I never get a turn!"); you are the helper. Walk each step slowly, and
   let the class name what to say next. End with a fair solution *both* agree to.
4. **Role-play in trios (15 min).** Take a role-play card. One of you is the helper; two
   are the sides. Run the steps. The helper keeps it calm and fair; the sides practice
   speaking and **repeating back**. If the first solution doesn't work, brainstorm again.
   Remember: if anyone is hurt, scared, threatened, or unsafe — or the conflict feels too
   big — the helper's first move is to bring in a trusted adult. A peer helper never
   resolves a serious conflict alone.
5. **Record and close (5 min).** On your page, write each side's need and the fair
   solution you reached. Then say one thing you noticed: *what helped the two sides
   actually listen to each other?*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with the steps printed as a checklist and a sentence starter
  for repeating back ("What I hear you saying is ___").
- **Extension:** Try a harder scenario where both sides' needs are partly right, and find
  a solution that honors both rather than splitting the difference.
- **Non-speaking & seated participation:** Role-play is done seated, so no walking or
  standing is required. A learner may express a need by pointing to a picture/word card,
  writing on the mediation record page, or gesturing; the helper then reads back or points
  to what each side showed. Repeating back can be done by pointing to the need card or
  writing, so fluent speech is never required to take a side or act as helper.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/observation):** In role-play, does the helper ensure each side
  speaks and listens (repeats back), and guide the pair to a fair solution both accept?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I help both sides speak? Did I help them listen?
  Was the solution fair to *both*, and did *both* accept it?"

## Home connection

Next time you see two people disagreeing at home or nearby, you can help each side speak
and listen — and find a solution both accept. Remember the safety line: if anyone is
hurt, scared, threatened, or unsafe, or the disagreement feels too big, the first step is
to bring in a trusted adult rather than handle it alone.
