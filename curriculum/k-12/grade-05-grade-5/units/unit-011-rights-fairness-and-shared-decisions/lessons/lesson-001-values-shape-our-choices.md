# Lesson 1 — Values Shape Our Choices

## Summary

Learners explain how a person's **values** shape their **choices**. They recall what a
value is, analyze a worked example (two characters in the *same* situation making
*different* choices because their values differ), then work through story cards in
pairs and write or tell their own example of a value guiding a choice.

## Objectives

- Explain how a person's values can shape their choices, using a story or example.
  _(D09.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Two children, one extra bowl of food. One passes it to the person who has none; the
other keeps it for later. Same situation, different choices — because they care about
different things. A **value** is something you care about, and it works like an inner
compass: it quietly points your choices one way or another, in a kitchen, a classroom,
a market, or anywhere people are together.

## Materials

- Value-and-choice poster
- Story cards
- Value-and-choice page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the value-and-choice poster (two characters, same situation, different
  values → different choices).
- Copy story cards for each pair and a value-and-choice page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle and prepare one told story for the whole-group opening.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is a single, precise
link: **a value (something a person cares about) shapes the choices they make.** Use
explicit instruction and a worked example first — this is a reasoning skill, and novices
learn it best by watching a model before they try it themselves (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Do **not** rank values ("kindness is better than looking out for oneself");
instead notice that different people hold different values, and both shape real choices.
Distinguish **evidence from values** (`docs/philosophy.md` §5): *that* values shape
choices is something we can observe in stories; *which* values to hold is each person's
and community's choice. Keep questions open — invite, never impose. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Earlier this year you met the word **value** — something you care
   about that guides what you do. Without looking back, try to remember: what is a value?
   Name one value you care about. (Pause to recall — that is retrieval, and it helps
   learning stick.)
2. **Meet the link (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two children, one extra bowl of food,
   and a person with none. One child **cares about kindness** and passes the bowl. The
   other **cares about looking out for themselves first** and keeps it. Same situation,
   different choices. Why? Because their **values** — the things they care about — pointed
   their choices in different directions. The link is: **value → choice.**
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch and help build one together. *Story:* "Lina finds
   a wallet on the path." What does Lina care about? If she **values honesty**, she
   returns it. If she **values not being noticed**, she might leave it. Name the value,
   then name the choice it shapes, then say the link in one sentence: "Lina values
   honesty, so she chose to return the wallet."
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a story card. Read it together. Ask the two
   questions: *What does this character care about? What choice does it shape?* Then say
   the link aloud in one sentence, the way you did in the worked example.
5. **Your own example (10 min).** On your page, name one value you care about, and
   describe a real or invented choice it shaped. Draw it if you like. There is no single
   right answer — your compass is your own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence starter ("I care about ___ , so I chose to ___") and a
  story card with a very clear value–choice pair; scribe as needed.
- **Extension:** Write a two-character story where the *same* situation leads to
  *different* choices because the two characters hold different values.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a value, name the choice it shaped,
  and connect them in one sentence?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their example and asks, "Is the choice really guided
  by the value I named — or by something else?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time a value they care about shaped a choice they made, then
share your example.
