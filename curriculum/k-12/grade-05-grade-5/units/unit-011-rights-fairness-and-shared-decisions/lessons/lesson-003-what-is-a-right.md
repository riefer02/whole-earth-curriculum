# Lesson 3 — What Is a Right?

## Summary

Learners define a **right** — a shared promise to protect something every child needs,
simply because they are a child. They sort needs and wants to feel the boundary of a
right, and learn that many rights are written down in an agreement countries made
together, the **United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC)**.

## Objectives

- Explain what a right is and where some rights come from — such as the UN Convention
  on the Rights of the Child. _(D09.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

You need to play, to learn, and to be safe. Those are not rewards you earn by being
good — they are things you need just because you are a child. A **right** is a promise
that these needs *should* be protected for you. Some rights are written down in an
agreement that many countries of the world made together, so that every child — not
just some — is meant to be protected.

## Materials

- What-is-a-right poster
- Rights, wants, and needs cards
- A right card

## Preparation

- Print or draw the what-is-a-right poster (definition + play/learn/be-safe icons).
- Copy card sets for each group and a right card for each learner.
- Have a child-friendly UNCRC summary on hand (print or projected) for the enriched path.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: (1) a **right is a
shared promise to protect something every child needs**, and (2) some rights are written
in the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child**, an agreement many countries made.
Frame the UNCRC as **a shared human agreement, not a revealed truth or a dogma** — people
wrote it, people can improve it, and it exists because adults agreed children should be
protected. Distinguish **evidence from values** (`docs/philosophy.md` §5): *that* the
Convention exists and what it says are facts we can check; *that* children's needs
*should* be protected is a value, held widely but still a value. This honors the unit's
rule: questions are held as questions, not answered as dogma. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have been thinking about values and choices. Now a new word:
   **right**. Try to remember — have you heard it before? What do you think it might
   mean? (Pause to recall.)
2. **Meet the definition (10 min).** Look at the poster. A **right** is a shared promise
   to protect something every child **needs** — like playing, learning, and being safe.
   A right is not a prize you earn. It is owed to you **because you are a child**, just
   as clean air is owed to every living thing because it needs to breathe.
3. **Sort: need or want? (15 min).** Take your cards. Sort each into one of two piles:
   **every child needs this protected** (a right), or **some children may want this, but
   it is not a need every child has** (a want). For example: *food and safety* — every
   child. *A new toy* — nice, but not a need. Talk over the hard ones with your group.
   There may be more than one reasonable answer — say your reason.
4. **Where rights come from (10 min).** Rights do not appear by magic. People noticed
   that children were not always protected, so adults from many countries talked
   together and **agreed** on a list: the **United Nations Convention on the Rights of
   the Child (UNCRC)**. It names rights like to **learn, play, be safe, rest, and be
   heard**. It is a promise countries made — and a promise is only as real as people
   keeping it.
5. **Write your right card (5 min).** On your card, write one right in your own words
   and what it protects. Share with a partner.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a sentence starter ("Every child has the right to ___ because
  ___") and a smaller card set with clear needs vs. wants.
- **Extension:** Find a second right in the child-friendly UNCRC summary and explain why
  it is a need every child has, not a want.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner say what a right is in their own words and
  name where some rights come from (an agreement many countries made, the UNCRC)?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their right card and asks, "Is this something every
  child needs — or only something some might want?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What does every child need to be safe and to grow?" Then tell them
what a right is and one right from the UNCRC.
