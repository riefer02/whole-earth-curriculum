# Lesson 9 — Fairness and Dignity: Honored or Denied

## Summary

Learners define **fairness** and **dignity** in plain, concrete terms and sort real-life
scenes into where each is **honored** and where it is **denied**. They see that fairness
means equal respect and a fair chance, and dignity means being treated as a person of worth
— and they name one real example of each, preparing them for the unit's capstone.

## Objectives

- Explain what fairness and dignity mean using a concrete example of when they were honored or
  denied. _(D08.S4.05.02)_

## Connection

Picture a shared table. When everyone has a place, a name that is said correctly, and a fair
share, something good is happening — that is **dignity honored**. Now picture a doorway where
some people are kept out, or paid less for the same work, or treated as if they matter less —
that is **dignity denied**. You already know these two feelings. Today you learn their names
and how to tell them apart in the world.

## Materials

- Fairness-and-dignity poster
- Honored or denied cards
- Fairness–dignity page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fairness-and-dignity poster (definitions plus honored and denied examples).
- Copy and cut the eight honored-or-denied scene cards, and copy a page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle or two ground circles for sorting.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is the **definitions, made
concrete**: fairness = equal respect and a fair chance, with rules applying to all; dignity =
being treated as a person of worth, never less than others. Use everyday, recognizable scenes
so the concepts stay concrete before any abstraction. Some learners may have experienced
denied dignity personally; keep the tone calm, name feelings, and connect to what learners can
*do* (welcome, include, speak up, be fair). Never imply any group "deserves" less. This lesson
produces the unit's **fairness-and-dignity example** portfolio artifact. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have met people who stood up to unfairness. Today you name the two
   big ideas behind it: **fairness** and **dignity** — and what it looks like when they are
   honored or denied.
2. **Meet the words (15 min).** Look at the poster. **Fairness** means everyone is treated with
   equal respect and gets a fair chance, and the rules apply to everyone — not just some.
   **Dignity** means being treated as a person of **worth**, never as less than others. *Honored:*
   a new family is welcomed by name, everyone gets a turn and a share, equal pay for equal
   work. *Denied:* separate doors for some people, being paid less for the same work, a name
   mocked instead of said with respect.
3. **Sort the scenes (20 min).** Take the eight cards and sort each into **honored** or
   **denied**, and say why. Check with a partner: do you agree? Notice that denied dignity is
   not "someone's fault" — it is a wrong that can be set right, as the people in our last lesson
   showed.
4. **Write your example (10 min).** On your page, write in your own words what fairness and
   dignity mean, and one real example of each being **honored** and one being **denied** — from
   your life, your reading, or this unit. This page becomes part of your portfolio.
5. **Close (5 min).** Fairness and dignity are not fancy words — they are the measure of how we
   treat one another. You now carry both, and you can name when they are honored or denied.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use picture scenes and sentence frames ("This shows dignity honored because…"),
  and scribe the learner's example.
- **Extension:** Connect one denied-dignity scene to a real historical example from this unit
  (the salt tax, unfair pay, being shut out) and explain how people eventually worked to honor
  dignity.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner state what fairness and dignity mean
  and give a concrete example of each being honored and denied?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I define both words in my own words, and did I give a
  real example of each — honored and denied?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what "dignity" means to them, and share your definition and one example
from today.

## Resources

- Fairness and dignity are widely shared ethical concepts; the curriculum teaches them as
  *values* (how things ought to be) rather than as facts, and distinguishes the two
  (docs/philosophy.md §5). The concrete examples (equal pay for equal work, being welcomed by
  name, being shut out or treated as less) are drawn from everyday life so the concepts stay
  concrete. The historical links — the salt tax, unfair pay, and being shut out — connect to
  the unit's resistance examples (the Salt March, the Aba Women's War, the Wave Hill walk-off),
  each documented in the previous lesson's Resources.
