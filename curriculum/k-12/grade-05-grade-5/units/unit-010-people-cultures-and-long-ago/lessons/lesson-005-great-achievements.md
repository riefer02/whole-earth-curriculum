# Lesson 5 — Great Achievements: Building, Art, and Learning

## Summary

Learners meet great achievements from **non-European civilizations** — **Great Zimbabwe's**
stone city, the **Library of Timbuktu**, **Benin's** brass plaques, the **Maya's** star
records, **Machu Picchu**, and the **Baghdad House of Wisdom** — and describe one using a
**what–how–shows** frame. They see that building, art, and learning are made by many
peoples, each showing what its society valued and could do.

## Objectives

- Describe an achievement in art, building, or learning from a non-European civilization and
  what it shows about that society. _(D08.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

Think of the greatest thing people in your region have made — a bridge, a school, a song, a
way of growing food. When you look at it, you are looking at what those people cared about
and what they could do. The same is true long ago and far away: a stone city in southern
Africa, a library in West Africa, star records in Mesoamerica — each is a message about the
people who made it. Today you read a few of those messages.

## Materials

- Achievements gallery cards
- Achievement page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the six achievements gallery cards (Great Zimbabwe; the Library of Timbuktu; Benin
  brass; Maya star records; Machu Picchu; the Baghdad House of Wisdom).
- Copy an achievement page for each learner.
- Arrange small groups or a circle for reading and sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **non-European
civilizations produced great achievements in building, art, and learning**, each showing
what its society valued — a direct counter to any idea that accomplishment belongs to one
region. Present each achievement with equal dignity; do not frame them as "surprisingly
advanced" (which implies a low expectation). Distinguish the *fact* (these works exist and
are documented) from the *value* (human accomplishment in all its variety is worth honoring).
If a learner's own people or region has a great achievement, invite them to add it to the
gallery. This lesson produces the unit's **non-European achievement** portfolio artifact. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have seen how tools and stories shape life. Today you meet what
   peoples **made** — their great achievements in building, art, and learning — and what each
   shows about them.
2. **Walk the gallery (20 min).** Read the six cards. *Great Zimbabwe:* a city of tall stone
   walls, built in southern Africa with stones fitted together **without mortar**, the heart
   of a trading kingdom — it shows skill in building and in organizing people. *The Library of
   Timbuktu:* in West Africa, thousands of manuscripts on astronomy, mathematics, medicine,
   and law were written and copied, and families guarded them for centuries — it shows a deep
   love of learning. *Benin:* in West Africa, artists cast brass plaques with the lost-wax
   method, recording their kingdom's history — it shows artistry and history-keeping. *The
   Maya:* in Mesoamerica, star-keepers recorded the movements of Venus and the Sun with great
   precision and built a calendar — it shows careful mathematics and time-keeping. *Machu
   Picchu:* a stone city built high in the Andes, fitted so tightly the stones still stand — it
   shows building skill and care for the mountain landscape. *The Baghdad House of Wisdom:* a
   center of learning where scholars gathered and translated knowledge from many lands — it
   shows how learning grows when peoples meet.
3. **Describe one (15 min).** Choose one achievement. Answer three questions: **What** was
   achieved? **How** was it done? **What does it show** about the society that made it? Write
   or draw your answers on the achievement page.
4. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your what–how–shows in one breath, then listen to
   theirs. Notice: every one of these is a human achievement, made by people who were not
   "behind" anyone — they were themselves.
5. **Close (5 min).** Building, art, and learning belong to all of humanity. You have read a
   few of the world's great messages, and added one to your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two illustrated gallery cards and a fill-in what–how–shows frame; scribe
  or draw with the learner.
- **Extension:** Compare two achievements from different places and explain what each shows
  about its society — and what the two have in common.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner describe one non-European achievement
  (what, how, and what it shows about the society)?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I describe what was made, how, and what it shows —
  and did I treat every achievement with the same respect?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to name a great achievement from your own region or people and what it
shows about them — and add it to your gallery.

## Resources

- Great Zimbabwe, a stone city of the 11th–15th centuries CE built without mortar and the
  center of a trading kingdom, is a UNESCO World Heritage site
  (<https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/364/>). Timbuktu's manuscripts and the city's role as a
  center of learning are documented by UNESCO (Memory of the World,
  <https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world>). The Benin bronzes, brass plaques cast with the
  lost-wax method, are held and studied at the British Museum and are the subject of ongoing
  returns to Nigeria. Maya astronomy and the calendar are documented by the Smithsonian
  Institution and the Maya themselves. Machu Picchu is a UNESCO World Heritage site
  (<https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/274/>). The Baghdad House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) is
  documented in the history of science. Dates and details are as scholars currently understand
  them and may be revised.
