# Lesson 1 — Two Rivers, Two Peoples: A Comparing Timeline

## Summary

Learners build their first **comparing timeline**: two civilizations on two parallel
rows that share **one time scale**. A worked example places **ancient Egypt** (the Nile)
and the **Indus Valley** (the Indus) side by side, and learners discover something
surprising: while Egypt was building the Great Pyramid, Indus cities were thriving at the
same moment — two peoples, thousands of kilometres apart, both growing great river-valley
cities. The timeline is this unit's first portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Build a timeline that compares two cultures or civilizations and identify what changed
  and what stayed the same in each. _(D08.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Think of two friends born on the same day, far apart. One is learning to swim by the sea
while the other is learning to herd goats in the hills. A timeline with **two rows** shows
that these two lives are happening **at the same time**, even though they look very
different. History is like that: peoples on different rivers, on different continents,
were building cities and telling stories **at the same moment** — and a comparing timeline
lets us see them together.

## Materials

- Comparing-timeline poster
- Event cards (Egypt and Indus Valley)
- Blank two-row timeline page
- Pencils and a straight edge

## Preparation

- Print or draw the comparing-timeline poster (Egypt top row, Indus Valley bottom row,
  shared BCE/CE scale).
- Copy the event cards and a blank two-row timeline page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for the worked example and a flat space for placing events.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **placing events on
a shared time scale and reading "at the same time" across two rows** — not memorizing
dates. Model the worked example fully (explicit instruction, guided practice) before
learners work on their own; a comparing timeline is a new skill for most Grade 5 learners.
Explain **BCE** ("before the common era") and **CE** ("common era") simply, and note that
many peoples count years in their own ways — BCE/CE is one shared convention, not the only
one. Guard against ranking ("Egypt was better than the Indus"): the point is that both
peoples built great things, differently. This lesson produces the unit's **two-civilization
timeline** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you put events on a single timeline with dates. Today you
   learn something bigger: a **comparing timeline** — two rows, two peoples, one shared
   time scale — so you can see what was happening in two places **at the same time**.
2. **Meet the two rivers (10 min).** Look at the poster. The top row is **ancient Egypt**,
   a civilization that grew along the **Nile River** in Africa. The bottom row is the
   **Indus Valley**, a civilization that grew along the **Indus River** in South Asia.
   Both rivers flooded and left rich soil for farming; both peoples built great cities.
   They lived thousands of kilometres apart — and neither one is "more important" than the
   other.
3. **Worked example: place the events (15 min).** Watch as we place the events in order,
   oldest to newest. *Egypt:* unified around **3100 BCE**; the **Great Pyramid at Giza**
   built around **2600 BCE**; the **New Kingdom**, a time of great power, around
   **1500 BCE**; and rule under the **Roman Empire** from **30 BCE**. *Indus Valley:*
   great cities such as **Harappa and Mohenjo-daro** thriving around **2600 BCE**; the
   cities **declining** around **1900 BCE**. Now look across the rows: while the Great
   Pyramid was rising in Egypt, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were busy, brick-built cities on
   the Indus. That overlap is the "same time" the timeline shows.
4. **Place your own (15 min).** Take the six event cards and place them on your blank
   two-row timeline — Egypt on top, Indus on the bottom, in date order. Use your straight
   edge to keep the rows even. Check with a partner: is every card on the right row and in
   the right order? Which two events happened at the same time?
5. **Close (5 min).** A comparing timeline lets you see two peoples **together**, at the
   same moment, each doing remarkable things. You have just built one. It becomes the first
   page of your portfolio for this unit.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-marked timeline with faint date lines and fewer event cards;
  scribe or place cards with the learner and read "at the same time" aloud together.
- **Extension:** Add a third row for one more civilization (for example, the Maya or early
  China) and place two or three of its events, noting any overlap with the first two rows.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner place events on the correct row
  and in the correct order, and point to two events that happened at the same time?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads the timeline back and asks, "Are the events in order,
  and can I name one thing that happened in both places at once?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you two things that happened in the same year in two different
places they know — and share the "same time" you found between Egypt and the Indus Valley.

## Resources

- Ancient Egypt: unification around 3100 BCE and the Great Pyramid at Giza around 2600 BCE
  are standard dates in the archaeological record (see the British Museum's Egypt
  collection, <https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/egypt>). The Indus Valley
  civilization's mature phase around 2600–1900 BCE and its cities Harappa and Mohenjo-daro
  are documented by the Archaeological Survey of India and UNESCO World Heritage listings
  (Mohenjo-daro, <https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/138/>). Dates are approximations that
  scholars revise as new evidence appears; the comparing idea, not the exact year, is what
  matters here.
