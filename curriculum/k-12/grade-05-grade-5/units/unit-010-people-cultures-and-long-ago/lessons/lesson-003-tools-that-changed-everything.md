# Lesson 3 — Tools That Changed Everything: Writing, the Wheel, the Plow

## Summary

Learners explore how a single tool changes a whole way of life. A worked example traces
**writing** — before it, people had to remember everything and ideas could only travel with
people; after it, records, trade, laws, and stories could last and spread. Learners then
choose the **wheel** or the **plow** and trace its own before-and-after chain, seeing
technology as something people *invented and used*, with effects both helpful and
complicated.

## Objectives

- Explain how a tool or technology, such as writing, the wheel, or the plow, changed how
  people lived and worked. _(D08.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

Imagine your day with no way to write anything down — no list, no message, no book. You
would have to carry every word in your head, and a story could only go as far as a person
could walk and remember it. Now imagine the same day with writing: a note left on a table
tells your family where you are. That difference — before and after a tool — is what we
study today. A tool changes the work, and the work changes the life.

## Materials

- Tool-effect chain poster
- Tool cards (writing, the wheel, the plow)
- Before–after page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the tool-effect chain poster for writing (before → after).
- Copy the three tool cards and a before–after page for each learner.
- Gather a few props for acting out (a log or round stones, a heavy object, a stick to
  "write" in sand).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **a tool changes how
people live and work, with effects both helpful and complicated** — not that tools are
simple "good" or "bad." Model the writing chain fully (worked example) before learners
trace the wheel or the plow. Keep it honest and global: writing appeared independently in
Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica; the wheel spread across Eurasia but appeared
only on toys in the Americas (where large draft animals were not available) — a real,
fascinating difference, not a ranking. Technology is taught with neither worship nor fear.
See `docs/philosophy.md` §7 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have seen change and continuity on a timeline. Today you meet a
   cause of change: the **tool** — something people invented that changed how they lived
   and worked.
2. **Meet writing (10 min).** Look at the chain poster. **Before writing:** people kept
   everything in memory, and ideas could only travel as far as a person could walk and
   remember. **After writing:** records were kept, trade was counted, laws were written
   down, and stories could last and travel far. Writing appeared independently in several
   places — **Mesopotamia** (clay tablets), **Egypt** (hieroglyphs), **China** (oracle
   bones), and **Mesoamerica** (Maya glyphs). No single people "invented it for everyone";
   many peoples invented it for themselves.
3. **Trace your own tool (20 min).** Choose the **wheel** or the **plow** from the tool
   cards. The wheel: first used for pottery, then for carts, it changed travel and the
   moving of goods — though in the Americas it appeared only on toys, because there were no
   large animals to pull carts. The plow: a tool pulled through soil, it let people farm
   more land and feed more people. On your before–after page, write or draw what work was
   like **before** the tool and **after** it, and one effect on daily life. Check with a
   partner: does your chain make sense, and could the same tool have had a *complicated*
   effect too?
4. **Share a chain (10 min).** Tell your partner your before–after chain in one breath:
   "Before the plow, people could only scratch small plots; after the plow, they could farm
   more land and feed more people — but working the soil also got harder and tied people to
   the land."
5. **Close (5 min).** A tool is not magic — it is a choice people made that changed their
   work and their life. You can now trace that change from before to after.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide picture prompts for each tool's before and after, and scribe the
  learner's chain as they act it out.
- **Extension:** Choose a second tool and write a before–after chain, then compare which
  tool you think changed daily life more — with reasons, and no single "right" answer.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner trace one tool from before to after and
  name an effect on how people lived or worked?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I show both the before and the after, and did I
  name at least one real effect — including a complicated one?"

## Home connection

Choose one tool your family uses every day (a phone, a stove, a bicycle) and ask someone at
home what life or work was like before it was common.

## Resources

- Writing emerged independently in several world regions — cuneiform in Mesopotamia
  (around 3200 BCE), Egyptian hieroglyphs, Chinese oracle-bone script, and Maya glyphs — a
  widely documented fact (British Museum, cuneiform; Smithsonian, writing systems). The
  wheel first appears around 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia (pottery then transport), and in the
  Americas it appears on toys but not for transport, in part because large draft animals
  were absent (Smithsonian Institution). Early plows (ards) appear in Mesopotamia and Egypt
  by roughly 4000–3000 BCE, with later iron plows in China; dates are approximate and
  revised as new evidence appears. The framing that tools have both helpful and complicated
  effects follows the curriculum's "honest about technology" commitment (docs/philosophy.md).
