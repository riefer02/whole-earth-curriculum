# Lesson 4 — Stories of Beginnings: Comparing Origin Stories

## Summary

Learners compare three **origin stories** as **living traditions**, told with respect: the
Māori separation of **Ranginui** (sky) and **Papatūānuku** (earth), the Haudenosaunee story
of **Sky Woman and Turtle Island**, and the Yoruba story of **land made from the waters**.
They see how each reflects the place its people live and the things they value — and they
practice holding many peoples' stories side by side without ranking them.

## Objectives

- Compare origin stories from two or more peoples and explain how each reflects the people
  who tell it. _(D08.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

Every family has a story about where it comes from — a grandparent's journey, a village
your people left, a place your name remembers. Those stories tell you something real about
who you are: what your people noticed, what they valued, where they lived. Peoples all over
the world keep such stories about the very beginning — and each one reflects the people who
tell it, the way a family story reflects a family.

## Materials

- Origin-story cards (Māori, Haudenosaunee, Yoruba)
- Reflection chart
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the three origin-story cards, written simply and respectfully, naming the people and
  noting each is a living tradition kept alive today.
- Copy a reflection chart for each learner.
- Review the note below on how to hold origin stories with respect.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). These are **living traditions** of real
peoples, not "myths" in the sense of "made-up tales." Use the words *origin story* and
*told by*, and name each people with respect. Some learners may belong to these peoples or
hold other sacred traditions; receive their knowledge warmly and let them be the authority
on their own story. Do **not** rank the stories or ask which is "true"; hold them side by
side, as the curriculum holds scientific and cultural accounts without ranking them
(`docs/philosophy.md` §11). Note that details vary from teller to teller and community to
community; that variety is part of a living tradition, not an error. This lesson produces
the unit's **origin-story comparison** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md` on
holding topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have traced change on timelines and in tools. Today you listen to
   how peoples tell the very beginning — their **origin stories** — and how each one reflects
   the people who tell it.
2. **Listen to three stories (20 min).** Read the cards. *Māori (Aotearoa New Zealand):* the
   sky, Ranginui, and the earth, Papatūānuku, held each other so closely their children lived
   in darkness — until the children pushed them apart and let light into the world. A
   sea-and-sky people tell of light and separation. *Haudenosaunee (North America):* Sky
   Woman fell toward the water, and the animals helped her; the earth grew on Turtle's back,
   becoming Turtle Island. A planting people tell of land carried and grown. *Yoruba (West
   Africa):* the creator sent beings to make land rise from the primordial waters, and the
   world began from water. A coast-and-water people tell of land made from the waters. Each
   story is kept alive by its people today.
3. **Find what each reflects (15 min).** On your reflection chart, write each story and what
   it shows about its people — the sky, earth, sea, water, planting, family, light, balance.
   Ask: *What does this people notice? What do they seem to value?* For example, the Māori
   story holds the sky and earth as parents and the longing for light; the Haudenosaunee story
   holds the land and the animals who helped; the Yoruba story holds water and the making of
   land.
4. **Compare, without ranking (10 min).** With a partner, find one thing the stories share —
   sky and earth, water and land, light and beginning — and one thing that is different
   because the peoples live in different places. Notice: no story is "better" than another;
   each is true *to its people*.
5. **Close (5 min).** Origin stories are a window: through them we see what a people noticed
   and treasured. You have held three windows side by side, with respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read one story at a time and draw its central image (sky/earth, turtle,
  water) before writing; use sentence frames ("This story shows the people valued…").
- **Extension:** Compare two stories directly in writing — what they share and how each
  reflects a different place and way of life.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner retell each story's central image
  and explain one thing it reflects about the people who tell it?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I hold each story with respect, and did I say what
  each one reflects — not which one is 'best'?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home if your family or community has a story about where you come from, and
share one thing it shows about what your people value.

## Resources

- These origin stories are living traditions of real peoples. The Māori account of Ranginui
  and Papatūānuku is told across iwi (tribes) of Aotearoa New Zealand (Te Ara — The
  Encyclopedia of New Zealand, <https://teara.govt.nz>). The Haudenosaunee (Six Nations)
  account of Sky Woman and Turtle Island is a living tradition of the Haudenosaunee
  Confederacy (National Museum of the American Indian,
  <https://americanindian.si.edu/>). The Yoruba account of creation, involving Olodumare and
  the making of land from the waters, is a living tradition of Yoruba-speaking peoples of
  West Africa (see the British Museum and UNESCO's documentation of Yoruba culture). Details
  vary by community; the point here is that each story reflects its people, not a single
  fixed wording. Treat each as its people's own account, not as a "myth" to rank.
