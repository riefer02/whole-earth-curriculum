# Lesson 6 — People on the Move: Tracing a Migration

## Summary

Learners trace a real migration — the settlement of the Pacific by **Polynesian voyagers**
over about three thousand years — and learn to separate **push** reasons (why people leave)
from **pull** reasons (why they choose a new place). Using a route map and a push–pull
chart, they record the movement and produce this unit's **migration note**, seeing migration
as a story of skill, courage, and hope, not of conquest.

## Objectives

- Trace the migration of a specific people and explain the push and pull reasons for their
  movement. _(D08.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

People move for reasons you can find in your own life. **Push** reasons make a person leave —
a field that no longer feeds the family, a crowded home, a storm, a need for safety. **Pull**
reasons draw a person to a new place — good land, clean water, work, family already there.
When your own family or a neighbor's moved, both pushes and pulls were at work. Today you
trace how a whole people moved, step by step, across a vast ocean.

## Materials

- Migration route map (Pacific)
- Push–pull chart
- Migration note page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the Pacific route map showing islands settled in order over about three
  thousand years.
- Copy a push–pull chart and a migration note page for each learner.
- Have a stone or shell ready for the listening circle if you use the low-tech path.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **migration is a
human story of skill and hope, driven by pushes and pulls — not a tale of conquest or of
"discovering" empty land.** The Polynesian settlement of the Pacific is a powerful example
precisely because it shows courage, navigation knowledge, and gradual settlement over
generations. Model the push–pull distinction explicitly (worked example) before learners
work alone. Be careful with any example that involves one people displacing another: keep
the focus on the moving people's skill, reasons, and agency, and avoid framing any place as
"empty" or "waiting to be found." This lesson produces the unit's **migration note**
portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have met achievements and stories. Today you trace movement — how
   a people crossed a vast ocean, and **why**.
2. **Meet the voyage (15 min).** Look at the route map. Starting from islands in the western
   Pacific, voyagers sailed in **double-hulled canoes**, reading the stars, the swells, and
   the birds, to settle island after island — Tonga, Samoa, then farther to Hawaiʻi, Rapa
   Nui, and Aotearoa New Zealand — over about three thousand years. This was no accident: it
   was planned, skilled navigation, passed down through families.
3. **Separate push from pull (15 min).** On your chart, list the reasons. **Push** (why
   leave): an island's food and space grew full as families grew; a need for new gardens and
   fresh water. **Pull** (why go): new islands with fresh water, coconut, taro, breadfruit,
   and fish; the chance to start a new settlement. The same voyage had both.
4. **Write your migration note (10 min).** On the note page, trace the route in one or two
   sentences and name **one push** and **one pull** reason. This note becomes part of your
   portfolio. Check with a partner: is the push really a *push* and the pull really a *pull*?
5. **Close (5 min).** Migration is one of the oldest human stories — people moving with skill
   and hope. You can now trace a people's movement and name the pushes and pulls behind it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn route and two labeled arrow cards (push, pull) to place at
  the right spots; scribe the note.
- **Extension:** Choose a second people's migration (Bantu-speaking farmers' slow spread
  across Africa, or the first peopling of the Americas) and name its push and pull reasons.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner trace the route and name at least one
  push and one pull reason, correctly distinguishing them?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I separate the pushes (why they left) from the pulls
  (why they went there), and did I tell the story with respect?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home why your family or a neighbor's family once moved — and see if you can
name the push and the pull in their story.

## Resources

- The settlement of the Pacific by Polynesian voyagers over roughly three thousand years, using
  double-hulled canoes and navigation by stars, swells, and birds, is documented by the
  Polynesian Voyaging Society (<https://hokulea.com/>) and by archaeological work across the
  Pacific. The 1976 voyage of the Hōkūleʻa re-created the ancient way and is a living
  continuation of that knowledge. The push–pull distinction is a standard framework in
  geography and migration study. Dates and routes are as scholars currently understand them
  and continue to be refined by new evidence.
