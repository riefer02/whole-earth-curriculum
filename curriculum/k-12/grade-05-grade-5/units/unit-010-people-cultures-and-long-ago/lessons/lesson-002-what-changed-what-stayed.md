# Lesson 2 — What Changed, What Stayed: Change and Continuity on the Nile and Indus

## Summary

Learners turn from *building* the comparing timeline to *reading* it. They sort features
of ancient Egypt and the Indus Valley into **what changed over time** and **what stayed
the same**, and discover that **change and continuity live side by side** in every
civilization — the river kept flooding every year while buildings grew grander, and writing
lasted for centuries while cities rose and later declined.

## Objectives

- Build a timeline that compares two cultures or civilizations and identify what changed
  and what stayed the same in each. _(D08.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Your own life has both change and continuity. Your body grows and your skills change every
year — yet some things stay the same: the way your family greets each other, the season the
rains come, the food at a festival. History works the same way. A civilization keeps some
things steady for a thousand years **while** other things change completely — and the
timeline we built yesterday is the map that shows both.

## Materials

- Changed / stayed the same chart
- Egypt and Indus fact strips
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the two-column chart (changed | stayed the same) for each pair.
- Copy and cut the fact strips for Egypt and the Indus Valley.
- Keep yesterday's comparing timeline visible so learners can point to where a change
  happened.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **change and
continuity are both real and both normal** — a civilization is not "better" because it
changed more or less. Model the first sort aloud (guided practice) so learners see the
reasoning: a feature is "continuity" if it lasted and returned, "change" if it appeared,
grew, or faded. Welcome disagreement and ask for reasons rather than one "right" answer;
some features (a city, a craft) are both. Keep both civilizations at equal dignity. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Yesterday you built a timeline of two river peoples. Today you read
   it a new way: **what changed** over time, and **what stayed the same**.
2. **Meet the two ideas (10 min).** **Change** is when something appears, grows, or fades.
   **Continuity** is when something lasts and returns. Look at the chart. *Changed:* the
   pyramids grew grander over centuries; the Indus cities rose, then later declined.
   *Stayed the same:* the Nile's flood returned every year; writing kept records across
   the centuries in both lands. Notice: a civilization has **both** at once.
3. **Sort the features (20 min).** Take the fact strips. For each strip, ask: did this
   **change** over the civilization's long life, or did it **stay the same**? Place it in
   the matching column. *Egypt:* the yearly flood; pyramids; farming along the river;
   writing in hieroglyphs; the power of rulers growing and shrinking. *Indus Valley:* grid
   streets and brick drains; the still-undeciphered script; farming; the cities rising and
   declining. Check with a partner: do you agree, and can you each give a reason?
4. **Point to the timeline (10 min).** Go back to yesterday's timeline. Point to *where*
   a change happened (a pyramid built, a city declining) and to what *lasted* across the
   whole row (the river, farming, writing). Say it in one sentence: "The river stayed the
   same, while the cities changed."
5. **Close (5 min).** Every people keeps some things steady and changes others — that is
   the rhythm of the human story. You can now find both on a timeline.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use picture cards instead of text strips, and sort one civilization with a
  partner while the facilitator models the reasoning aloud.
- **Extension:** Choose one feature that could be *both* change and continuity (a city, a
  craft) and write two sentences — one for each side — with reasons.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort features into changed or stayed and
  give a reason, and name one change and one continuity for each civilization?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I explain *why* each feature is change or
  continuity, and did I find at least one of each?"

## Home connection

Name one thing in your home or neighborhood that has changed in your lifetime and one
thing that has stayed the same — and ask a grown-up which they think changed the most.

## Resources

- The idea of change and continuity as two lenses on the past is a standard concept in
  historical study; it is the foundation of the D08.S1 strand (Time, Change & Continuity).
  Archaeological evidence for the specific features — the Nile's annual flood and
  irrigation (British Museum, Egypt collection) and the Indus cities' grid streets and
  drainage (UNESCO, Mohenjo-daro, <https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/138/>) — supports the
  changed/stayed sorting. The Indus script remains undeciphered, which is itself an
  example of continuity (a lasting record we cannot yet read).
