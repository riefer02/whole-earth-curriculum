# Lesson 1 — Kind Feedback: Giving and Receiving

## Summary

Learners open the unit by learning to **give and receive feedback kindly**. They meet a
contrast of **kind, specific feedback about the work** and **hurtful feedback about the
person**, practice a **two-part starter** ("One thing I liked was ___. One thing that could
be even better is ___.") plus a **receiving reply** ("Thank you — I'll think about that"),
and exchange one real, kind comment with a partner on a small shared thing.

## Objectives

- Give and receive feedback kindly, and understand that kind, specific feedback helps a
  person improve shared work. _(D02.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Two people make one sign together. One says, "That's sloppy." The other says, "This part
is clear — could we label the second step?" The first stings and teaches nothing; the
second is a gift. Good feedback is like a friend showing you the one stone to step on
next: it is **about the work, not the person**, and it believes you can improve. And
receiving it well — saying "thank you, I'll think about that" instead of flinching — is
what lets the gift land.

## Materials

- Kind feedback poster
- Feedback sentence-starter card
- A small shared-work sample

## Preparation

- Print or draw the kind feedback poster (kind vs. hurtful comment, the hurtful one
  crossed out).
- Copy a feedback sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Have each pair make or bring one small shared thing — a drawing, a two-line story, a
  paper sign — to practice on.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **feedback about the
work, not the person** — kind, specific, and usable — and that **receiving is a skill
too**. Model the contrast vividly and stop any comment about the person the moment it
appears, redirecting to the work. Feedback is among the strongest influences on learning
(Hattie, *Visible Learning*), but only when it is clear and kind. Honor every learner's
pace: feedback is about a next step, never about ranking. Because giving and receiving
feedback is a skill, model it explicitly and do guided practice before independent work
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time someone told you something about your work that
   helped you — or a time someone's words stung. Today you learn to tell the truth about
   shared work **kindly**, and to **receive** it without feeling attacked.
2. **Meet kind feedback (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two comments on the same sign.
   *Hurtful:* "That's sloppy." Crossed out — it names the *person*, not the *work*, and
   helps nothing. *Kind:* "This part is clear — could we label the second step?" It is
   **specific** (names one thing), **kind** (believes you can grow), and **about the
   work**. That is feedback that helps.
3. **Learn the starters (5 min).** Read the two-part starter: "**One thing I liked was**
   ___ . **One thing that could be even better is** ___ ." And the receiving reply:
   "**Thank you — I'll think about that.**" Practice both once with a partner.
4. **Give and receive (15 min).** Look at your pair's small shared thing. One partner
   gives one kind, specific comment using the starter. The other partner **receives it**
   with the thank-you reply — then actually thinks about it. Swap. Each of you gives and
   receives once.
5. **Close (5 min).** A kind, specific word can help someone improve faster than hours of
   silent work — and a graceful "thank you" keeps the door open for more help. You can be
   that help for someone, and you can ask for it for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the starter printed large with blanks, and model one full
  give-and-receive exchange before pairs begin; allow a comment about one single, visible
  thing.
- **Extension:** Distinguish feedback about the *work* from feedback about the *person*,
  and rewrite two hurtful comments into kind, specific ones.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Does the learner give a comment that is kind,
  specific, and about the work, and can they receive a comment with a thank-you instead of
  a flinch?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Was my comment about the work, not the person, and
  would I welcome hearing it myself?"

## Home connection

Give one kind, specific piece of feedback to someone at home today — and practice
receiving one with a simple "thank you."
