# Lesson 5 — A Day in Someone Else's Life

## Summary

Learners practice **perspective-taking** by stepping into a child's daily life in a
different culture or place. They read four respectful day cards — a high Andes farming
village, a desert oasis town, an Arctic coastal community, and a Pacific island village —
and describe one child's day, comparing **what is the same and what is different** from
their own, always treating one child as one child, never as "all people."

## Objectives

- Take the perspective of a person from a different culture or place and describe their
  daily life, comparing it to my own without stereotyping. _(D02.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

Everyone on Earth does a version of the same day: wake, eat, learn or work, play, rest,
sleep. But the details are a hundred different worlds — one child helps carry water before
school, another herds goats, another walks through a snowy village to a lamp-lit classroom.
Seeing someone's day **from the inside** — what they see when they wake, who they eat
with, what they look forward to — is how we meet people unlike ourselves with curiosity
instead of guesses.

## Materials

- A day in the life cards
- My day / their day page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the four day cards (Andes village, desert oasis, Arctic coast, Pacific island),
  each naming one child and their ordinary day.
- Copy a my-day/their-day page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for reading and sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **perspective-taking
without stereotyping**. The highest risk here is that one child's story becomes "that's how
*those people* live." Guard against it explicitly: each card describes *one child in one
place* — there is no single "Andean child" or "desert child"; even within one village,
lives differ by family, work, and means. Model the same-and-different comparison as wonder,
not ranking ("in this place, water is carried; in mine, it comes from a tap — neither is
better"). If a learner offers their own life, receive it as the authority they hold on it.
See `docs/facilitation.md` on honoring cultural contexts without ranking.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned to keep your team's promises. Today the view
   turns outward: you will walk a day in **someone else's life**, in a different place and
   culture.
2. **Meet one day (10 min).** Read the four day cards. Each names **one child** and their
   ordinary day — a child who helps tend potatoes on a high Andes terrace, a child who
   draws water at a desert oasis, a child who helps with a fishing boat on an Arctic
   coast, a child who helps gather breadfruit on a Pacific island. Notice: each is *one
   person*, not "all people" of a place.
3. **Walk the day (10 min).** Choose one card. Go through the child's day slowly: what do
   they see when they wake? What do they do? Who do they eat with? What do they look
   forward to? Now do the same for **your own** day.
4. **Same and different (15 min).** On your page, list what is the **same** (both wake,
   both eat, both learn or help, both play) and what is **different** (the work, the food,
   the place, the weather). Share one comparison with a partner. Did anyone say "that's
   weird"? Reframe it gently: it is not weird — it is **different, and it makes sense in
   its place**.
5. **Close (5 min).** Stepping into someone's day is how we understand people we have
   never met. Every life has a rhythm; the details differ. Meeting those differences with
   curiosity — not judgment — is the beginning of belonging that reaches across the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer illustrated picture cues for each part of the day, and scribe the
  same–different list.
- **Extension:** Add a third place's day from memory or research, and name one thing you
  would still want to check with the person before assuming it is true of them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe one child's daily life in a
  different place and compare it to their own without ranking or stereotyping?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I describe *one* child's day, or did I slip into
  saying 'they all'? And did I treat the difference with curiosity, not judgment?"

## Home connection

Ask a family member or neighbor about a day in a place they have lived or know well, and
retell it in the same-and-different way.

## Resources

  first domesticated in the Andes and thousands of native varieties are still farmed there
  (International Potato Center); people have adapted to desert life for thousands of years
  around wells and oases (National Geographic Education); and Inuit communities across the
  Arctic have long-lived ways of hunting, fishing, and sheltering (Inuit Circumpolar
  Council). For classroom SEL framing of social awareness, see CASEL:
