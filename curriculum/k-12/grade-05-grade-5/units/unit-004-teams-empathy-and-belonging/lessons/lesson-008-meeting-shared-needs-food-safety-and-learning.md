# Lesson 8 — Meeting Shared Needs: Food, Safety, and Learning

## Summary

Learners investigate how a community meets three **shared needs — food, safety, and
learning**. They meet a needs frame, brainstorm who feeds, protects, and teaches their own
community, and then investigate their home or neighborhood to answer **how each need is
met**, producing a record of the community's shared work.

## Objectives

- Investigate how people in a community meet shared needs such as food, safety, and
  learning. _(D02.S3.05.02)_

## Connection

Think about one ordinary day. Someone grew or made the food you ate, someone kept the
street or home safe, and someone taught you a thing you now know. A community is, in part,
a **system for meeting shared needs**: food, safety, and learning are not solved by one
person alone — they are solved by many hands, from the farmer and the shopkeeper to the
neighbor who looks out and the elder or teacher who passes on what they know.

## Materials

- Community-needs poster
- Investigate-a-need page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the community-needs poster (three columns: food, safety, learning).
- Copy an investigate-a-need page for each learner.
- Identify a few local examples of food, safety, and learning places to prime the
  discussion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **communities are
systems of shared care**: food, safety, and learning are met collectively, not alone. Keep
it concrete and local first — the learner's own neighborhood — before broadening. Frame
the investigation as wonder, not a checklist to be graded. Be sensitive: some learners
experience hunger or unsafe conditions; keep the discussion about *how communities meet
needs* (the system), never about a family's lack, and always connect to agency and hope —
something learners can contribute. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficulty with
hope.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you saw how shared work, symbols, and stories build a
   community. Today you look closer: how does a community meet its **shared needs**?
2. **Meet the three needs (10 min).** Look at the poster. Every community must meet three
   needs. **Food:** who grows, catches, makes, or sells it? **Safety:** who looks out,
   what rules and helpers keep people safe? **Learning:** who teaches, and where do people
   learn — schools, elders, workshops, libraries?
3. **Brainstorm together (10 min).** For your own community, name one real answer for
   each need. Food: a market, a garden, a shop. Safety: a neighbor who looks out, a
   crossing guard, rules everyone follows. Learning: a school, an elder, a library, a
   place where a skill is taught.
4. **Investigate one need (15 min).** Choose one need and go deeper. On your page, answer:
   *How does my community meet this need? Who does it, and where?* Write or draw what you
   find. If you can, notice it with your own eyes — a walk to a shop, a look at a garden,
   a question to an adult.
5. **Close (5 min).** A community meets its needs through **many hands working together**.
   Today you saw the system behind your own ordinary day — and you saw that you are part
   of it, too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide an illustrated "who does this?" sorting activity and scribe the
  one-need answer.
- **Extension:** Compare how two communities — your own and one from a Lesson 5 day card —
  meet the same three needs differently, and note why the difference makes sense.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner name how their community meets
  each shared need — food, safety, learning — and describe who does it?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I find a real answer for how my community meets
  the need, and can I name who makes it happen?"

## Home connection

At home, trace one shared need back to the people who meet it — ask who grew or made the
food, who keeps the home safe, and who taught a family skill.

## Resources

- Meeting shared needs is framed by the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, which
  names rights to food and health (Art. 24, 27), safety (Art. 19), and education (Arts.
  28–29) — held as a shared agreement to work toward, not a claim that every child already
  has them honored. On food systems, see the FAO's food-and-agriculture work
  (<https://www.fao.org/>); on water as a shared need, see WHO's drinking-water guidance.
