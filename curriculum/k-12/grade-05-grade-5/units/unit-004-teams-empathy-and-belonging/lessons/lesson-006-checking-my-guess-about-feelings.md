# Lesson 6 — Checking My Guess About Feelings

## Summary

Learners learn that a **first guess about someone's feelings can be wrong**, and how to
**check instead of assume**. They meet the idea that what we see on the outside may not
match what is felt inside, practice the two-step move — **notice clues, then check with
the person** — and rehearse a checking question on everyday scenarios where looks can
mislead.

## Objectives

- Recognize when a first guess about someone's feelings might be wrong, and check with
  them instead of assuming. _(D02.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

Your friend slams a book down and you think, "She's angry at me." Then she says, "Sorry —
I didn't sleep and my head hurts." Your first guess was wrong. Faces and actions are only
**clues**, not proof — and people hide feelings, or have more than one at once, or show
them differently than you would. So the kindest move is to hold your guess lightly and
**check**: "I thought you might be upset — how are you really doing?"

## Materials

- Guess–check poster
- Scenario cards
- Checking sentence-starter card

## Preparation

- Print or draw the guess–check poster (a face that looks one way while the inside is
  another, beside the two-step move).
- Copy the scenario cards and a checking sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Arrange pairs for acting out scenarios.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **epistemic humility
about others' feelings**: my guess is a guess, and the person is the authority on their own
inner world — so I check. This is the anti-stereotyping lesson of the unit. Watch for two
slips: assuming an expression always means one feeling, and assuming a *group* shares one
feeling. Model checking as gentle and optional — never pressure someone to disclose; a
person may say "I don't want to talk about it," and that is an answer to respect. Hold the
question "how are you really doing?" as an invitation, not an interrogation. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding feelings safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you guessed how someone felt and later found out
   you were wrong. Today you learn to **check your guess** instead of trusting it blindly.
2. **Meet the guess and the check (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the outside: a frown
   and crossed arms — you guess *angry*. On the inside, the thought bubble: "I didn't
   sleep and my head hurts." The outside is a **clue**, not the whole truth. So we do two
   steps: **notice the clues**, then **check with the person**.
3. **Learn the checking question (5 min).** Read the starter: "**I thought you might feel
   ___ , but I want to check — how are you really doing?**" Practice saying it once with a
   partner, gently.
4. **Act out and check (15 min).** Take a scenario card: a quiet child at a party, a
   frowning child with a headache, a child smiling while worried about a test. Partner A
   acts it; Partner B names their **guess**, then uses the checking question. Partner A
   answers honestly. Swap. Notice: was your guess right? Sometimes — but often not, and
   that is the point.
5. **Close (5 min).** Guessing someone's feelings is natural; trusting the guess without
   checking is where we go wrong. A single kind question — "how are you really doing?" —
   turns a guess into understanding. That is empathy that checks.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two illustrated clue cards and a scripted checking exchange to
  rehearse; scribe the checking question if needed.
- **Extension:** Explain *why* a guess can be wrong — feelings are hidden, mixed, or shown
  differently by different people — and write one rule you will follow before assuming.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/self):** Can the learner name their guess, then check with the person
  using a gentle question, and accept the real answer?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I hold my guess lightly, ask instead of assume,
  and accept the answer I was given?"

## Home connection

Before you assume how someone at home feels today, check — ask them how they are really
doing, and listen.
