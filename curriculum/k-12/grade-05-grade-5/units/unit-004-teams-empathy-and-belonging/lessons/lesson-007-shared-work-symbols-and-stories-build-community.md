# Lesson 7 — Shared Work, Symbols, and Stories Build Community

## Summary

Learners explore how communities are **built and held together** through **shared work,
symbols, and stories**. They meet a **three-builders** frame, consider real examples from
around the world — a harvest, a patterned cloth, an origin story — and then describe one
community they belong to through its shared work, a symbol, and a story, producing a
**portfolio artifact**.

## Objectives

- Explain how communities are built through shared work, symbols, and stories, using an
  example I know. _(D02.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

A group of strangers becomes a "we" when they build something together — a garden, a
festival, a home — when they share a **symbol** that says "this is us" (a crest, a flag, a
pattern), and when they tell the same **stories** (how we began, who we remember, what we
value). Think of your own class, family, or neighborhood: what do you do together, what
mark or pattern stands for you, and what story gets told again and again? Those three
things are the glue.

## Materials

- Three-builders poster
- My community builder page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-builders poster (shared work, symbols, stories).
- Copy a my-community-builder page for each learner.
- Gather one or two local examples of a symbol or story to prime the discussion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **belonging is
built, not automatic** — it is made through shared work, shared symbols, and shared
stories. Ground it in concrete examples the learners already know before the abstraction.
Honor every community's symbols and stories as meaningful in their own place; never rank
one culture's symbol or story above another. If a learner shares a symbol or story from
their own culture, let them be the authority on its meaning. This lesson produces the
unit's **community-building description** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned to check your guess about feelings. Today you
   ask a bigger question: what turns a group of people into a **community** — a real
   "we"?
2. **Meet the three builders (10 min).** Look at the poster. Communities are built three
   ways. **Shared work:** people plant, build, or celebrate together. **Symbols:** a
   patterned cloth whose colors carry meaning, a crest, a flag, a mark that says "this is
   us." **Stories:** the tales and songs people tell again and again to remember who they
   are. Each one makes belonging.
3. **See it in the world (10 min).** Think of examples. A village that harvests together.
   A patterned cloth — like the kente cloth of the Akan peoples of West Africa — whose
   patterns carry meaning and are worn on important days. An origin story told by elders
   around a fire. Name one you know or have heard of.
4. **Describe your own community (15 min).** Choose one community you belong to — your
   class, family, neighborhood, team, or a community of faith or culture. On your page,
   write or draw **one shared work, one symbol, and one story** of that community, and one
   sentence about how each builds belonging. This page becomes part of your portfolio.
5. **Close (5 min).** A community is not just people in one place — it is people who
   **work together, share a sign, and remember a story**. When you name those three
   things, you see the invisible glue that holds your own "we" together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide illustrated examples to sort into the three boxes, and scribe the
  one-sentence explanations.
- **Extension:** Compare two communities' symbols or stories and explain how each one says
  "this is who we are."

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/self):** Does the learner name one shared work, one symbol, and
  one story of a community, and explain how each builds belonging?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their page and checks that all three builders are
  present and each is tied to belonging.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a symbol or story of a community they belong to, and tell them
one of yours.

## Resources

- Belonging and inclusion are a pillar of UNESCO's "Learning to Live Together"
  (<https://en.unesco.org/themes/learning-live-together>); shared activity and trust build
  community (Putnam, *Bowling Alone*). For symbols with meaning, see kente cloth of the
  Akan peoples (Smithsonian National Museum of African Art) and, for story and knowledge
  passed person to person, UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage (<https://ich.unesco.org/>).
