# Lesson 9 — Mutual Aid Across Cultures and Times

## Summary

Learners meet **mutual aid** — people helping one another **not for pay but because they
belong to each other**. They read six case cards from different cultures and times — a
barn-raising, **harambee** in Kenya, **gotong royong** in Indonesia, Inuit food-sharing,
Andean communal farming, and Wangari Maathai's tree-planting movement — record one example
using a **who–what–why** frame, and connect it to helping they have seen or joined,
producing a **portfolio artifact**.

## Objectives

- Describe examples of mutual aid from different cultures and times, and explain why
  people help one another. _(D02.S4.05.01)_

## Connection

When a family moves to a new home, neighbors sometimes come to carry boxes — no one is
paid, and everyone is fed at the end. When a village digs a well or brings in a harvest,
many hands show up. This is **mutual aid**: helping because we belong to each other, not
because we are hired. People all over the world, in every time, have built this habit —
and it is one of the oldest and most human things we do.

## Materials

- Mutual-aid gallery cards
- My mutual-aid example page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the six mutual-aid gallery cards (barn-raising; harambee; gotong royong; Inuit
  food-sharing; Andean communal farming; the Green Belt Movement).
- Copy a my-mutual-aid-example page for each learner.
- Arrange small groups or a circle for reading and sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **mutual aid as a
universal human practice, not a charity or a "quaint" custom**: people help because they
belong to each other, in every culture and time — none is more "advanced" than another.
Present each example with the same dignity; guard against ranking ("that is old-fashioned"
or "that is exotic"). Distinguish the *fact* (these traditions exist and are documented)
from the *value* (helping one another is good). If a learner shares an example from their
own culture, receive it warmly and let them be its authority. This lesson produces the
unit's **mutual-aid example** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you saw how communities meet shared needs. Today you
   meet one of the oldest human practices: **mutual aid** — helping one another not for
   pay, but because we belong to each other.
2. **Meet the gallery (15 min).** Read the six case cards. *A barn-raising:* neighbors
   gather to raise a family's barn in a day, and the favor returns when another family
   builds. *Harambee:* in Kenya, people pull together — "all pull together" — to build a
   school or help a family. *Gotong royong:* in Indonesia, neighbors share the work of a
   village — a road, a harvest, a celebration. *Inuit food-sharing:* a successful hunt is
   shared widely, so no one goes without. *Andean communal farming:* neighbors farm
   terraces together, in a *minga*, for the good of all. *The Green Belt Movement:* begun
   by Wangari Maathai in 1977, people planted trees together — over thirty million — to
   heal the land.
3. **Find the who–what–why (10 min).** Choose one example. Answer three questions: **Who**
   helped? **What** did they do? **Why** did it matter — and why did they do it without
   pay? The answer to "why" is almost always: because they belonged to each other.
4. **Record your example (10 min).** On your page, write or draw the example you chose,
   plus one act of mutual aid **you have seen or joined** — neighbors helping, a harvest
   shared, a meal carried to someone sick. This page becomes part of your portfolio.
5. **Close (5 min).** Mutual aid is not one culture's idea — it is a human habit that
   appears everywhere and always. When you help because you belong, you are joining the
   oldest team on Earth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two illustrated case cards and a fill-in who–what–why frame, and
  scribe the local example.
- **Extension:** Compare two examples across time or place and explain what they share —
  help without pay, from belonging — and what differs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/self):** Can the learner describe one mutual-aid example (who,
  what, why) and name one they have seen or joined?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I capture who helped, what they did, and why —
  and did I hold every example with the same respect?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time people helped without pay — a harvest, a move, a
neighbor in need — and add it to your page.

## Resources

- The concept of mutual aid as a widespread human practice is named in Kropotkin's
  *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902). Specific examples: the Green Belt Movement,
  begun by Wangari Maathai in 1977, planted over thirty million trees (Nobel Prize facts,
  <https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2004/maathai/facts/>); Andean communal farming
  and the potato's Andean origin (International Potato Center); Inuit food-sharing and
  community life (Inuit Circumpolar Council); the 1976 Hōkūleʻa voyage as a whole
  community's shared work (Polynesian Voyaging Society). Harambee, gotong royong, and
  barn-raising are widely documented traditions of communal help; verify local details
  with community members.
