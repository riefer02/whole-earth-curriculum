# Lesson 1 — Paraphrase a Speaker's Points

## Summary

Learners learn to **paraphrase**: to restate a speaker's points in their **own words** while
keeping the **meaning** the same. They meet a worked example, practice a "**listen → hold
the idea → say it back**" routine with sentence starters, and check their paraphrase
against the original with a partner. This is the first of two skills for understanding a
speaker; asking clarifying questions follows in the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Paraphrase a speaker's points accurately. _(D04.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

A neighbor gives you directions: "Walk to the mango tree, turn left at the blue gate, and
it's the second door." If you only nod, you might get lost. But if you say it back — "So I
go to the mango tree, turn left at the blue gate, then take the second door?" — the
neighbor can catch a mistake before you walk. Paraphrasing is that "say it back": putting
someone's words into **your own words** while keeping the **meaning** the same, whether the
directions lead through a busy city street or across a mountain field.

## Materials

- Paraphrase poster
- Paraphrase sentence starters
- A short spoken message to practice on

## Preparation

- Print or draw the paraphrase poster (original words → paraphrase, arrow labeled "same
  meaning").
- Copy a paraphrase sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Have ready a few short messages (2–3 sentences) learners can use: directions, a piece of
  news, a classroom reminder.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that a **paraphrase keeps
the meaning and changes only the words** — it is not a shorter version (that is summarizing)
and not a repeat (that is quoting). Model the worked example aloud and slow it down: listen,
hold the idea, then say it back. Because paraphrasing is a skill, use explicit instruction
and guided practice before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Honor every
home language: learners may paraphrase into their strongest language, which is itself a
valuable act of understanding. Stop any correction that shames a learner's word choice; the
test is *meaning*, not grammar.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you repeated someone's words and they smiled and said
   "yes, exactly" — or a time you got it wrong and had to be corrected. Today you learn to
   **paraphrase**: to say a speaker's points back in **your own words** without changing the
   **meaning**.
2. **Meet paraphrasing (10 min).** Look at the poster. A speaker says, "Walk to the mango
   tree, turn left at the blue gate, and it's the second door." A listener paraphrases: "So
   I go to the mango tree, turn left at the blue gate, then take the second door." The
   words changed; the meaning stayed the same. That is a paraphrase. A paraphrase is **not**
   a shorter version, and **not** word-for-word — it is the same idea in your own words.
3. **Learn the starters (5 min).** Read the starter card: "**So what you're saying is** ___."
   "**In other words,** ___." "**If I understand right,** ___." These are doors into your own
   words. Practice saying one starter once, aloud.
4. **Listen and say it back (15 min).** With a partner, one of you says a short message
   aloud — directions, news, or a reminder. The other **paraphrases** using a starter, in
   your own words. Then check together: did the meaning stay the same? If not, try again.
   Swap roles so each of you paraphrases twice.
5. **Close (5 min).** A good listener is not just quiet — they say back what they heard so
   both people know it landed. Paraphrasing is how you make sure you *understood*, not just
   *heard*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the message in short, simple sentences and a printed word bank of
  synonyms; allow a one-sentence paraphrase first.
- **Extension:** Paraphrase a longer or more technical message and circle the exact words
  you changed, explaining why you kept the meaning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Can the learner restate a short spoken message in their
  own words so the meaning stays the same, and catch a changed meaning with a partner?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If the speaker heard my version, would they say 'yes,
  that's what I meant'?"

## Home connection

Listen to a family member's news or directions today, say it back in your own words, and ask
them if you got it right.
