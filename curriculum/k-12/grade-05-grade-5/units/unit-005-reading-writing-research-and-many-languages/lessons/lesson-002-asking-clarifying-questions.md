# Lesson 2 — Asking Clarifying Questions

## Summary

Learners learn to ask **clarifying questions** when a speaker's meaning is unclear. They
meet a worked example, learn a set of **question starters** ("Did you mean ___?", "What do
you mean by ___?"), and practice on deliberately unclear messages until the meaning becomes
clear. This completes the pair of listening skills begun in the paraphrase lesson.

## Objectives

- Ask clarifying questions when unsure of a speaker's meaning. _(D04.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Someone says, "Bring the *thing* we use for the water." Which thing? A bucket, a gourd, a
cup, a pump? Guessing could waste a trip; asking — "Do you mean the bucket, or the gourd?" —
saves it. A **clarifying question** is the question you ask when you are **not sure** what a
speaker means. It is not rude and it is not a sign of being slow; it is how careful people
make sure they *understand* before they act, in a kitchen, a workshop, or a classroom.

## Materials

- Clarifying-question poster
- Clarifying-question starter card
- An unclear message to practice on

## Preparation

- Print or draw the clarifying-question poster (a puzzled listener asking a clarifying
  question beside a speaker, with starters listed).
- Copy a starter card for each pair.
- Prepare a few deliberately unclear messages (a direction missing a detail, an instruction
  with an unknown word).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **asking for clarity
is a strength, not a weakness** — it prevents errors and shows respect for the speaker's
meaning. Some classrooms or homes teach children to stay silent rather than question; gently
reframe asking as *wondering together*, and offer the "wonder box" for anonymous questions
where speaking up feels unsafe (see `docs/contexts.md`). Distinguish a clarifying question
(it stays on the speaker's meaning) from a new-topic question (it moves elsewhere). Model
the starters and do guided practice before independent work.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned to paraphrase — to say a speaker's points back
   in your own words. But sometimes you *can't* paraphrase, because you are not sure what the
   speaker meant. Today you learn the second listener's tool: the **clarifying question**.
2. **Meet the clarifying question (10 min).** Look at the poster. A speaker says, "Meet me at
   the tall tree at noon." A listener is puzzled: which tall tree? The listener asks, "**Which
   tall tree — the one by the well, or the one by the road?**" That is a clarifying question:
   it asks about the *speaker's meaning*, not a new topic. The speaker answers, and now the
   message is clear.
3. **Learn the starters (5 min).** Read the starter card: "**Did you mean** ___?", "**Could
   you say more about** ___?", "**What do you mean by** ___?", "**Which** ___ do you mean?"
   Practice saying one aloud.
4. **Ask until it's clear (15 min).** With a partner, one of you says a deliberately unclear
   message. The other **asks a clarifying question** using a starter. Keep asking — one, two,
   or three questions — until the meaning is truly clear. Then check: did your questions stay
   on the speaker's meaning? Swap roles.
5. **Close (5 min).** Asking "what do you mean by ___?" is not admitting failure — it is
   building a bridge to understanding. The best listeners ask, and the best speakers are glad
   they did.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a two-choice clarifying question ("Do you mean A or B?") as a scaffold,
  and praise any question that stays on the meaning.
- **Extension:** Sort a mixed list of questions into "clarifies the message" and "changes the
  subject," and rewrite the off-topic ones as clarifying questions.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Does the learner ask a question that stays on the
  speaker's meaning and results in clearer understanding?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did my question help me understand *this* speaker's
  meaning — not just make conversation?"

## Home connection

The next time an instruction at home is unclear, ask one clarifying question instead of
guessing — and notice how much smoother the task goes.
