# Lesson 9 — Write for a Purpose and Audience

## Summary

Learners learn to **write for a purpose and an audience**, choosing an appropriate **style**
and **structure**. They meet the idea that the same idea takes different forms for different
readers, choose a real writing task (purpose + audience), draft it, and check that each choice
— words, order, and form — fits the reader.

## Objectives

- Write for different purposes and audiences, choosing an appropriate style and structure.
  _(D04.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

You want people to come to a clean-up day. For the whole village you might write a big, bold
**poster** with short, cheering words. For your younger cousin you might write a warm **note**
with simpler words and a "can you come?" at the end. Same idea, two readers, two very
different pieces. Writing well is not finding one "right" way — it is **choosing** words, order,
and form to fit **who will read it** and **what you want them to do**.

## Materials

- Purpose-and-audience chooser poster
- Writing task cards
- Blank writing paper

## Preparation

- Print or draw the purpose-and-audience poster (invitation / instructions / request side by
  side).
- Copy writing task cards for each group.
- Provide blank drafting paper with a line for "purpose" and "audience" at the top.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **style and structure
are choices**, not rules, and the right choice depends on purpose and audience. Writers may
write in their home language; the goal is a piece that *works for its reader*. Model one "same
idea, two audiences" contrast aloud, then let learners choose tasks they find real and
meaningful. Keep feedback about whether the piece fits the reader, not about "correctness."
Writing is a skill — pair the choice-making with a worked example before independent drafting
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one thing you want to tell people — a game you know, a place
   you love, a task that needs doing. Today you learn that *how* you write it depends on *who*
   reads it and *what you want*.
2. **Meet the chooser (10 min).** Look at the poster. One idea — a clean-up day — becomes a
   bold **poster** for the whole village and a warm **note** for a younger cousin. Your
   **purpose** (to invite, to teach, to request) and your **audience** (who reads it) together
   shape your **style** (your words and tone) and your **structure** (your order and form).
3. **Choose a task (10 min).** Pick a writing task card, or invent your own: a purpose and an
   audience. Write them at the top of your page: "I am writing to ___ for ___."
4. **Draft it (15 min).** Draft your piece so it fits your reader. Ask yourself as you go: are
   these the words this reader would understand? Is this the form they would notice — a poster,
   a note, a list, a letter? Does it do what I want it to do?
5. **Check and share (10 min).** Read your draft to a partner who *pretends to be* your
   audience. Do they understand it? Would they act? Adjust if needed.
6. **Close (5 min).** You have just done what writers everywhere do: matched the words to the
   reader. Next lesson you will take one idea and write it a *second* way.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter sentence and a choice of two audiences; allow drawing with
  labels as the draft.
- **Extension:** Write the same idea for three different audiences and annotate exactly what
  changed — words, order, and form — and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/portfolio):** Does the piece fit its stated purpose and audience in word
  choice, order, and form?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If my audience read this, would it make sense to them and
  move them the way I intended?"

## Home connection

Write a real note for someone at home — a reminder, a request, or an invitation — choosing
words that fit them, and see how they respond.

## Resources

- Writing for authentic purposes and audiences supports engagement and transfer (see Graham &
  Perin, "Writing Next," Carnegie Corporation, 2007). On explicit instruction for novice
  writers, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
