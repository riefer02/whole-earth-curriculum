# Lesson 10 — One Idea, Two Forms

## Summary

Learners adapt one idea into a **second written form** for a different audience, applying the
purpose-and-audience thinking from Lesson 9. They take the piece they drafted, rewrite it for a
new reader, and compare the two versions to name exactly what changed — words, order, and form
— and why.

## Objectives

- Write for different purposes and audiences, choosing an appropriate style and structure.
  _(D04.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

A message you'd whisper to a friend becomes different when you say it to a visiting elder, and
different again when you post it for the whole community. The idea stays; the **form** bends.
Being able to **adapt** one idea for many readers is a superpower — it is how a farmer explains
a planting plan to a child, a neighbor, and an official, and how a single good idea reaches
everyone it needs to.

## Materials

- One idea, two forms poster
- The learner's piece from Lesson 9
- New audience task cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the one idea, two forms poster (same idea in two forms, changes circled).
- Have learners bring their drafted piece from Lesson 9.
- Copy new-audience task cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **adapting form to
audience is a real, useful skill**, not a busywork rewrite. Model the before/after contrast and
circle the changes (simpler words, different greeting, different order, different form). Honor
home languages: a learner may adapt *into* a second language or *between* two languages, which
is advanced and valuable. Keep the focus on *fit for the reader*, not on grammar errors. Use a
worked example first, then let learners revise their own work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you wrote one piece for one reader. Today you take that same
   idea and write it a **second way**, for a **different reader** — and you learn to *notice*
   what changes.
2. **Meet two forms (10 min).** Look at the poster. One idea appears twice: a friendly
   step-by-step **note** for a friend, and a polite **letter** for a visiting elder. The changes
   are circled: simpler words, a different greeting, a different order, a different form. The
   idea stayed; the form bent to fit the reader.
3. **Choose a new audience (10 min).** Look at your piece from last lesson. Pick a **new
   audience** from the cards — a younger child, an elder, a visitor, the whole community. Say
   your idea once to yourself, *as if* speaking to them.
4. **Rewrite for the new reader (15 min).** Write your piece again, for the new audience. Change
   what needs to change: words, tone, order, form. Keep the idea the same.
5. **Compare the two (10 min).** Put your two versions side by side. List the **three biggest
   changes** and say *why* each one fits the new reader. Share with a partner.
6. **Close (5 min).** A writer who can change form for a new reader can carry one idea to many
   people — and that is how ideas travel.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a side-by-side frame with the original copied in one column and prompts
  ("change the greeting, choose simpler words") in the other.
- **Extension:** Produce a third form — a spoken announcement or a picture poster — and compare
  all three, explaining which audience each serves best.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/portfolio):** Do the two versions keep the same idea while changing words,
  order, or form to fit each reader?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I name the three biggest changes and explain why each
  fits the new reader?"

## Home connection

Take one idea you've been meaning to share and tell it to two different people at home,
adjusting your words each time — then notice which version landed better and why.
