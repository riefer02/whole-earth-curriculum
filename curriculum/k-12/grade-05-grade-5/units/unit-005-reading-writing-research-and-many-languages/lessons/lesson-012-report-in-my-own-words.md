# Lesson 12 — Writing My Report in My Own Words

## Summary

Learners turn their research notes into a **short report written in their own words**. They
meet the rule that copying a source word-for-word without credit is **plagiarism**, practice
restating a fact in their own words, and write a short report with a simple **sources line**
that gives credit.

## Objectives

- Write a short report in their own words, gathering information from several sources and noting
  key facts. _(D04.S3.05.02)_

## Connection

A friend finds a fact and reads it to you word-for-word from a book. That's fine — if they say
*where* it came from. But if they hand in the book's exact sentences as if the words were their
own, something is wrong: that is **plagiarism**, taking someone else's words without credit.
The honest way is to learn the fact, then say it **in your own words** and name your source:
"I learned from the gardener and the tree book that tamarind trees can live more than a
century." Own words plus credit — that is a report you can be proud of.

## Materials

- Own-words poster
- Source notes from Lesson 11
- Report frame

## Preparation

- Print or draw the own-words poster (copying crossed out vs. own words + credit).
- Have learners bring their note cards from Lesson 11.
- Copy the report frame.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The two ideas to land are **own words** and
**credit** — and that copying without credit is plagiarism, which is about honesty and fairness
to the person who did the work, not just a school rule. Frame it through the ethics lens: we
give credit because we value people's work. Model restating one note in own words (a novice
skill — use a worked example and guided practice; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Honor
home languages: writing the report in a home language is welcome, and paraphrasing into a
second language is advanced, valuable work. Keep the credit format simple and age-appropriate.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you gathered key facts from several sources. Today you turn
   those notes into a **report** — but there is one important rule: the words must be **your
   own**, and you must **give credit**.
2. **Meet own words (10 min).** Look at the poster. A source says: "The tamarind tree can live
   over a hundred years." **Copying** it word-for-word and calling it yours is **plagiarism** —
   crossed out. **Own words:** "Tamarind trees can grow for more than a century." Same fact,
   your words. Then **credit:** "Sources: the gardener, the tree book." That is the honest
   three-step: learn the fact, say it your way, name your source.
3. **Practice restating (10 min).** Take one note card. Read the key fact, then turn the card
   over and **say it in your own words** without looking. Write what you said. Check: did the
   fact stay true, but in your words? This is the skill from Lesson 1, now on the page.
4. **Write your report (15 min).** Using the report frame, write your short report: an opening
   sentence that says your topic, your key facts in **your own words**, a closing sentence, and
   a **sources** line naming where your facts came from.
5. **Check for honesty (10 min).** Read your report next to your note cards. Is every sentence
   in your own words? Did you credit your sources? Ask a partner to check too.
6. **Close (5 min).** You have done what honest writers everywhere do: gathered, understood,
   and shared — in your own voice, with credit given. That report is your capstone's raw
   material.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence bank to restate each fact, and allow a shorter report with two
  facts and one source.
- **Extension:** Add a sentence noting where two sources agree, and use two different ways to
  restate the same fact, choosing the clearer one.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/self):** Is the report written in the learner's own words with key
  facts from several sources and a credit line?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Would someone reading my report and my sources say the
  facts are true, the words are mine, and the credit is given?"

## Home connection

Share one fact from your report with someone at home — in your own words — and tell them which
source it came from.

  is a values commitment; see `docs/philosophy.md` §4–5.
