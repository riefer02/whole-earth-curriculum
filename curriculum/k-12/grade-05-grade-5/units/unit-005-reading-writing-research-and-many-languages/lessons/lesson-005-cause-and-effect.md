# Lesson 5 — Author's Structure: Cause and Effect

## Summary

Learners learn to recognize how authors use a **cause-and-effect structure** to organize
ideas. They meet **signal words** ("because," "so," "as a result"), mark causes and effects in
a short passage, and map the chain with arrows. This is the first of two lessons on an
author's text structure; problem and solution follows next.

## Objectives

- Explain how an author uses a cause-and-effect structure to organize ideas.
  _(D04.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

A puddle appears after rain; a pot boils because the fire is lit; a crop grows because the
seed got water. The world runs on **cause and effect** — one thing makes another happen.
Authors who want you to understand *why* something happens arrange their writing the same
way: first the cause, then the effect, linked with words like **because** and **so**. When you
spot that pattern, the text stops being a list of facts and becomes a chain you can follow.

## Materials

- Cause-and-effect poster
- Cause-and-effect short passage
- Cause/effect signal-word card

## Preparation

- Print or draw the cause-and-effect poster (boxes and arrows, with signal words).
- Copy a short informational passage written in cause-and-effect structure for each pair.
- Copy the signal-word card.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **structure is a tool
authors choose** — a cause-and-effect text is organized as a chain, and spotting it helps a
reader follow the reasoning. Teach the signal words explicitly (a novice skill), then let
learners hunt for the chain themselves. Choose passages that reflect learners' own biomes and
lives — flooding, drought, seed-to-plant, fishing seasons — and avoid any single region's
default. Some effects are painful (a crop lost); hold them with the calm, hopeful tone in
`docs/facilitation.md`, and always connect cause-and-effect thinking to agency: "if we change
a cause, we can change an effect."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at a small thing that happened in your day — a puddle, a full
   stomach, a finished chore. Something *made* it happen. Today you learn how **authors**
   organize writing the same way: as **cause and effect**.
2. **Meet the structure (10 min).** Look at the poster. It shows a chain: **heavy rain → river
   rises → fields flood → crops are lost**. Each box after the first is an **effect** caused by
   the one before it. Authors signal this with words like **because**, **so**, **as a result**,
   and **therefore**. Find those words and you've found the chain.
3. **Read and mark (15 min).** With a partner, read the short passage. Underline each **cause**
   once and each **effect** twice. Circle the **signal words** that connect them. Then draw the
   chain of boxes and arrows the author built.
4. **Explain the author's choice (10 min).** Answer together: why did this author choose cause
   and effect? What did the chain help you understand that a simple list of facts would not?
5. **Close (5 min).** Readers who see structure read with power — they can follow an author's
   thinking instead of just collecting words. You now hold one of the author's tools.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the passage with causes pre-underlined, and have the learner find the
  matching effects and draw the arrows.
- **Extension:** Add one missing link to the chain the author left out, and explain where it
  belongs and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner locate causes and effects in a passage and
  explain the chain the author organized?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I draw the arrows from each cause to its effect, and
  say the signal word that links them?"

## Home connection

Find one cause-and-effect chain at home tonight — the fire and the cooked food, the sun and
the dry clothes — and say it aloud with a signal word.
