# Lesson 8 — Author's Purpose and Point of View

## Summary

Learners learn to identify an author's **purpose** (to persuade, inform, or entertain) and
**point of view** (whose eyes the reader sees through). They examine short texts through a
two-part lens — "Why did the author write this?" and "Whose point of view is it, and whose
voice is missing?" — and compare two points of view on one event.

## Objectives

- Identify an author's purpose and point of view. _(D04.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

A seller at the market tells you the fruit is the finest anywhere; a sign lists the prices; a
grandmother tells a funny story about the same market. Three texts, one place, three
**purposes** — one wants to persuade you, one to inform you, one to entertain you — and each
comes from a different **point of view**. The seller sees the market one way, the sign sees
numbers, the grandmother sees a story. Reading well means asking two questions: *why did this
person write this?* and *through whose eyes am I seeing it?*

## Materials

- Purpose poster
- Point-of-view lens card
- Short texts to examine

## Preparation

- Print or draw the purpose poster (three doors: persuade / inform / entertain).
- Copy the point-of-view lens card for each pair.
- Gather a few short texts with different purposes and points of view (an ad, a fact sheet, a
  story, a letter).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land are that every text has a
**purpose** and a **point of view**, and that a thoughtful reader asks after both. The
egalitarian lens matters here: the question "whose voice is missing?" is a habit of mind that
notices who is left out — the worker, the child, the other side. Keep it gentle and curious,
not accusatory; a text that leaves a voice out is not evil, just incomplete. Where possible,
use texts from many cultures and both oral and written traditions. See `docs/facilitation.md`
on inquiry over creed.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you told facts from opinions. Today you ask about the
   *person* behind the words: **why** did the author write this, and **whose point of view** is
   it?
2. **Meet purpose (10 min).** Look at the poster. Authors usually write to do one of three
   things: **persuade** (change your mind or your action), **inform** (teach you facts), or
   **entertain** (give you a story or a laugh). The ad persuades; the fact sheet informs; the
   grandmother's story entertains.
3. **Meet point of view (10 min).** Now the lens card: "**Whose eyes do we see through?**" The
   seller sees the market as a living; the sign sees prices; the grandmother sees a memory.
   And ask the second question: "**Whose voice is missing?**" Who else is at that market that
   none of these three shows us?
4. **Examine texts (15 min).** With your group, read each short text. For each one, answer:
   what is its **purpose** (persuade, inform, entertain)? What is its **point of view**? Whose
   voice is missing?
5. **Close (5 min).** You are not a sponge that soaks up words — you are a reader who asks
   why, and through whose eyes. That question is the beginning of thinking for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use texts with a single, obvious purpose and a clearly named speaker; provide
  the three purpose labels as a visual to point to.
- **Extension:** Take one event and write two short versions from two different points of view,
  noticing what each chooses to tell and leave out.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner name a text's purpose and point of view,
  and notice whose voice is missing?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I say why this was written and through whose eyes —
  and name one voice it leaves out?"

## Home connection

Ask about a sign, ad, or story you meet today: why was it written, and whose point of view does
it show? Share your answer with someone.

## Resources

- Author's purpose and point of view are standard comprehension targets (see Duke & Pearson's
  synthesis and the IES practice guides). The question "whose voice is missing?" reflects the
  curriculum's egalitarian and global lenses (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
