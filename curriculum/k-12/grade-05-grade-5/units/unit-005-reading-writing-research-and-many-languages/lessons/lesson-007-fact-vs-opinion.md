# Lesson 7 — Fact or Opinion?

## Summary

Learners learn to distinguish **facts** (claims that can be checked) from **opinions** (what
someone thinks or feels). They meet two test questions — "**Can I check it?**" versus "**Does
someone believe it?**" — sort sentences into the two columns, and write a fact and an opinion
of their own, stating how the fact could be checked.

## Objectives

- Distinguish fact from opinion. _(D04.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

"This river floods every year" and "this river is the most beautiful in the world" are two very
different sentences. The first you can **check** — ask people, look at records, watch through a
season. The second is a matter of **taste** — someone's feeling, and another person may feel
differently. Both have their place, but they are not the same thing. Learning to tell them
apart is how you keep from being fooled — in a market, in the news, in a family argument.

## Materials

- Fact vs. opinion poster
- Fact-or-opinion sentence strips
- Two-column sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fact vs. opinion poster (two columns with the two test questions).
- Copy a set of fact-or-opinion sentence strips for each pair.
- Copy the two-column sorting page.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that the difference between
fact and opinion is *how the claim behaves* — a fact can be checked, an opinion is held. Avoid
framing opinions as "bad"; they are how we feel and choose, and they matter. The skill is
*telling which is which* and knowing what each is for. Be careful with close calls (a taste
judgment dressed up as fact, or a value stated as a fact) — those are the interesting ones to
discuss, not to rush past. This is a critical-thinking habit, not a one-day module
(`docs/philosophy.md` §7). Keep sorting non-competitive and let learners justify their choices.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of two things someone said today — one you could check, and one
   that was just how they felt. Today you learn to name that difference: **fact** and
   **opinion**.
2. **Meet the two questions (10 min).** Look at the poster. A **fact** is a claim you can
   **check** — "this river floods every year" can be checked by asking and observing. An
   **opinion** is what someone **thinks or feels** — "this river is the most beautiful in the
   world" is a feeling, not a check. Two questions sort them: **Can I check it?** (fact) and
   **Does someone believe or feel it?** (opinion).
3. **Sort the strips (15 min).** With a partner, read each sentence strip and place it in the
   "fact" or "opinion" column. For each one, say *why*: what could you check, or whose feeling
   is it? Take your time with the close ones.
4. **Write your own (10 min).** Write one **fact** and one **opinion** about the same thing — a
   food, a place, a game. Under the fact, write how you could check it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Facts tell us what is; opinions tell us what matters to someone. A
   thoughtful person keeps both — and never confuses the two.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start with obvious pairs ("It rained yesterday" vs. "Rain is wonderful") and
  provide a checking-words bank (ask, look, measure, count, record).
- **Extension:** Take a sentence that mixes both ("This cheap, delicious fruit grows here") and
  separate its fact parts from its opinion parts.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/self):** Can the learner sort sentences into fact and opinion and say how a
  fact could be checked?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "For each one, could I say exactly how to check it — or
  whose belief it is?"

## Home connection

Listen for one fact and one opinion at home or in the street today, and tell someone which is
which and why.
