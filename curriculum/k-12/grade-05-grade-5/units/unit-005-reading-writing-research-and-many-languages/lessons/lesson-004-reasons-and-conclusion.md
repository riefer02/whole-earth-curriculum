# Lesson 4 — Supporting Reasons and a Conclusion

## Summary

Learners complete their short presentation by adding **supporting reasons** and a
**conclusion** to the main idea they built in Lesson 3. Using a "**presentation sandwich**" —
main idea on top, reasons in the middle, conclusion at the bottom — they assemble and deliver
a complete short talk to a small audience.

## Objectives

- Give a short presentation with a clear main idea, supporting reasons, and a conclusion.
  _(D04.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

"You should try growing your own beans." Why? Without reasons, that sentence floats. "You
should try growing your own beans — they're cheap to plant, they don't need much space, and
they taste better fresh." Now the idea has feet. And a good speaker ends by landing it: "So
if you have a pot and a patch of sun, give beans a try." A main idea is the start; **reasons**
carry it, and a **conclusion** brings it home — whether you're talking about beans, a book, or
a football match.

## Materials

- Presentation sandwich poster
- Reason and conclusion frames
- The learner's main idea from Lesson 3

## Preparation

- Print or draw the presentation sandwich poster (main idea / reasons / conclusion).
- Copy reason-and-conclusion frames for each learner.
- Have learners bring or recall the one-sentence main idea they built last lesson.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that a presentation has a
**shape**: a clear main idea, **supporting reasons that stay on it**, and a **conclusion that
returns to it**. Model one complete sandwich aloud first. Watch for "drifting" reasons — ones
that wander to a new topic — and gently bring them back with "does that support your main
idea?" Let learners deliver to a small, friendly audience; a warm listener makes a nervous
speaker clearer. Speaking in the home language is welcome; clarity for *this* audience is the
measure.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you built your presentation's heart: one clear main idea.
   Today you give it strength — **reasons** — and a strong finish — a **conclusion**.
2. **Meet the sandwich (10 min).** Look at the poster. A presentation is like a sandwich. The
   top slice is your **main idea**. The middle is **supporting reasons** — two or three, each
   one staying on the idea. The bottom slice is your **conclusion**, which returns to the main
   idea and leaves the audience with one thing to remember. A worked example: *Main idea:*
   "Growing your own beans is worth trying." *Reason 1:* "They're cheap to plant." *Reason 2:*
   "They need little space." *Reason 3:* "They taste better fresh." *Conclusion:* "So if you
   have a pot and some sun, give beans a try."
3. **Learn the frames (5 min).** Read the frames: "**One reason is** ___." "**Another reason
   is** ___." "**So, remember:** ___." Practice each once, aloud.
4. **Build your sandwich (20 min).** Take your main idea from last lesson. Say it, then add
   **two or three supporting reasons**, each one checking itself: does this support my main
   idea? End with a **conclusion** using "So, remember: ___." Deliver the whole short
   presentation to a small audience. They answer one question: "Could you follow the idea, the
   reasons, and the ending?"
5. **Close (5 min).** You have now done what strong speakers do everywhere: one clear idea,
   reasons to believe it, and an ending that lands. Next you will look inside what you *read*
   the way you just built what you *say*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a reason bank to choose from, and allow one reason plus a simple
  ending; scribe if writing is a barrier. A learner may present while seated and may show each
  part — main idea, reasons, conclusion — by pointing, signing, or using a device instead of
  speaking aloud.
- **Extension:** Reorder reasons from strongest to weakest, and add a "so what" sentence to the
  conclusion that tells the audience why it matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** Does the presentation have a clear main idea, at least two
  on-topic reasons, and a conclusion that returns to the idea?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did each reason point at my main idea, and did my ending
  bring the audience back to it?"

## Home connection

Deliver your mini presentation to someone at home — main idea, two reasons, and a conclusion —
and ask which reason convinced them most.

## Resources

- Clear organization (main idea → support → conclusion) aids audience recall; see the

  structure. For classroom speaking-and-listening framing, see CASEL:
