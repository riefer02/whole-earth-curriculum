# Lesson 13 — Many Writing Systems

## Summary

Learners discover that **many writing systems exist** and learn to identify a few. They meet
four big types — **alphabets, abjads, abugidas, and character scripts** — through five
examples (Latin, Arabic, Devanagari, Chinese characters, and Hangul), sort script samples, and
celebrate the scripts present in their own classroom and homes.

## Objectives

- Recognize that many writing systems exist, such as alphabets and character scripts, and
  identify a few. _(D04.S4.05.01)_

## Connection

Two children write the word for "water." One writes "water," another writes "水," a third
writes "पानी," and a fourth writes "물." All four are writing, and all four are right. Human
beings have invented **many writing systems** — hundreds — and each one records language in its
own way. No script is better or more "advanced" than another; each is a beautiful, working
invention, shaped by the people who use it. When you can recognize a few, a whole world of
signs, books, and names opens up.

## Materials

- Writing-systems poster
- Script sample cards
- A "name the script" sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the writing-systems poster (Latin, Arabic, Devanagari, Chinese characters,
  Hangul, with type labels).
- Copy script sample cards for each group.
- Copy the sorting page.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **many writing systems
exist, each a valid and complete way to write**, and that a few big types can be recognized:
**alphabets** (letters for sounds, like Latin and Hangul), **abjads** (mostly consonant letters,
like Arabic), **abugidas** (a consonant base plus vowel marks, like Devanagari), and
**character scripts** (a symbol for a meaning, like Chinese). Take care to be accurate: Arabic
is written right-to-left; Devanagari's vowel marks sit around the consonant; Chinese characters
mostly stand for meanings (morphemes), not just sounds. Above all, treat every script and every
learner's home script as **equally worthy** — never rank them. Invite learners to share their
own scripts; this is a celebration, not a comparison. The technical facts here are evidence;
the claim that "no script is better" is a value we hold (`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of all the ways people you know write things down. Today you learn
   that there isn't one way to write — there are **many writing systems**, and you can learn to
   recognize a few.
2. **Meet the types (10 min).** Look at the poster. Five systems, four big ideas about how
   writing can work. **Alphabets** write sounds with letters — Latin ("A a B b") and Hangul
   ("한글," where letters are grouped into syllable blocks). **Abjads** write mostly consonants
   and let readers add the vowels — Arabic ("سلام," written right-to-left). **Abugidas** start
   with a consonant and add vowel marks around it — Devanagari ("नमस्ते"). **Character scripts**
   use a symbol for a meaning — Chinese ("水" means "water").
3. **Sort the samples (15 min).** With your group, take the script sample cards and sort them
   under the headings: **alphabet, abjad, abugida, character script**. For each one, say *why*
   you placed it there.
4. **Name what's in the room (15 min).** Which scripts do you and your classmates already know
   or use? Where you can, write your name in your home script and share it. Add any new scripts
   to your sorting page.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every script on that poster is a whole way of thinking and writing, used
   by millions of people. Knowing a few — and honoring them all — is part of being a citizen of
   a many-voiced world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start with two systems (the learner's own script and one new one), and use the
  "one symbol per sound / syllable / meaning" hand signals. Offer the scripts as large cards
  and describe each aloud — its shape, direction, and how it encodes sound — so learners with
  low vision can follow by listening and tracing.
- **Extension:** Research a sixth script — Cherokee syllabary, Ge'ez (used for Amharic), or Thai
  — and add it to the poster with its type and a sample.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner identify the script types of a few samples
  and name at least two writing systems?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to each sample and say how it records language —
  by sound, by consonant, by consonant-plus-vowel, or by meaning?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to show you their writing system or one they know, and learn one new thing
about how it works.

## Resources

- The typology of writing systems (alphabet, abjad, abugida, logographic) is standard in
  linguistics; see Peter T. Daniels & William Bright, eds., *The World's Writing Systems*
  (Oxford, 1996), and John DeFrancis on Chinese writing. The claim that no script is inherently
  better is a value commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
