# Lesson 6 — Author's Structure: Problem and Solution

## Summary

Learners learn to recognize how authors use a **problem-and-solution structure** to organize
ideas. They meet **signal words** ("the problem is," "one solution is," "to solve this"), label
the problem and solution in a short passage, and explain how the author organized the text.
This completes the pair of lessons on an author's text structure.

## Objectives

- Explain how an author uses a problem-and-solution structure to organize ideas.
  _(D04.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

A roof leaks, so someone patches it. A path washes out, so the village digs a channel. Life is
full of **problems** and the **solutions** people build for them. Authors who write about real
challenges — a dry well, an invasive insect, a flooded market — often organize their writing
exactly that way: first the problem, then the solution, linked with words like **so** and **to
solve this**. Spot the pattern and you can see not just what happened, but how people answered
it.

## Materials

- Problem-and-solution poster
- Problem-and-solution short passage
- Problem/solution signal-word card

## Preparation

- Print or draw the problem-and-solution poster (problem box → arrow → solution box, with
  signal words).
- Copy a short passage organized by problem and solution for each pair.
- Copy the signal-word card.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that a
**problem-and-solution structure** is a second tool authors use, and that spotting it turns a
reader from a receiver of facts into a follower of an argument. Teach the signal words
explicitly, then let learners hunt. Choose problems from many places — water, soil, pests,
transport, food storage — and never frame any community as helpless; the point is human
agency and shared solutions. Connect the structure to hope: a problem named is a problem that
can be met. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding real-world difficulty with agency attached.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you learned how authors chain **cause and effect**. Today
   you meet a second structure: **problem and solution** — how authors set up a challenge and
   then show the answer.
2. **Meet the structure (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the left, a **problem**: "the well
   runs dry." On the right, a **solution**: "the community builds a shared rain barrel." An
   arrow marked **so** connects them. Authors signal this with words like **the problem is**,
   **one solution is**, and **to solve this**.
3. **Read and label (15 min).** With a partner, read the short passage. Mark the **problem**
   and the **solution**. Circle the **signal words**. Then draw the two boxes and the arrow
   the author used.
4. **Explain the author's choice (10 min).** Answer together: why did this author choose
   problem and solution? How is it different from the cause-and-effect chain you read last
   time?
5. **Close (5 min).** Readers who can name a text's structure can hold its whole shape in their
   hands — and writers (like you) can use the same shape to make their own ideas clear.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the passage with the problem pre-marked, and have the learner find the
  solution and the signal words.
- **Extension:** Find a text that offers two solutions, compare them, and say which the author
  seems to favor and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner identify a passage's problem and solution
  and explain how the author organized the text?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I say the problem in one sentence, the solution in one
  sentence, and the signal word that joins them?"

## Home connection

Name one real problem at home and one solution your family uses for it — and say it with a
signal word like "to solve this."
