# Lesson 3 — A Presentation with One Main Idea

## Summary

Learners begin building a short presentation by learning to state a **single, clear main
idea**. They meet a worked example, use a **sentence frame** to shrink a topic into one
memorable sentence, and give that sentence aloud to a small audience. Supporting reasons and
a conclusion follow in the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Give a short presentation with a clear main idea. _(D04.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

A speaker stands up and talks about the weather, then a game, then a cousin's visit — and you
leave not sure what you were meant to remember. Now imagine another speaker who begins, "I
want you to know one thing: every year, monarch butterflies travel farther than any
classroom in this building." You remember it. A presentation works when it has **one main
idea**, stated clearly at the start — like a single strong rope the audience can hold onto,
whether you speak under a tin roof or in a courtyard.

## Materials

- Main-idea poster
- Main-idea sentence frame
- Topic cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the main-idea poster (one bright banner vs. a crossed-out tangle of ideas).
- Copy a sentence frame for each learner.
- Cut topic cards naming simple, appealing topics.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that a strong presentation
rides on **one clear main idea**, not many. Model the shrinking of a rambling example into one
sentence, and let learners choose topics they genuinely care about — interest is the engine of
clarity. Speaking in the home language is welcome; the goal is being understood by the
audience in front of them. Keep the tone warm and unranked: a main idea is "clear" or "not
yet clear," never "good" or "bad." Use explicit instruction for the sentence frame, then let
choice and practice carry the rest.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time a speaker lost you — too many ideas at once. Today you
   start building a short presentation, and you begin with its heart: the **main idea** — the
   one thing you want your audience to remember.
2. **Meet the main idea (10 min).** Look at the poster. One side shows a tangle of many small
   ideas, crossed out. The other shows one bright banner: "**I want you to know one thing:
   monarch butterflies migrate farther than most animals their size.**" A main idea is one
   clear sentence, stated early. Everything else in a presentation *serves* that sentence.
3. **Learn the frame (5 min).** Read the sentence frame: "**Today I want you to know one
   thing:** ___." Practice saying it once with a simple topic, aloud.
4. **Find your main idea (15 min).** Pick a topic you care about — from the cards or your own
   life. Say the topic to yourself, then shrink it into **one clear main-idea sentence** using
   the frame. Say it to a small audience (a partner or group). They answer one question: "Can
   you tell me the one thing they wanted you to remember?" If they can, your main idea is
   clear.
5. **Close (5 min).** A clear main idea is a gift to your audience — it tells them exactly
   what to hold onto. You have now built the spine of your presentation.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a menu of ready main ideas to choose from, then let the learner say one
  aloud and adjust it. A learner may show their main idea instead of speaking — pointing to a
  prepared card, signing, or using a communication device — and may present while seated; the
  audience still states back the one idea.
- **Extension:** Write a main idea, then rewrite it for a different audience (younger
  children, a grandparent) and notice how the wording shifts.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** Can the audience state back the speaker's one main idea
  after a single hearing?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If someone repeated my main idea, would it match the one
  I meant to send?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one thing you learned today in a single clear sentence — and ask if they
can repeat your main idea.

## Resources
