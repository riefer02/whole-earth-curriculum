# Lesson 14 — Language and Culture

## Summary

Learners discover that **language is tied to culture** by comparing words for **family, food,
and respect** across languages. They see how different languages make different distinctions
(one word for "uncle" versus several; informal and formal "you"), share words from their own
home languages, and reflect on what each reveals about what a culture values.

## Objectives

- Describe how language is tied to culture, such as words for family, food, or respect that
  differ across languages. _(D04.S4.05.02)_

## Connection

In English, one word — "uncle" — names your father's brother and your mother's brother. In
Hindi, they are different people with different words: **चाचा** (*chacha*, father's younger
brother) and **मामा** (*mama*, mother's brother). In Spanish, you say **tú** to a friend and
**usted** to someone you respect or do not know well. Languages are not just different labels
for the same things — they **sort the world differently**, and the way they sort it shows what
a culture cares about: exactly how you are related, or exactly how much respect to show.

## Materials

- Language-and-culture poster
- Word comparison cards
- A "my language, my culture" page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the language-and-culture poster (family / food / respect rows).
- Copy word comparison cards for each group.
- Copy the "my language, my culture" page.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **words carry culture**:
the distinctions a language makes reveal what its speakers value, and no language's
distinctions are "better" — they are different and each is meaningful. Use accurate examples
and check them (Hindi *chacha*/*mama*; Spanish *tú*/*usted*; Japanese honorifics; the two
routes that spread the word "tea"/"chai"). Invite learners to be the experts on their own home
languages; treat every contribution as a gift, and never rank or correct a learner's language.
The linguistic facts are evidence; the claim that all languages are equally worthy is a value
we hold (`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a word you use with your family that a stranger might not know
   — a name for a relative, a dish, a way of saying "please." Today you learn that **words
   carry culture**: they show what a people values.
2. **Meet family words (10 min).** Look at the poster's first row. English has one word,
   "uncle." Hindi has **चाचा** (*chacha*) and **मामा** (*mama*) — it sorts the father's side and
   the mother's side. Neither is better; each shows what that culture pays attention to.
3. **Meet food and respect words (10 min).** Row two: the same drink spread by sea as "tea" and
   by land as "chai" — one word, two journeys, two sounds. Row three: Spanish has **tú**
   (familiar) and **usted** (formal). The language *requires* you to choose how much respect to
   show. What does that reveal?
4. **Compare and share (15 min).** With your group, sort the word cards into family, food, and
   respect — and talk about what each word reveals. Then, on your page, write a word or two from
   **your home language** and one thing it reveals about your family's culture.
5. **Close (5 min).** A language is a map of what its speakers treasure. Listening for the
   words that don't translate — and the distinctions they make — is how you meet a culture with
   respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one row (family) with two concrete examples, and allow drawing the
  relationship or the food alongside the word.
- **Extension:** Compare how two languages handle respect or kinship in more depth, and reflect
  on what each culture foregrounds.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner give an example of how a language's words
  for family, food, or respect differ and say what that difference reveals?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I name one word in my home language and explain what
  it shows about my family's culture?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one word that doesn't translate easily, and learn the story behind what
it shows about your family's way of life.

## Resources

- Kinship terminology and honorifics as windows into culture are standard in linguistic
  anthropology; see Daniel L. Everett, *Language: The Cultural Tool*, and the classic work of
  Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf on language and thought. The spread of the words "tea"
  and "chai" is documented in the *World Atlas of Language Structures* and popular linguistic
  histories. The claim that all languages are equally valid is a value commitment
  (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
