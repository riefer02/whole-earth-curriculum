# Lesson 3 — How an Artwork Reflects Its Culture's Place, Materials, and Beliefs

## Summary

Learners meet artworks from several world regions and learn to **read them as windows**
into a culture's **place, materials, and beliefs**. A worked example reads a Māori
whakairo carving (forest wood, the koru fern spiral, carved ancestors) and an Akan adinkra
cloth (plant dye, proverb symbols). Learners then observe other traditions — always as
**living arts made by living people** to honor, never as specimens to rank.

## Objectives

- Explain how an artwork from a specific culture reflects that culture's place,
  materials, or beliefs, citing what they observe. _(D07.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

A friend's home tells you about them — the plants in the window, the food on the shelf,
the pictures on the wall. An **artwork** is a community's home made visible: it shows
what materials the place gives, and what the people hold dear. A carving from a forest
land is made of wood; a cloth from a place of cotton and dye carries its colors; a floor
drawing made of rice flour feeds the insects when it is done. Reading art this way is how
we meet a community with **respect and wonder**, not as a strange "other" but as people
who, like us, make beauty from what surrounds them.

## Materials

- Artwork-from-a-culture image cards
- Observation chart

## Preparation

- Prepare the image cards, each labeled with its community and place.
- Copy an observation chart for each learner.
- Recall Grade 4: learners identified the subject, materials, and likely purpose of an
  artwork from another culture and asked one respectful question.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **non-ranking
observation**: we look closely at what a tradition is *made of* and *shows*, and connect
those observations to a place, its materials, and its beliefs — never to rank one
tradition "better" or "more advanced." These are **living traditions**, made today by
living artists and communities; say this plainly and often. If a learner (or their
family) belongs to a tradition shown here, invite them to be the authority on its
meaning and never correct them about their own culture. Some works carry sacred meaning
(Aboriginal Australian Dreaming stories, Tibetan sand mandalas); present them with
reverence as knowledge held by specific communities, not as curiosities. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on honoring living traditions and `docs/contexts.md` on cultural
power dynamics.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you finished an artwork of your own. Today you look
   at artworks made by communities around the world — and read them the way you read a
   friend's home: what does this tell me about the **place**, the **materials**, and the
   **beliefs** of the people who made it?
2. **Meet the reading (15 min).** Look at the first card, a **Māori whakairo** carving
   from Aotearoa (New Zealand). *What I observe:* it is carved from wood, with a spiral
   like an unfurling fern frond and carved figures. *What it tells me:* wood is the forest
   material of Aotearoa; the **koru** spiral is the unfurling fern, a plant of that land;
   the carved figures are ancestors, showing **whakapapa** — belonging through family and
   ancestry. Now the second card, **adinkra** cloth from Ghana. *What I observe:* cloth
   stamped with repeating symbols, dyed with plant color. *What it tells me:* cotton and
   dye are the materials of the place; each **symbol is a proverb** — a belief in picture
   form; the cloth is worn at ceremonies and in daily life.
3. **Observe your own (15 min).** Choose two more cards — a rangoli/kolam floor drawing,
   an Aboriginal Australian dot painting, an Inuit soapstone carving, a Persian tile
   mosaic, or a Tibetan sand mandala. For each, fill your chart: one thing you **observe**,
   and what it tells you about the **place**, the **materials**, or the **beliefs**.
4. **Share and compare (10 min).** In a small group, share one of your readings. Listen
   for the difference between *what you see* (the observation) and *what it tells you*
   (the meaning). Notice how every tradition makes sense in its own place.
5. **Close (5 min).** Art is a people's way of saying "this is where we live, this is
   what we have, this is what we hold dear." Every tradition you met today is alive and
   belongs to people who still make and love it. We honor it by looking closely and
   speaking with respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sentence starter — "I see ___, and it tells me the place is ___ /
  the people value ___" — and allow a single observation-to-meaning pair per artwork.
- **Extension:** Research a third tradition (with a source), complete a chart row, and
  write one sentence on how its materials come from its environment.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name at least one observable feature
  of an artwork and connect it, with a reason, to the culture's place, materials, or
  beliefs — without ranking the tradition against another?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name something I actually saw, and did I
  connect it to place, materials, or beliefs with a reason — speaking about living people
  with respect?"

## Home connection

Find one object or image from a culture different from your own (online, in a shop, in a
neighbor's home) and name one thing you observe and one thing it might tell you about its
place or people.
