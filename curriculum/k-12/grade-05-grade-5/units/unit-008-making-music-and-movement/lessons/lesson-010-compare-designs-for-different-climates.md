# Lesson 10 — Compare Designs for Different Climates: How Environment Shapes Design

## Summary

The capstone brings the unit together: learners **compare shelters designed for different
climates and resources** and explain how the environment shapes design — an igloo's snow
dome for the cold, a yurt's felt for the steppe, a stilt house for the floodplain, an
adobe house for dry heat. They revisit the unit's through-lines — plan and complete an
artwork, compose and perform, choreograph together, design with reason — in a closing
reflection.

## Objectives

- Compare designs made for different climates or resources and explain how the
  environment shapes design. _(D07.S4.05.02)_
- Revisit planning and completing an artwork _(D07.S1.05.01)_, composing and performing a
  piece _(D07.S2.05.01)_, and choreographing cooperatively _(D07.S3.05.01)_.

## Connection

Your home is shaped by where you live, even if you never noticed. Thick walls keep out
desert heat; stilts lift a house above floodwater; snow blocks, packed hard, trap warmth
in the bitter cold; a felt tent rolls up and travels with a herding family across the
steppe. Builders everywhere answer the same need — shelter — with whatever the land gives
and the climate demands. Design is the meeting of **people, place, and need**. Today you
compare those answers and see the pattern: **the environment shapes the design**.

## Materials

- House cards
- Comparison chart

## Preparation

- Prepare the house cards, each labeled with its place and climate.
- Copy a comparison chart for each learner.
- Recall Grade 4: learners explained how an object from another culture reflects its
  materials, needs, and ideas.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **environment shapes
design**, read through comparison — never ranking. An igloo and an adobe house are each
*perfect* for their place; neither is "better." Guard against framing any people's
building as "primitive"; these are sophisticated, time-tested answers to real conditions,
still built and valued by living communities. The closing reflection is the unit's
retrieval practice: recall the plan-and-complete artwork, the composed and performed
piece, and the cooperative dance — not as review but as a chance to see the whole. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** This unit you planned and completed an artwork, composed and
   performed a piece, choreographed a dance together, and built something that solved a
   real problem. Today one last idea ties it all together: **design answers its
   environment**.
2. **Meet the shelters (15 min).** Look at the house cards, one at a time. The **igloo** —
   a dome of snow blocks in the Arctic cold: the dome sheds the wind, and packed snow traps
   body heat. The **yurt (ger)** — a round tent of felt over a wooden lattice on the open
   steppe: felt from sheep wool is warm and rolls up, so herding families can move with
   their animals. The **stilt house** — a wooden house raised on posts where rivers flood:
   the stilts lift the living space above the water. The **adobe house** — thick
   mud-brick walls in dry heat: the thick walls hold out the sun's heat and keep the inside
   cool. Each is a different answer to the same question: *how do we shelter ourselves
   here, with what this place gives?*
3. **Compare (15 min).** On your chart, fill in each shelter: its **climate and place**,
   its **materials**, and **how the design fits the environment**. Then write one sentence
   that states the pattern you see across all of them.
4. **Share the pattern (10 min).** In a small group, share your sentence. Listen for how
   each person names the pattern: the *materials* come from the place, and the *shape*
   answers the climate. No design is "better" — each fits where it lives.
5. **Revisit the unit (10 min).** Look back: you planned an artwork with a clear idea and
   completed it; you composed a piece with a beginning, middle, and end and performed it;
   you choreographed a dance cooperatively; you designed and built a fix and explained
   your choices. Say which of these you are most proud of, and why.
6. **Close (5 min).** The arts and design are one human act: seeing the world, and
   shaping something honest and useful from it — with the land, with each other, and with
   a clear idea. That act belongs to everyone, everywhere, and now it belongs to you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each house to its climate with a sorting activity first, and use a
  sentence starter — "Because the climate is ___ and the material is ___, the design
  ___."
- **Extension:** Design a shelter for a chosen climate (cold, wet, hot, windy), choose
  materials, and write a short defense of the choice.

## Assessment

- **Summative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner compare at least two designs for
  different climates and explain, with reasons, how each environment shaped its design?
  Does the closing reflection recall the unit's through-lines?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I explain how climate and materials shape a
  design, and can I name what I am most proud of from this unit?"

## Home connection

Look at your own home (or a neighbor's) and name one way its design answers your local
climate or materials — thick walls, a sloped roof, shade, or a water channel.
