# Lesson 5 — Practice Builds Skill

## Summary

Learners discover that a **motor skill** — throwing accurately or balancing — improves with
**repeated practice**. They meet a **worked example** of a throw improving over three tries,
then practice a chosen skill themselves in short, repeated rounds, tallying attempts to watch
their own skill refine. Because this is a *new skill*, the lesson uses **explicit steps and
guided practice** before independent work (see `docs/philosophy.md` §13).

## Objectives

- Refine a specific motor skill, such as throwing accurately or balancing, through repeated
  practice and give a peer helpful feedback. _(D03.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

Think of the first time you tried to ride a bicycle, braid hair, or throw a ball — it was
clumsy, and then, try after try, it got smoother. **Practice is how a body learns**: each
repeat builds the path in your brain and your muscles until the clumsy try becomes a steady
skill. Whether the skill is throwing at a target, balancing on a line, carrying water, or
kicking a ball, the rule is the same everywhere — **repetition refines**.

## Materials

- Practice builds skill poster
- Target (drawn or taped) and a soft ball or rolled sock
- Attempt tally page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the practice builds skill poster (three attempts at a target — far, closer,
  hit — with a tally, labeled "practice builds skill").
- Set up a safe throwing target and balance line; prepare a soft ball or rolled sock per
  pair.
- Copy an attempt tally page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **skill improves with
repeated practice**, and the learner can *see* it in their own tally. Teach the skill with
**explicit steps and a worked example** (this is a novice skill, not a discovery). Keep it
**non-competitive**: the learner compares their *own* first round to their own last round,
never to a peer. Offer the balance line as an equal choice for learners who cannot or prefer
not to throw, and offer a seated or adapted version of either skill (throw from a chair, trace
a line with a finger, balance a soft object on a flat hand) so every body can practice; honor
every body's pace — a learner may improve slowly or not yet, and that is information, never a
verdict. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You know your body grows, and you know your mind and body are
   connected. Now the joy of **movement**: today you learn that **practice builds skill**.
2. **Meet the skill (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example**. Throw one: the
   ball lands far from the target. Throw two: closer. Throw three: a hit. Same body, same
   target — the difference is **repetition**.    Watch the steps: stand or sit tall, eyes on the target,
   arm back, step (or lean forward) and release toward the target, follow through. For
   **balancing**: eyes on a steady point ahead, arms out like wings, slow your steps, foot in
   front of foot — or, seated, trace a line with your finger or balance a soft object on a
   flat hand.
3. **Guided practice (10 min).** In pairs, try the steps together slowly, ten times each. A
   partner watches and counts. Each round, **tally** your tries on your page.
4. **Repeat and refine (15 min).** Do three more rounds of ten. After each round, notice:
   does your first round or your last round look steadier? Keep the tally. Compare **your own**
   early tries to your own later tries — not to your partner's.
5. **Close (5 min).** A skill is not a gift you either have or do not have; it is something
   you **build**, one repetition at a time. The tally is the proof.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Break the skill into fewer, slower steps and use a closer target or a wider
  balance line; offer a seated or one-handed version of the skill for learners who need it;
  scribe the tally.
- **Extension:** Move to a farther target or a narrower line and track improvement over more
  rounds, noting what each repetition changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance):** Can the learner follow the steps of the skill, tally their
  attempts, and describe how their later tries improved over their earlier ones?
- **Self-check:** The learner compares their first and last tally and says in one sentence
  what changed — accuracy, steadiness, or confidence.

## Home connection

Pick one small skill at home — folding, pouring, hopping, drawing — and practice it ten times,
watching it get steadier.

## Resources

- Repeated practice building a motor skill reflects well-established research on motor
  learning and deliberate practice. For novice skills, explicit instruction with worked
  examples and guided practice is more effective than discovery alone (Kirschner, Sweller &
  Clark, 2006). For children's movement, see the World Health Organization physical activity
  fact sheet: <https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity>
