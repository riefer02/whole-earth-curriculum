# Lesson 7 — Movement Helps My Heart

## Summary

Learners discover that the **heart is a muscle** and that movement makes it work — and
**regular movement makes it stronger**. They feel and count their own **pulse** at rest and
after a short burst of movement, then connect the faster beat to the heart being exercised,
the way practice builds a skill. This is the first of two lessons on how movement benefits
the body.

## Objectives

- Explain how regular movement benefits the heart, muscles, and mood. _(D03.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

Put your hand on your chest and you will feel your **heart** beating — it has been beating
since before you were born, and it is a **muscle**. Like any muscle, it gets stronger when it
works. When you run, dance, or climb, your heart pumps faster to carry blood — with the oxygen
from the air you breathe — to your body; do that regularly, and the heart grows strong and
steady, so it does not have to work so hard when you rest. Whether you walk to a well, play a game, or dance at a festival, your
heart is getting its exercise.

## Materials

- Heart and movement poster
- Pulse count page
- A clock or timer

## Preparation

- Print or draw the heart and movement poster (a heart and a moving figure with a pulse line
  that quickens, labeled "movement makes the heart pump; regular movement makes it stronger").
- Copy a pulse count page for each learner.
- Prepare a way to time 15 seconds and 2 minutes, and a safe space to move.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that the **heart is a
muscle strengthened by regular movement**. Keep the movement **short, joyful, and inclusive**:
hopping in place, dancing, brisk walking, or arm circles — every body can move in its own way,
and seated or adapted movement exercises the heart too. Some learners may have a heart
condition or feel anxious about their heartbeat; reassure them that a faster beat after
movement is normal and healthy. Do not push any learner beyond comfort. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have practiced a skill and given feedback. Now a bigger question:
   **what does movement do for your body?** Today, your **heart**.
2. **Meet the heart (10 min).** Look at the poster. Your heart is a **muscle** about the size
   of your fist. It pumps blood — carrying oxygen and fuel — to your whole body, all day, every
   day. The wavy line
   is a **pulse** — the rhythm of the heart's beats. When you move, the line gets taller and
   faster: the heart works harder. Do that **regularly**, and the heart grows **stronger and
   steadier** — exactly like practice builds a skill (remember Lesson 5).
3. **Feel your pulse (10 min).** Find your pulse: press two fingers gently on the inside of
   your wrist, or on the side of your neck. Count the beats for 15 seconds with the timer.
   Write the number on your page. That is your **resting** pulse.
4. **Move and feel again (15 min).** Move for **two minutes** — hop, dance, walk fast, or pump
   your arms. Then find your pulse again and count for 15 seconds. Write the number. Compare:
   why is the second number bigger? Because your heart was **working** — and work is how a
   muscle grows strong.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your heart works for you every second. When you move, you give it its
   exercise — and a stronger heart is a gift you carry for life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Guide learners to find the pulse by demonstrating on your own wrist, and count
  beats aloud together; offer seated movement options.
- **Extension:** Explain why the heart, being a muscle, strengthens with repeated work — and
  connect this to the *resting* heart working less hard in fit bodies.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner feel and count a pulse, and explain in their
  own words why movement makes the heart work and regular movement makes it stronger?
- **Self-check:** The learner compares their two pulse counts and says one sentence about what
  the bigger number means.

## Home connection

With someone at home, find your pulses, move together for two minutes, and feel them again —
then share what changed and why.

## Resources

- The heart is a muscle that strengthens with regular aerobic activity, and physical activity
  supports heart health. The World Health Organization recommends that children and young
  people aged 5–17 do at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily:
  <https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity>
