# Lesson 1 — My Mind and My Body Are Connected

## Summary

Learners meet the **mind–body connection**: a thought or feeling in the mind can show up
as a **sensation in the body**. They work with a simple, relatable example — worry making
a stomach feel tight — and then trace one safe feeling of their own, marking where it
shows up in their own body on a **body outline**. This is the first step of a unit about
knowing the body with precision, dignity, and joy.

## Objectives

- Explain the mind–body connection with a simple example, such as how worry can make a
  stomach feel tight. _(D03.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Think of a time you had to speak in front of people, or wait for a result that mattered.
Maybe your **stomach felt tight**, or your **hands felt shaky**, even though your mind was
just thinking. That is the mind–body connection: what happens in your mind — a worry, a
hope, an excitement — sends a **signal** to your body. Whether you live in a busy city or
on a mountain farm, and whether the feeling is a flutter or a knot, your mind and your
body are always talking to each other.

## Materials

- Mind–body connection poster
- Blank body outline page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the mind–body connection poster (a worried thought flowing into a body
  outline with a shaded, tight stomach).
- Copy a blank body outline page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and a quiet space for individual work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **the mind and
body are connected** — a thought or feeling can show up as a body sensation. Keep the
examples **ordinary and safe**; a learner may live with real stress or illness, so do not
push anyone to reveal more than they choose. The skill is *noticing* the signal with
curiosity, never judging it as good or bad. If a learner names something heavy, honor it
calmly and move gently. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned that your body changes with activity and
   rest — your heartbeat, your breathing, your warmth. Today you learn something new and
   powerful: your **mind** and your **body** are connected. A thought or feeling can show
   up in your body before you even put it into words.
2. **Meet the connection (10 min).** Look at the poster. It shows a **worked example**:
   a thought — "I have a big test tomorrow" — flows down into a body, and the **stomach
   feels tight**. That tight feeling is the body answering the mind. This is what "mind and
   body are connected" means. It works the other way too: a deep breath can calm a racing
   mind. The two always talk.
3. **Scan your body (10 min).** Think of one **safe, ordinary** feeling you had this week
   — excitement, worry, tiredness, joy. Soften your gaze. Where does it show up in your
   body? A flutter in the chest? Warm hands? Heavy shoulders? A tight tummy? Name the
   spot without judging it.
4. **Trace your own (15 min).** On your body outline page, **color the spot** where that
   feeling lives for you, and write the feeling's name beside it. Check with a partner:
   does your friend's feeling show up in the same place or a different one? There is no
   wrong answer — every body speaks in its own way.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your body is not separate from your feelings; it is where they live.
   Noticing the signal is the first step to understanding yourself — and to taking care of
   yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a word bank of feelings and a pre-labeled body outline to color
  instead of writing; scribe the feeling name.
- **Extension:** Trace two feelings and compare where each lives, then name one way the
  body signal helped them know what they felt sooner.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a feeling and point to a body spot
  where it shows up, explaining the mind–body connection in their own words?
- **Self-check:** The learner points to their marked spot and asks, "Is this truly where
  and how that feeling shows up for me?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Where in your body do you feel worry or joy?" and share one body
signal you noticed in yourself today.

## Resources

- The idea that thoughts and feelings are connected to body sensations is central to
  embodied emotion and to cognitive-behavioral approaches (see the work of Aaron Beck).
