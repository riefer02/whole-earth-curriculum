# Lesson 6 — Helpful Feedback to a Peer

## Summary

Learners learn to give **feedback that helps** a peer improve a motor skill — feedback that is
**specific, kind, and about the action, never the person**. They meet a contrast of helpful
and hurtful feedback, practice a **three-part starter** ("I noticed ___. What helped me was
___. Try ___.'"), and give one real, kind tip as their partner practices the skill from Lesson
5 — then try again.

## Objectives

- Refine a specific motor skill, such as throwing accurately or balancing, through repeated
  practice and give a peer helpful feedback. _(D03.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

If a friend misses a throw, which helps more — "**you're bad at this**" or "**I noticed your
elbow dropped — try keeping it up**"? The first hurts and teaches nothing; the second is a
gift. Good feedback is like a teammate's hand on your back: it names what is real, in a way
you can use, and it believes you can improve. Whether in a game, a kitchen, or a workshop,
the people who help us grow are the ones who tell us the truth **kindly**.

## Materials

- Helpful feedback poster
- Feedback sentence-starter card
- Target or balance line and a soft ball

## Preparation

- Print or draw the helpful feedback poster (helpful vs. hurtful comment, the hurtful one
  crossed out).
- Copy a feedback sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Reuse the throwing target or balance line from Lesson 5.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **feedback about the
action, not the person** — specific, kind, and usable. Model the difference vividly and stop
any feedback that is about the person ("you're slow," "you're bad") the moment it appears,
redirecting to the action. Feedback is the strongest lever in learning (Hattie, *Visible
Learning*), but only when it is clear and kind. Honor every body's pace: feedback is about a
next step, never about ranking. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned that practice builds skill. Today you learn the
   partner skill: **giving feedback that helps** a peer improve.
2. **Meet helpful feedback (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two comments about the same missed
   throw. *Hurtful:* "You're bad at this." Crossed out — it names the *person*, not the
   *action*, and helps nothing. *Helpful:* "I noticed your elbow dropped — try keeping it up."
   It is **specific** (names one thing), **kind** (believes you can grow), and **about the
   action**. That is feedback that helps.
3. **Learn the starter (5 min).** Read the three-part starter: "**I noticed** ___ . **What
   helped me was** ___ . **Try** ___ ." Practice it once with a partner about a simple skill,
   using kind words.
4. **Give real feedback (15 min).** Partner up at the target or balance line. One partner
   practices the skill from Lesson 5; the other **observes quietly**, then gives one specific,
   kind tip using the starter. The first partner **tries again** with the tip in mind. Then
   swap. Each of you gives and gets one tip.
5. **Close (5 min).** A kind, specific word can help someone improve faster than hours of
   silent practice. You can be that help for someone — and ask for it for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the starter printed large with blanks, and model one full example
  before partners begin; allow a tip about one single, visible thing.
- **Extension:** Distinguish feedback about the *action* from feedback about the *person*, and
  rewrite two hurtful comments into helpful ones.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/performance):** Does the learner give feedback that is specific, kind, and
  about the action, and can their partner act on it and try again?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Was my tip about the action, not the person, and was it
  kind enough that I would welcome hearing it myself?"

## Home connection

Give one specific, kind piece of feedback to someone at home today — and notice whether it
helps them more than a general "good job."

## Resources
