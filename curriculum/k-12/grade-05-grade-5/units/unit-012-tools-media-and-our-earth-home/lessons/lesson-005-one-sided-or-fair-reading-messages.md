# Lesson 5 — One-Sided or Fair: Reading Messages

## Summary

Learners learn to recognize when a message **favors one side** and to name what **fair
reporting** would include. A worked example contrasts a one-sided message about a new road
("JOBS! Wonderful for everyone!") with what fair reporting would add — the **benefits**, the
**concerns**, and **who is speaking**. Learners then analyze one-sided message cards and build
a fair version with three questions, learning to read messages without cynicism or fear:
one-sided *pushes*; fair reporting *informs*.

## Objectives

- Recognize when a message favors one side, and explain what fair reporting would include.
  _(D10.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

You have seen messages that push you to feel one way — an ad that says a toy is "the best
ever," a poster that says a new road is "wonderful for everyone." A message that shows only one
side is not *lying*; it is just leaving things out. Fair reporting is not about tearing
messages down — it is about *filling in what is missing*: the benefits, the concerns, and who
is speaking. Today you learn to do that.

## Materials

- One-sided vs. fair poster
- Message cards
- Fair-reporting page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the one-sided vs. fair poster.
- Copy message cards for each pair and a fair-reporting page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **three-question
fairness test**: *what benefits does the message show? what concerns does it leave out? who is
speaking — and who is not heard?* Model the road message fully as a worked example. Teach
this **without cynicism or fear**: the goal is not to distrust every message but to *complete*
one-sided messages into fair ones. Fair reporting is taught as a positive ideal — include all
sides, give evidence, and say who is speaking. Distinguish evidence from values: *what a
message includes and omits* is observable; *that fairness requires hearing all sides* is a
value we hold. Choose examples that are age-safe and globally adaptable — a road, a market, a
playground — so no region is framed as "the problem." See `docs/philosophy.md` §4–5 and
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall the last two lessons: we check claims, and we note when sources
   agree or disagree. Today, a closely related skill: noticing when a message shows only one
   side.
2. **Meet one-sided vs. fair (12 min).** Look at the poster. A **one-sided message** says,
   "The new road brings JOBS! Wonderful for everyone!" — only the good side, only one voice.
   **Fair reporting would add:** the **benefits** (jobs, faster travel), the **concerns**
   (homes and trees lost, air and noise), and **who is speaking** (the builder? neighbors?
   families who live there?). Three questions: *what benefits? what concerns? who is
   speaking?*
3. **Practice with a partner (18 min).** Take a message card — a new playground, a school
   uniform rule, or a new phone, each told one-sided. Read it, then answer the three
   questions. On your fair-reporting page, write or draw the **benefits** it shows, the
   **concerns** it left out, and **who is speaking** — and who is not heard.
4. **Build a fair version (8 min).** With your partner, say the fair version in one breath:
   "Fair reporting would say ___ (benefits), ___ (concerns), and it would tell us ___ (who is
   speaking)."
5. **Close (5 min).** A one-sided message pushes you; a fair report informs you. You can now
   tell the difference — and you know how to complete the picture.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with the three rows partly filled in and a sentence starter;
   work one message aloud first.
- **Extension:** Rewrite a one-sided message into a fair one and compare how each version makes
   a reader feel and decide.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify that a message favors one side and name
   at least one benefit, one concern, and one missing voice that fair reporting would include?
- **Peer check:** Partners ask each other, "Did you name the benefits, the concerns, and *who
   is speaking* — not just say 'it's unfair'?"

## Home connection

Notice a message at home — an ad, a poster, a news clip. Ask the three questions: what
benefits, what concerns, who is speaking? Tell someone what a fair version would add.

## Resources

- The idea that fair reporting includes all sides, evidence, and transparency about who is
  speaking reflects media-literacy teaching standards (for example, UNESCO's *Media and
  Information Literacy* curriculum and Common Sense Media's guidance for ages 8–11). The
  "benefits, concerns, who is speaking" test is an age-appropriate version of source- and
  perspective-analysis taught worldwide. On the value stance (fairness as a commitment, not a
  fact), see `docs/philosophy.md` §4–5.
