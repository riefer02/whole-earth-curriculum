# Lesson 1 — An Everyday Tool, Before and After

## Summary

Learners trace how one everyday **technology** changed **daily life** over time. A worked
example follows the **mobile phone** — before it, messages traveled only as fast as a person
could walk; after it, a call or text reaches someone far away in seconds, with **new needs**
(charging, repairs, cost) too. Learners then choose a tool — a water pump, a refrigerator, a
sewing machine, or a radio — and trace its own before–after chain, seeing technology as
something people *use and shape*, with helpful and complicated effects.

## Objectives

- Explain how a technology people use every day, such as a phone or a water pump, has
  changed daily life over time. _(D10.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last message you received — a text, a call, a note. Now imagine the same message
before phones existed: someone would have had to walk or ride all the way to you to tell you.
That one difference — before and after a tool — is what we study today. A tool changes the
day, the work changes, and new needs appear along with the new ease.

## Materials

- Before–after chain poster (the phone)
- Tool menu cards (water pump, refrigerator, sewing machine, radio)
- Before–after page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the before–after chain poster for the phone.
- Copy the tool menu cards and a before–after page for each learner.
- If doing the acted version, gather a prop or two (a stick to "pump," a pretend message).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **a tool changes daily
life, with effects both helpful and complicated** — not that tools are simple "good" or
"bad." Model the phone chain fully (worked example) before learners trace their own tool
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep it honest and global: the phone and the water pump
did not arrive everywhere the same way or do the same job everywhere — that is the next
lesson. Distinguish evidence from values: *what changed* is something we can investigate;
*that ease is good and that care for new costs matters* are values we hold. Choose examples
that include the learner's own region and others, without ranking any as "behind" or
"ahead." See `docs/philosophy.md` §7 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall from Grade 4: people design tools to meet a need. Ask: what is
   one tool you used *today*? What did it help you do?
2. **Meet the phone chain (12 min).** Look at the poster. **Before the phone:** messages
   traveled only as fast as a person could walk, and news waited for a traveler. **After the
   phone:** a call or a text reaches someone far away in seconds, and families separated by
   distance can stay in touch. **And new needs:** the phone must be charged, repaired, and
   paid for. Say it as a chain: "before… after… and also…".
3. **Trace your own tool (18 min).** Choose a tool from the menu — the **water pump** (before:
   people drew water by hand or carried it from a river; after: a handle brings water up with
   less effort, but the pump needs parts and fuel), the **refrigerator** (before: food spoiled
   quickly and had to be eaten or preserved; after: food lasts days, but fridges use
   electricity), the **sewing machine** (before: clothes were sewn slowly by hand; after: many
   more garments, made faster, but the work changed), or the **radio** (before: news traveled
   with people; after: one voice could reach a whole region). On your page, write or draw what
   daily life was like **before** the tool and **after** it, and name one **effect** and one
   **new need**.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Swap pages. Ask each other: "Does the chain make sense?
   Did you show both a helpful effect *and* a new need?" Fix anything that is unclear.
5. **Close (5 min).** A tool is not magic — it is a choice people made that changed their
   days, for easier and for needing care. You can now trace that change from before to after.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide picture prompts for one tool's before and after, and scribe the
  learner's chain as they say it aloud.
- **Extension:** Choose a second tool, write its before–after chain, and compare which tool you
  think changed daily life more — with reasons, and no single "right" answer.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner trace one tool from before to after and name a
  real effect on daily life — including a new need?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I show both the before and the after, and did I name
  at least one helpful effect *and* one new need?"

## Home connection

Choose one tool your family uses every day (a phone, a stove, a bicycle, a pump) and ask
someone at home what daily life or work was like before it was common.

## Resources

- The mobile phone spread worldwide and reshaped daily communication — an evidence-based
  overview of how phones changed how people connect and work is summarized by the World Bank
  and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). Water pumps, refrigeration, and sewing
  machines each have documented before-and-after histories (hand-drawn wells to mechanical
  pumps; ice and preservation to electric refrigeration; hand-sewing to the machine that
  changed garment work). The framing that tools have both helpful and complicated effects
  follows the curriculum's "honest about technology" commitment (`docs/philosophy.md`).
