# Lesson 2 — Same Tool, Different Lives: Two Places

## Summary

Learners compare how people in **two different places** use a similar technology
**differently**, and suggest **why**. A worked example follows the **mobile phone**: in one
place, far from banks, it became a **wallet** for mobile money; in a busy city, it is mostly
for **calls, photos, and messages**. Learners then compare the **bicycle** or the **water
pump** across two places, discovering that a tool's use grows from the needs, land, and life
of each place — not from one place being "ahead" of another.

## Objectives

- Compare how people in two different places in the world use a similar technology
  differently, and suggest why. _(D10.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

The same bicycle you ride to a friend's house can, in another place, carry sacks of grain to
market, a passenger to a clinic, or a stack of water jugs home. The same phone that plays your
music is, elsewhere, a bank in someone's pocket. A tool is not used the same way everywhere,
because the *life around it* is different. Today you learn to compare two places and ask the
one question that explains it: **why?**

## Materials

- Two-places poster (the phone as wallet vs. the phone for calls and photos)
- Two-places comparison cards (the bicycle, the water pump)
- Compare page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-places poster for the phone.
- Copy the comparison cards for each pair and a compare page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **compare-and-why
move**: name the two places, name the two uses, then suggest *why* they differ. Model the
phone example fully before learners compare the bicycle or water pump. Keep the global frame
egalitarian: the point is **that** uses differ and **why**, never that one place is "advanced"
and another "behind." The mobile-money example is well documented (M-Pesa and similar services
grew where bank branches were scarce). Distinguish evidence from values: *where and how a tool
is used* is a fact we can investigate; *that each use is a fitting, intelligent response to a
place's needs* is the value stance we hold. See `docs/philosophy.md` §4 and
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall from last lesson: a tool changes daily life. Today, a new idea —
   the *same* tool can change daily life *differently* in two places.
2. **Meet the phone in two places (12 min).** Look at the poster. **Place A**, far from bank
   branches: people use the phone as a **wallet** — to send money, pay at a market, and save.
   **Place B**, a busy city with many banks: people use the phone mostly for **calls, photos,
   and messages**. Same phone, two lives. **Why?** In Place A there are few banks nearby, so
   the phone filled that need; in Place B there are many, so the phone does other jobs.
3. **Compare your own tool (18 min).** Choose the **bicycle** or the **water pump**. Bicycle:
   in one place it is a load-carrier and taxi (boda-boda) moving people and goods on dirt
   roads; in another it is a daily commuter ride on flat, bike-friendly streets — *why?*
   terrain, distance, the cost of fuel, and what needs moving. Water pump: in one place it
   lifts water for **drinking**; in another it irrigates **fields** — *why?* what each place
   needs the water for. On your compare page, write or draw Place 1 and its use, Place 2 and
   its use, and one **why**.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Tell your comparison in one breath: "In ___, people use
   the ___ for ___; in ___, they use it for ___ — because ___." Ask: does the "why" make
   sense, and does it respect both places equally?
5. **Close (5 min).** No use is "better" — each fits its place. People shape tools around the
   life they have.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with the two places and uses partly filled in, and a "why"
   sentence starter; work one comparison aloud first.
- **Extension:** Compare a tool your own family uses with how a friend or relative elsewhere
   uses it, and write a "why."

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name two places, two different uses, and one
   reason the uses differ?
- **Peer check:** Partners ask each other, "Did you name *two places*, *two uses*, and a *why*
   — without ranking either place as better?"

## Home connection

Think of one tool your household uses. Ask someone at home how a relative or friend in another
place uses that same tool differently, and why.

## Resources

- Mobile money (for example, M-Pesa) grew in places with few bank branches, letting people
  send, save, and pay by phone — documented by the Consultative Group to Assist the Poor
  (CGAP) and the World Bank. The bicycle's many uses — commuting in some places, carrying
  loads and passengers in others — and the water pump's double life as a drinking-water and
  irrigation device are widely documented (for example, ITU and UN Water materials). The
  egalitarian framing — no use ranked as "better" — follows `docs/philosophy.md` §4.
