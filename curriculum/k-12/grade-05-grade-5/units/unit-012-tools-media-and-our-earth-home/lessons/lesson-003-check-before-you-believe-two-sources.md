# Lesson 3 — Check Before You Believe: Two Sources

## Summary

Learners learn to **check a claim** across more than one **source** before believing it. A
worked example follows the claim "camels store water in their humps" through a book and a
wildlife website — both say the hump stores **fat, not water** — to the verdict **likely
false**. Learners then check safe, fun claims on cards, filling a five-box claim-check page
and stating a **verdict** grounded in where the sources agree.

## Objectives

- Evaluate whether a claim is likely true by checking more than one source and noting where
  they agree or disagree. _(D10.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever heard something and wondered, "is that really true?" — a friend says a rumor, a
screen shows a surprising fact, a post makes a big promise. One source is a start, but two
that agree are stronger. Checking a claim is not about distrusting people; it is about *being
careful with what we believe* — a habit that protects us and everyone we share information
with.

## Materials

- Claim-check flow poster
- Claim cards
- Claim-check page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the claim-check flow poster for the camel claim.
- Copy claim cards for each pair and a claim-check page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The routine to land is the **five-box
claim-check**: claim → source 1 → source 2 → where they agree → verdict. Model it fully as a
worked example before independent practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Teach
checking **without cynicism or fear**: the tone is "be careful, not suspicious," and the goal
is to *become harder to fool* while staying open-hearted. The camel claim is true-to-evidence
(a camel's hump stores fat, an energy reserve) and age-safe. Keep all practice claims
non-frightening. Distinguish evidence from values: *what the sources say and whether they
agree* is evidence; *that we should check before we believe* is a value this curriculum holds.
See `docs/philosophy.md` §5 and §7 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Ask: has someone ever told you a surprising fact, and you were not
   sure it was true? Today you learn the routine that settles it: **check more than one
   source**.
2. **Meet the claim-check (12 min).** Look at the flow poster. **Claim:** "camels store water
   in their humps." **Source 1** — a desert-animals book: "the hump stores fat, not water."
   **Source 2** — a wildlife website: "the hump stores fat, used for energy." **Where they
   agree:** the hump stores fat, not water. **Verdict:** the claim is **likely false**. Notice
   the claim did not pass just because one source said so — it failed because *two* agreed on
   something else.
3. **Practice with a partner (20 min).** Take a claim card — "goldfish only remember for three
   seconds," "lightning never strikes the same place twice," or "cracking your knuckles causes
   arthritis." Read the claim, then the two mini-sources on the card. On your claim-check page,
   fill the five boxes: claim → source 1 → source 2 → where they agree → verdict. State your
   verdict with the reason: "likely ___ because both sources say ___."
4. **Peer check (8 min).** Swap pages. Ask: "Did you fill all five boxes, and does your
   verdict match where the sources agreed?" Help fix any mismatch.
5. **Close (5 min).** A claim is a question until you check it. Two sources that agree are
   stronger than one — and you are now harder to fool.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a claim-check page with two boxes pre-filled, and a sentence starter
   for the verdict; work one claim aloud first.
- **Extension:** Find your own second source for a claim (a book, an adult, a trusted site)
   and explain which source you trust more and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a verdict for a claim and point to where
   two sources agreed to support it?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I check more than one source, and did my verdict
   follow from what they agreed on?"

## Home connection

Hear a surprising claim at home? Check it with a second source (a book, a grown-up, another
site) and tell someone what you found and why you now believe or doubt it.

## Resources

- The camel's hump stores fat (an energy reserve), not water — widely documented in children's
  science references (for example, BBC Bitesize and Britannica Kids). The "goldfish memory" and
  "lightning never strikes twice" claims are common myths corrected by science-education
  sources. The five-box claim-check routine (claim → source 1 → source 2 → agreement →
  verdict) operationalizes the standard's "checking more than one source"; on modeling the
  routine before practice, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1>.
