# Lesson 4 — When Sources Agree and Disagree

## Summary

Learners learn what to do when sources **agree** — and when they **disagree**. A worked
example compares two sources on how much of Earth the oceans cover: they agree on "about
seven-tenths" and disagree only on the exact number, so the claim is likely true but the
number stays open. Learners then sort source pairs into **overlap** and **gap**, learning that
disagreement is information — a sign to say **"not sure yet"** and look for a third source,
never to hide the gap.

## Objectives

- Evaluate whether a claim is likely true by checking more than one source and noting where
  they agree or disagree. _(D10.S2.05.01)_

## Connection

Two friends both watched the same football match. One says the score was 3–1; the other says
3–2. They *agree* someone won by a goal or two, but they *disagree* on the exact score. When
you notice where sources overlap and where they split, you can say what you are sure of, what
you are not, and when to look for a third voice. That is today's skill.

## Materials

- Agree–disagree poster (ocean coverage)
- Source-pair cards
- Agree–disagree page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the agree–disagree poster.
- Copy source-pair cards for each pair and an agree–disagree page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **overlap-and-gap
move**: name where sources *agree* (the overlap), name where they *disagree* (the gap), then
state a verdict that respects both — and say "not sure yet" when the gap matters. Model the
ocean example fully as a worked example. Teach that disagreement is normal and useful, not a
failure: honest sources often differ on details, and a learner who notices the gap is thinking
*well*, not badly. Keep the tone non-cynical: the goal is careful, fair judgment, not
suspicion of everything. Distinguish evidence from values: *where sources overlap and where
they split* is evidence; *that we should be honest about both* is a value we hold. See
`docs/philosophy.md` §5 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall last lesson: two sources that agree are stronger than one. But
   what if they do *not* agree? Today you learn what to do.
2. **Meet the overlap and the gap (12 min).** Look at the poster. **Source A:** "oceans cover
   about 71% of Earth." **Source B:** "oceans cover about 70% of Earth." **Where they agree:**
   about seven-tenths of Earth is ocean. **Where they disagree:** the exact number — 71 or 70.
   **Verdict:** likely true — oceans cover roughly seven-tenths; the exact number is still
   open. Notice you did not throw the claim away because of a tiny difference, and you did not
   pretend the difference was not there.
3. **Practice with a partner (20 min).** Take a source-pair card. One pair is about how much
   sleep children need (Source A says about 9 hours; Source B says 9–11 hours). Another is
   about whether eating breakfast helps school focus (Source A says "studies show it helps";
   Source B says "the evidence is mixed"). On your page, fill the overlap (where they agree)
   and the gap (where they disagree), then write a verdict. When the gap matters, write "not
   sure yet — I would check a third source."
4. **Peer check (8 min).** Swap pages. Ask: "Did you name both the overlap and the gap? Does
   the verdict match — or honestly say 'not sure yet'?"
5. **Close (5 min).** Disagreement is information, not a problem. Note it, name it, and when
   it matters, say "not sure yet."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card where the overlap is partly filled in and a verdict sentence
   starter; work one pair aloud first.
- **Extension:** Find a third source on the topic and explain whether it tips the verdict
   toward "likely true" or keeps it "still open."

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name where two sources agree, where they
   disagree, and give a verdict that respects both — including "not sure yet" when needed?
- **Peer check:** Partners ask each other, "Did you name *both* the overlap and the gap, and
   did you say 'not sure yet' when the gap mattered?"

## Home connection

When two people at home tell you slightly different versions of the same event, name what they
agree on and where they differ — and ask whether the difference matters enough to check a third
source.

## Resources

- Oceans cover roughly 70–71% of Earth's surface — a figure that appears in slightly different
  forms across references (NASA, USGS, NOAA), which is exactly why it makes a good worked
  example of agreement-with-a-small-gap. Children's sleep recommendations are given as a range
  by health bodies (for example, WHO and national sleep foundations), and the breakfast–focus
  question is genuinely "mixed evidence" in the research literature — useful real cases where
  honest sources differ. The "overlap and gap" routine follows the standard's "noting where
  they agree or disagree"; on modeling before practice, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1>.
