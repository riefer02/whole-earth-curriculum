# Lesson 3 — Traits Pass from Parents to Offspring

## Summary

Learners explain **inheritance**: how **traits** (features like coat color, ear
shape, kernel color, or leaf shape) pass from **parents** to **offspring** through
instructions carried in the parents' cells. They match offspring to their parents
and gather evidence that offspring resemble their parents — in every culture and
ecosystem on Earth.

## Objectives

- Describe how traits pass from parents to offspring. _(D06.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

A litter of puppies looks like their parents, and a field of maize grows plants that
look like the maize that came before. This has been noticed by people everywhere for
thousands of years: rice farmers across Asia, maize growers in the Americas, and
cattle herders across Africa and the Middle East all selected seeds and animals with
traits they valued, because they knew those traits would pass to the young. Today
you learn the *how* behind what every farmer and herder already knew.

## Materials

- Traits-from-parents diagram
- Family trait cards
- My traits page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the traits-from-parents diagram and copy a trait page per learner.
- Prepare family trait cards (a parent pair + several offspring to match).
- Have real seeds or seedlings on hand if available.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that offspring **receive the
instructions for their traits from both parents** — so they resemble their parents,
yet are not copies. The child-friendly word is "instructions"; the scientific name is
**genes**, and Grade 8 will open that box fully. Keep it faithful and simple: a trait
passes from a parent to an offspring through the parent's cells, not through the
parent "teaching" it or the offspring "wanting" it. The claim that offspring resemble
their parents is **evidence** gathered over millennia; the claim that we ought to
respect every living thing regardless of its traits is a **value** — name that
distinction if it comes up ([`docs/facilitation.md`](../../../../../../docs/facilitation.md)).
Avoid comparing humans in ways that rank or shame; use plants and animals for the
worked examples, and let learners share their own family traits only if they wish.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of an animal or plant family you have seen — a litter,
   a nest, a garden. What do the young share with their parents? What you just named
   are **traits**.
2. **Meet the diagram (10 min).** Look at the parents. The mother has **floppy ears**
   and a **brown coat**; the father has **pointed ears** and a **spotted coat**. The
   puppy has **floppy ears** (from the mother) and a **spotted coat** (from the
   father). Each parent passed some **instructions** for traits to the offspring. The
   scientific name for those instructions is **genes**.
3. **How do we know? (10 min).** People did not need microscopes to notice this: for
   thousands of years, farmers and herders chose seeds and animals with useful traits
   and saw those traits appear again in the next generation. That repeated pattern is
   **evidence**. Ask: what is the difference between "offspring resemble their
   parents" (evidence we can see) and "so traits are carried in instructions" (an
   inference we make to explain it)?
4. **Match offspring to parents (15 min).** With a partner, look at the family trait
   cards. Match each offspring to its parent pair, and name at least one trait each
   offspring inherited. Check your match: could this offspring have come from the
   other parents? Why or why not?
5. **Close (5 min).** Every living thing you have ever seen got its traits the same
   way — instructions passed from two parents. Resemblance is not magic; it is
   inheritance, and it happens in a rice paddy, a coral reef, a savanna, and in you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two very different parent pictures and one obvious trait; provide
  a sentence frame: "The puppy got its ___ from its ___."
- **Extension:** List three traits in one organism and decide, with a partner, which
  likely came from each parent, then explain why resemblance is evidence of
  inheritance.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one trait an offspring inherited
  from each parent, and explain that instructions (genes) carry the trait?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to one trait in an offspring and
  say which parent it came from?"

## Home connection

Look at a family of plants or animals near you — or a photo of your own family. Name
one trait the young share with a parent, and one way each is still their own.
