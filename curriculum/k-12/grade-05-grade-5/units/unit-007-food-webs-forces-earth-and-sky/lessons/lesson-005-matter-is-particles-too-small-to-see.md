# Lesson 5 — Matter Is Made of Particles Too Small to See

## Summary

Learners explain that everything is made of **particles** too small to see, and use
this model to explain the three states of matter: in a **solid** particles are packed
and hold shape, in a **liquid** they slide past each other, and in a **gas** they
spread far apart. Heat makes particles move more (solid → liquid → gas); cooling
makes them move less. Learners separate what they can *see* from the particle model
they *infer*.

## Objectives

- Explain that matter is made of particles too small to see, and use this idea to
  explain changes of state. _(D06.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

Ghee melts in a warm pan. Ice melts in a hot afternoon. Water in a pot boils away
into steam, and a wet cloth dries in the wind. These happen in every kitchen and
market on Earth. You cannot see *why* they happen, because the reason is made of
**particles too small to see**. Today you learn the model scientists use to explain
what your eyes cannot.

## Materials

- Particle-model diagram
- A small sealed cup or bottle of water and a spoon
- Particle-drawing page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the particle-model diagram and a drawing page per learner.
- Have stones, seeds, or beads ready for the low-tech model.
- Recall Grade 3: the three states of matter and how heating and cooling change them.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). The heart of this lesson is the distinction between
**evidence** and **model**: we cannot see particles, so the particle picture is an
**inference** — a model we build to explain what we *can* see (melting, evaporating,
smells spreading, sugar dissolving). Say this plainly; it is the "how do we know"
through-line. The particle model is well-supported science, but it is still a model,
not a photograph. The body-and-stones activities make the abstract concrete before
learners draw it (concrete-before-abstract). Watch for one slip: "particles grow" or
"particles turn into air" — they do not; the *same* particles move farther apart or
closer together. Grade 3 met the states; Grade 6 will add atoms and elements
([`docs/facilitation.md`](../../../../../../docs/facilitation.md)).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall Grade 3: solids keep their shape, liquids flow, gases
   spread out. Now ask a deeper question: what is everything actually made of? Your
   guess is a model in the making.
2. **Meet the model (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Every bit of matter — ice,
   water, air, you — is made of tiny **particles** too small to see. In a **solid**
   they are packed close and hold their shape. In a **liquid** they are close but
   slide past each other, so the liquid pours. In a **gas** they are far apart and
   spread out to fill the space.
3. **What heat does (10 min).** **Add heat**, and particles move more: a solid's
   particles shake loose into a liquid (**melting**), and a liquid's particles fly
   apart into a gas (**evaporating/boiling**). **Take heat away**, and the reverse
   happens: gas → liquid (**condensing**), liquid → solid (**freezing**). The
   particles themselves stay the same — only their motion and spacing change.
4. **Model it with your body (10 min).** Stand shoulder-to-shoulder (solid). Now
   slide gently past each other but stay close (liquid). Now spread far apart and
   move freely (gas). Feel the difference. Or, with stones: pack them, loosen them,
   scatter them.
5. **Draw and check (15 min).** On your drawing page, draw the particles for a
   solid, a liquid, and a gas, and label each with a real example (ice, water,
   steam). Check against the diagram. Then ask: did you ever *see* the particles, or
   are you *inferring* them from what happens? Which is evidence, which is model?
6. **Close (5 min).** You cannot see the particles, but they explain everything you
   can see — why ghee melts, why water boils, why a puddle dries. A model is a way
   of thinking that lets us see with our minds what our eyes are too big to catch.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the stones model first and provide a pre-drawn outline with
  particle dots to finish; describe each state aloud as learners move the stones.
- **Extension:** Explain melting and boiling as "the particles gain enough motion to
  break free," and predict what would happen if you kept cooling a gas below its
  freezing point.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner draw and name the particle arrangement
  for each state, and explain that heat increases particle motion?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I draw the *same* particles moving apart or
  together — not changing into something else — and can I name one change of state?"

## Home connection

Watch water in a pot heat to a boil (with an adult) or watch a wet cloth dry. Tell
someone what the particles are doing that you cannot see.
