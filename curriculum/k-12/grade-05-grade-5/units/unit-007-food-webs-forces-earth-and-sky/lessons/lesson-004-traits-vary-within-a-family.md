# Lesson 4 — Traits Vary Within a Family

## Summary

Learners explain **variation**: offspring of the same parents share family traits but
are not identical — each is a unique mix. They observe real variation (in seeds,
leaves, or a litter) and connect it to the value that difference is ordinary and
worthy of respect, in living things and in people.

## Objectives

- Describe how traits pass from parents to offspring and how some traits vary within
  a family. _(D06.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

Open a pod of beans or a handful of rice and look closely: the seeds all come from
the same parent kind, and they all look alike in the big ways — yet each one is a
little different in size, shade, or shape. The same is true of a litter of puppies, a
clutch of chicks, and a human family. Brothers and sisters share a family, and still
each one is their own. That ordinary difference is called **variation**, and it
appears in every family of living things on Earth.

## Materials

- Variation-in-a-family diagram
- A handful of seeds or beans of one kind
- Variation observation page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the variation diagram and a table page per learner.
- Gather a handful of seeds, beans, or leaves of one kind per pair.
- Have the Lesson 3 diagram ready to recall how traits pass.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson lands two ideas at once: **variation is
real and normal** (evidence), and **every individual deserves respect regardless of
its traits** (a value we hold). Keep those separate and say so. The scientific claim —
offspring of the same parents vary because each receives a different mix of the
parents' instructions — is sound at this level; the fuller mechanism (genes shuffling
and combining) is Grade 8. Be careful with human comparisons: use seeds, leaves, and
animals for the evidence, and let learners share their own family traits only if they
choose. This is the "vary within a family" half of the standard; it sets up Grade 7's
work on variation and natural selection ([`docs/facilitation.md`](../../../../../../docs/facilitation.md)).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall Lesson 3: traits pass from parents to offspring. Now
   ask: do two brothers and sisters in one family look exactly alike? Why do you
   think they differ? That difference is **variation**.
2. **Meet the diagram (10 min).** Look at the family. All the puppies have the same
   parents — so they share the family's general shape. But look again: one has a
   solid coat, one a spotted coat, one a patchy coat, and one has slightly different
   ears. Same family, different mixes. That is variation.
3. **Observe real variation (15 min).** Take your handful of seeds or beans (all of
   one kind). Sort them, then record on your table two ways they are **alike** and
   two ways they **differ**. Are any two exactly the same? Talk with a partner: how
   do you know they all belong to the same kind even though they differ?
4. **Why it matters (10 min).** Variation is not a mistake — it is normal. Because
   offspring differ, some may cope a little better when conditions change (a colder
   year, a drier season). That is an **inference** from what we observe, not
   something we can watch happen in one afternoon. Discuss: what is the difference
   between "these seeds differ" (evidence) and "some differences may help them
   survive" (inference)?
5. **Close (5 min).** Every family — of beans, of puppies, of people — holds both
   sameness and difference. The sameness is inheritance; the difference is
   variation. Both are ordinary, and both deserve respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort seeds by one trait only (size), and use the sentence frame "They
  are alike because ___, and different because ___."
- **Extension:** Measure one trait (length of seeds) and make a simple ordered line
  to show the range of variation, then predict how that range could help in a change
  of conditions.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner name one way siblings in a family
  are alike and one way they differ, and explain that variation is normal?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I tell the difference between 'these differ'
  (evidence) and 'differences may help survival' (inference)?"

## Home connection

Look at a family you know — people, pets, or plants — and name one trait the family
shares and one way the members differ. Ask an adult whether they think the
differences matter, and why.
