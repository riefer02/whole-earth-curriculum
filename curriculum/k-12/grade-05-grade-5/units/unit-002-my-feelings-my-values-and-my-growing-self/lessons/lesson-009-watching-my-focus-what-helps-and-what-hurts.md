# Lesson 9 — Watching My Focus: What Helps and What Hurts

## Summary

Learners practice **monitoring their own attention** during a real task. They learn that
focus has **helpers** (quiet, a plan, water, a short break) and **hurts** (noise, hunger,
a ringing phone, a wandering thought), and they stop at checkpoints to notice where their
attention has gone and name what helped or hurt it — turning self-awareness into a tool
they can use for the rest of their learning.

## Objectives

- Monitor their own behavior and feelings during a task and name what helped or hurt
  their focus. _(D01.S3.05.01)_

## Connection

You sit down to a task, and for a while your mind is right there — and then it is not.
Maybe a phone buzzes, maybe your stomach is empty, maybe the room is loud, or maybe a
worry tugs at you. Some of those things **help** your focus (a quiet corner, a clear
plan, a glass of water) and some **hurt** it (noise, hunger, interruptions, tiredness).
Learning to *notice* which is which — in a classroom, at a market stall, or under a tree
— is a skill you can use everywhere, for your whole life.

## Materials

- Focus monitor chart
- Blank focus-monitor page
- A short, quiet task

## Preparation

- Print or draw the focus monitor chart (two columns: helps / hurts, with picture
  prompts).
- Copy a focus-monitor page for each learner.
- Choose a simple 10-minute task all learners can do, and prepare checkpoint signals.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **monitoring, not
judging**: learners notice where attention goes and name helpers and hurts, without being
called "lazy" or "unfocused." Normalize wandering attention — everyone's mind wanders;
the skill is *noticing* and *returning*. Include bodily hurts (hunger, thirst, tiredness)
alongside environmental ones (noise, devices) — many learners cannot remove a hunger or a
noisy home, and the goal is *naming* what affects them, not blaming them for it. The
**technology lens** invites a real question: when do devices help focus, and when do they
hurt it? Let learners answer from their own experience. See `docs/contexts.md` on
self-paced, low-tech structures.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have grown your skills all unit — noticing thoughts, memories,
   bodies, values, and identity. Now a skill for *doing*: noticing your own **focus**.
2. **Meet helps and hurts (10 min).** Look at the chart. Two columns: **what helps my
   focus** (a quiet spot, a clear plan, a glass of water, a short break) and **what hurts
   it** (noise, hunger, a ringing phone, tiredness, a wandering worry). Some of these you
   can change; some you can only notice and work around. Naming them is the first step.
3. **Try a task and watch (20 min).** Begin the short task. Every few minutes, at the
   signal, pause and ask yourself: *Where is my attention right now? What helped it stay?
   What pulled it away?* Jot one word on your focus-monitor page. Then return to the
   task. Repeat until the task is done.
4. **Name your helpers and hurts (10 min).** Look at your page. Which things appeared in
   the "helped" column? Which in the "hurt" column? Write or say your **top helper** and
   your **top distractor**. With a partner, share one thing you could change to help your
   own focus next time.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your attention is like a lantern — it swings. Noticing where it
   swings, without blaming yourself, is how you learn to guide it back.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a two-card signal (green "on task" / red "wandered") at checkpoints
  instead of writing; scribe the top helper and distractor.
- **Extension:** Design a short "focus plan" — remove one distraction, add one helper —
  and try it on the spot, then note any difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner, after a task, name at least one thing
  that helped and one that hurt their focus?
- **Self-check:** The learner compares their page to what actually happened and asks,
  "Did I name it truthfully?" and adjusts.

## Home connection

Try a short task at home and notice one helper and one hurt — then share them with
someone.
