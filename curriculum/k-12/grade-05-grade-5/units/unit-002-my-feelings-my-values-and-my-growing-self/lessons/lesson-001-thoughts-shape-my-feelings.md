# Lesson 1 — Thoughts Shape My Feelings

## Summary

Learners discover that a feeling is not caused *only* by what happens, but also by the
**thought** they have about it. Using a **thought–feeling chain** (event → thought →
feeling), they trace a real event of their own and then swap in a second thought to see
how the feeling changes. This is the first of three "shapers" they will meet this unit:
thoughts, memories, and body sensations.

## Objectives

- Explain how thoughts, memories, and body sensations can shape a feeling.
  _(D01.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

The same rain can fall on two neighbors and mean two different things. One thinks,
"Our game is ruined," and feels **disappointed**. The other thinks, "The garden needs
this water," and feels **relieved**. Same rain, different thoughts, different feelings.
Whether you live in a busy city or on a mountain farm, the *thoughts you carry* about an
event are part of what makes the feeling. Learning to notice your thoughts is like
learning to read the weather inside you.

## Materials

- Thought–feeling chain poster
- Blank thought–feeling chain page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the thought–feeling chain poster (event → thought → feeling, with two
  different thoughts for the same event).
- Copy a blank chain page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and a quiet space for individual work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **thoughts
are one shaper of feelings** — not that a learner's feelings are "wrong" or that they
should simply "think positive." Present the second thought as *another real way of
seeing the same event*, never as the "correct" one. Some learners carry heavy events
(loss, illness, uncertainty); do not push them to reframe something that genuinely
hurts. The skill is *noticing* the thought, with gentleness. See `docs/facilitation.md`
on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned that feelings have a strength — a little,
   some, or a lot. Today you learn something new: feelings are also **shaped** by the
   thoughts you have. The same event can feel very different depending on what you think
   about it.
2. **Meet the chain (10 min).** Look at the poster. It shows a chain: **event →
   thought → feeling**. Here is a worked example. *Event:* it rains all afternoon.
   *Thought one:* "Our game is ruined." *Feeling one:* disappointed. Now a second
   thought about the *same* event: "The garden needs this water." *Feeling two:*
   relieved. Same event, two different thoughts, two different feelings. This is what
   "a thought shapes a feeling" means.
3. **Trace a real event (15 min).** Think of one small, safe event from your week — a
   test, a game, a visit, a chore. On your chain page, fill the three boxes: what
   happened (**event**), what you told yourself about it (**thought**), and what you
   felt (**feeling**). Check it with a partner: does the chain make sense? Could the
   same event have led to a different thought for someone else?
4. **Swap the thought (10 min).** Try a **second, true** thought about the same event —
   not a made-up one, but another real way of seeing it. Write it and the feeling it
   would lead to. Notice: the event stayed the same; your feeling shifted with the
   thought.
5. **Close (5 min).** You are not ruled only by what happens to you — the thoughts you
   notice are part of the story too. Noticing them is the first step to understanding
   yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer event picture cards and a word bank of thoughts and feelings to
  arrange into a chain; scribe the learner's words.
- **Extension:** Trace a second event where a thought *intensified* a feeling, and
  explore what thought might soften it, without judging either as wrong.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state an event, a thought, and the
  feeling it produced, and suggest a second thought that would change the feeling?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their chain back and asks, "Is this really what I
  thought and felt?" and adjusts if not.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time the same event meant something different to two people
— and share one thought that changed your own feeling today.
