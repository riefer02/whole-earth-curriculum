# Lesson 8 — My Identity Map and Every Family's Shape

## Summary

Learners assemble the threads from Lesson 7 into a full **identity map**: a center "me"
surrounded by many connected bubbles — family, culture, place, history, language,
interests, and more. They explicitly honor that **every family shape** (two parents, one
parent, grandparents, adoptive, chosen, blended) and **every place and history** belongs,
and they celebrate the many different maps in the room as a shared strength.

## Objectives

- Explain how a person's identity is shaped by family, culture, place, and history.
  _(D01.S2.05.02)_

## Connection

Draw a dot in the middle and call it **me**. Now draw a ring of bubbles around it and
fill each with a part of you — the people who care for you, the language you speak, the
food you eat, the place you call home, the story of how you got here. That ring is your
**identity map**. No two maps are alike, and no map is "more complete" than another — a
child raised by grandparents, a child in a blended family, and a child who moved across
the sea each has a map that is whole and true.

## Materials

- Identity map poster
- Blank identity map page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the identity map poster (center "me," ring of connected bubbles).
- Copy a blank identity map page for each learner.
- Arrange a space for a gallery walk at the end.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **identity has
many connected parts, and every family's shape, place, and history is whole and worthy**.
This is the unit's deepest "honor every family" moment — be explicit that families come in
many shapes: two parents, one parent, grandparents, adoptive or foster parents, siblings,
chosen family, and blended families, and that all belong. Model inclusivity naturally
(mention adoption and moving as ordinary, not "special cases"). If a learner reveals a
loss, receive it with calm care; never require detail. The gallery walk should celebrate
*difference*, never compare maps as better or worse. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/art-style.md` on representing many kinds of family.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you met four shapers of you. Today you draw the whole
   map — the many parts that make you, you.
2. **Meet the map (10 min).** Look at the poster: a center **me**, with a ring of bubbles
   connected to it — my family, my culture, my place, my history, my language, my
   interests. Each bubble is a part of your whole self. Here is a worked example: one
   child's map has "my grandmother" (family), "my two languages" (culture), "the village
   by the river" (place), "we moved last year" (history), and "football" (interests).
   Every bubble is a real thread.
3. **Draw your map (20 min).** On your page, write **me** in the center, then draw your
   own bubbles and fill each one. Add any part that matters — a friend, a faith, an
   ability, a dream. Your map is yours; there is no "right" way to be a family or to
   belong.
4. **Gallery walk (10 min).** Walk quietly and look at the maps around the room. Notice:
   how many different family shapes? How many places? How many languages? Each map is
   whole and true. Find one thing on someone else's map you admire.
5. **Close (5 min).** You are many parts, one whole self — and every person's map
   deserves honor. Carry that with you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide pre-drawn bubbles with picture prompts to fill in; scribe labels
  as needed.
- **Extension:** Connect two bubbles with a line and write one sentence about how those
  two parts of you meet in your life (e.g., "my language and my family meet at our
  holiday meals").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner produce a map with several connected parts
  and name how at least one part shapes who they are?
- **Portfolio:** The identity map, kept as a growing artifact to revisit later in the
  year.

## Home connection

Show someone at home your map and invite them to add one bubble you may not have thought
of — a story, a place, a person, or a dream.
