# Lesson 4 — How Cultures and Situations Shape Feeling Expression

## Summary

Learners widen the view from families to **cultures and situations**. They explore
respectful greetings from around the world — a bow, a handshake, a hug, a raised hand, a
nod — and discover that **where and when** a feeling happens shapes how it is shown. The
same joy is danced at a festival and held quietly at a ceremony. Learners complete a
**two-situation comparison** for one feeling, always holding every custom as worthy of
respect and none as "better."

## Objectives

- Compare how feelings are expressed in different families, cultures, and situations.
  _(D01.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

Think of a single feeling — **joy**. At a festival you might dance, shout, and throw your
arms wide. At a quiet ceremony for someone important, that same joy might be shown with a
small smile and folded hands. Same feeling, different situation, different expression.
Around the world, people also greet each other in many respectful ways — a bow, a
handshake, a raised hand to the heart. Every custom makes sense in its own place. Knowing
this helps you meet people unlike yourself with **curiosity and respect, not judgment**.

## Materials

- Greetings-around-the-world cards
- Blank situation–expression page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the greetings-around-the-world cards (bow, handshake, hug, raised hand,
  nod).
- Copy a blank situation–expression page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle with room to practice gestures.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **non-ranking
comparison across cultures and situations**. This is a place where stereotypes can slip
in ("those people are loud," "those people hide their feelings") — model instead a
posture of *wonder*: "In this place, that custom shows respect." If a learner offers a
custom from their own culture, receive it warmly and let them be the authority on its
meaning; never correct a learner about their own culture. Distinguish *how* a feeling is
shown from *whether* it is felt — the feeling is real in every case. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on honoring cultural contexts without ranking.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you saw that different *families* show the same feeling
   in different ways. Today the view gets wider: different **cultures** and different
   **situations** shape how a feeling is shown — and every way deserves respect.
2. **Meet the greetings (10 min).** Look at the cards: a bow, a handshake, a hug, a
   raised hand to the heart, a nod. Each is a respectful way to greet someone, from a
   different part of the world. Practice each one gently. Think: in what situation would
   each fit? Why might a person bow in one place and shake hands in another? (Both show
   respect — the *form* differs.)
3. **Same feeling, two situations (15 min).** Here is a worked example. *Feeling:* joy.
   *Situation one:* a festival — people dance and cheer. *Situation two:* a solemn
   ceremony — the same joy is shown with a small smile and quiet hands. Now choose your
   own feeling — joy, sadness, or respect. On your page, draw or write **one feeling, two
   situations, and two different ways of showing it.**
4. **Compare with a partner (10 min).** Share your page. Ask: is the feeling real in
   both situations? (Yes.) Does the situation change only *how* it is shown? (Yes.) Did
   anyone judge another way as "wrong"? Catch it if so — and gently reframe it as "just
   different."
5. **Close (5 min).** The same heart can show itself in a hundred ways, each fitting its
   own place and moment. Meeting those ways with respect is how we make room for one
   another.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a set of illustrated "ways to show it" to sort into the two
  situations rather than drawing; scribe the one-sentence comparison.
- **Extension:** Add a third situation for the same feeling, or recall a second culture's
  greeting and explain the situation where it belongs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe how one feeling is shown
  differently across two situations or cultures, without ranking one as better?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their comparison and checks that it names the
  feeling as real in both situations.

## Home connection

Ask a family member or neighbor about a respectful greeting or custom from a culture
they know, and try learning to show it.

## Resources
