# Lesson 1 — Reading and Writing Decimals to Thousandths

## Summary

Learners read and write decimals to the thousandths. They build numbers on a
**place-value chart**, name each place (**tenths, hundredths, thousandths**), and write
the same number three ways — **standard form** (3.456), **word form** ("three and four
hundred fifty-six thousandths"), and **expanded form** (3 + 4/10 + 5/100 + 6/1000). They
see that the decimal point is just a marker between whole ones and the parts smaller than
one.

## Objectives

- Read, write, and expand decimals to the thousandths and name the value of each place.
  _(D05.S1.05.01)_

## Connection

You already know whole numbers — ones, tens, hundreds. Now think about one single
**chapatis or flatbread** torn into ten equal strips. Each strip is **one tenth** of the
bread. Tear one strip into ten again and you get **one hundredth**; tear again and you get
**one thousandth**. Decimals are how we write those small parts next to whole ones — and
people use them every day for money, medicine, measurements, and weather. In many parts of
the world the decimal point is written as a **comma** instead (3,456), but the idea is the
same: a marker between whole ones and parts of one.

## Materials

- Place-value chart to thousandths
- Digit cards (0–9) and a decimal-point card
- Decimal recording page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the place-value chart (ones · tenths · hundredths · thousandths).
- Copy digit-card sets for pairs and a recording page for each learner.
- Have two or three example numbers ready to build (3.456, 0.205, 12.7).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a decimal point
separates whole ones from parts smaller than one**, and that each place to the right is ten
times smaller — **tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths**. Because this is a foundational
skill, use **explicit instruction and worked examples** before independent practice
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model building 3.456 step by step, name each place, then
guide a second number together before learners work alone. Watch for two common slips: reading
"3.456" as "three point four five six" *only* (also teach the place-value reading "three and
four hundred fifty-six thousandths"), and skipping the value of a zero (in 3.05, the zero
*holds* the tenths place — there are 0 tenths). Note the global point: many countries use a
comma as the decimal separator and a space or period for thousands — this is a difference of
convention, not of correctness. The number facts here are evidence; the claim that no system
is "better" is a value we hold (`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of the smallest amount of money you know. A whole coin or bill
   is "one." What about a part of it? Today you learn how to read and write parts of one
   using **decimals**.
2. **Meet the chart (10 min).** Look at the place-value chart. To the left of the decimal
   point are **whole ones**; to the right are **parts**: **tenths** (1/10), **hundredths**
   (1/100), **thousandths** (1/1000). Each place to the right is ten times smaller than the
   one before it.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Build **3.456** together. Put a 3 in the ones column, a 4 in
   the tenths, a 5 in the hundredths, and a 6 in the thousandths. Read it two ways: "three
   point four five six," and "three and four hundred fifty-six thousandths." Write it in
   **expanded form**: 3 + 4/10 + 5/100 + 6/1000. Notice the 4 means 4 tenths, the 5 means 5
   hundredths, the 6 means 6 thousandths.
4. **Build your own (15 min).** With a partner, build **0.205** and **12.7**. For each, say
   each digit's place and value aloud, then write it in standard, word, and expanded form.
   Check: in 0.205, what is the 0 in the hundredths place doing? (Holding the place — there are 0
   hundredths.)
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the decimal point is just a marker. Every digit in a decimal
   has a name and a value, and now you can read and write them all the way to the thousandths.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start with tenths and hundredths only; use a place-value chart with the value
  of each column printed in it. Describe each place aloud and trace the digits with a finger
  so learners with low vision can follow by listening and touch.
- **Extension:** Write decimals with a zero in a middle place (3.05, 0.040) and explain the
  zero's role; extend the chart one more place to ten-thousandths and name it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner place digits of a decimal to the
  thousandths and name each place's value?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I read 3.456 two ways, and can I write any decimal to
  thousandths in expanded form?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home where they see decimals — on a receipt, a scale, a medicine label, a
weather report — and read one decimal aloud together.

## Resources

- Place value with decimals is standard in elementary mathematics; see John A. Van de Walle,
  *Elementary and Middle School Mathematics* (10th ed., 2019) for place-value teaching models.
  The note that a comma serves as the decimal separator in many countries is a documented
  convention (ISO 80000-1, which stipulates the decimal sign is "either a comma or a point on
  the line," and national standards); treating all conventions as valid is a value commitment
  (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
