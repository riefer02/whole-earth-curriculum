# Lesson 3 — Adding and Subtracting Decimals with Money

## Summary

Learners **add** and **subtract decimals** by lining up the **decimal points** and working
place by place, **regrouping** when needed. They practice in a **market context** — totaling
purchases and giving change with money — and check that every answer makes sense with the
amounts they started with.

## Objectives

- Add and subtract decimals in a real money context, lining up place values and regrouping as
  needed. _(D05.S1.05.02)_

## Connection

You are at a market with 5.00. You buy rice for **2.50** and fruit for **1.75**. How much do
you pay, and how much **change** do you get back? Adding and subtracting decimals is exactly
the math of buying and selling — the same skill a seller uses to give the right change in a
market in Lagos, a shop in Lima, or a floating market in Bangkok. Money works in many
currencies, but the place-value idea — line up the points, work place by place — is the same
everywhere.

## Materials

- Market price tag cards
- Decimal money recording page
- Play money or paper coins

## Preparation

- Copy price tag cards for pairs and the recording page for each learner.
- Set up play money or paper coins for groups.
- Have a worked example ready: 2.50 + 1.75 = 4.25; change from 5.00.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to carry is **aligning the decimal
points** so tenths add to tenths and hundredths to hundredths, then **regrouping** exactly as
with whole numbers (ten hundredths make a tenth; ten tenths make a one). Because this is a
foundational skill, **model a worked example step by step**, then guide a second together
before independent practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Common slips to watch for:
lining up numbers on the right edge instead of at the decimal point, and forgetting to write
the decimal point in the answer. Retrieval: ask learners to recall last lesson's rounding rule
and reuse it to estimate ("about 2.50 + 1.75 ≈ 4.25, so the answer should be near 4"). Using
real local currency is fine; keep the math generic so it works with any currency, and avoid
assuming one currency is the "default." The arithmetic is evidence; the habit of giving exact
change so no one is cheated is a fairness value (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall: how do you round 4.36 to the nearest tenth? Today you use
   decimals to add and subtract — the math of money.
2. **The rule (5 min).** When you add or subtract decimals, **line up the decimal points**,
   then work place by place, just like whole numbers. The decimal point in the answer sits
   right under the others.
3. **Worked example — add (10 min).** A customer buys rice (**2.50**) and fruit (**1.75**).
   Write 2.50 and 1.75 with the points lined up. Add hundredths: 0 + 5 = 5. Add tenths: 5 + 7
   = 12 tenths → write 2, carry 1 one. Add ones: 2 + 1 + 1 = 4. The total is **4.25**.
4. **Worked example — subtract (10 min).** The customer pays **5.00**. Subtract 4.25 to find
   the change. Line up the points: 5.00 − 4.25. Hundredths: borrow from the tenths — 10 − 5 =
   5. Tenths: 9 − 2 = 7. Ones: 4 − 4 = 0. The change is **0.75**.
5. **Practice at the market (15 min).** With a partner, pick two or three price tags, total
   them, and find the change from an amount you choose. Use play money to act it out, then
   check each other's work — did you line up the points? Is the change right?
6. **Close (5 min).** Adding and subtracting decimals is a daily-life skill. When the points
   line up, the math is as easy as whole numbers — and you can always estimate first to check
   your answer is sensible.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use amounts with only tenths (2.5, 1.7) and no regrouping at first; keep the
  place-value chart visible and describe each step aloud. Provide tactile markers so learners
  with low vision can follow.
- **Extension:** Solve multi-item totals with several regroupings, estimate the change
  mentally first, and compare your estimate to the exact answer.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner line up decimal points, add and subtract
  with regrouping, and give correct change in a market task?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I line up the decimal points, and does my answer make
  sense for the amounts I started with?"

## Home connection

Next time something is bought, help figure out the total or the change, and line up the
decimal points to do it.
