# Grade 10 Scope

## How to read this scope

Each `unit_id` in the frontmatter resolves to a unit file under `units/`.
`start_day`/`end_day` map the unit onto the 180-day school year; the list is a
continuous sequence from day 1 to day 180 with no gaps or overlaps. `domain` and
`pillar` name the primary domain(s) and pillar(s) each unit serves. `domain_weighting`
expresses the intended balance of instructional time across the ten domains, in
percent of the year. `lesson_count` counts the core lessons; the days between lessons
carry flex time, assessment, integration, and celebration. `assessment_plan`
describes how a learner's growth is seen and recorded at this level.

## Year at a glance

The Grade 10 year is a spiral that deepens what Grade 9 began — and it is the year
the learner becomes a full co-author, not only of their education but of their
considered position in the world. Where Grade 9 learned to *synthesize and hold
competing truths* — to build a claim while naming its limits, to reason across more
than one ethical framework, to model the world with functions and prove with
coordinates — Grade 10 learns to *examine, defend, and act on* what it synthesized:
to examine identity as socially and historically constructed, to critique rhetoric
and moral arguments for hidden assumptions, to extend number and model complex
systems, to face the hardest truths of history and the environment with intellectual
tools and agency, never nihilism. The year opens with three inward units that
consolidate the self, the body, and the community.

*Feelings, Identity & Purpose* (U.10.001, D01) opens the year where Grade 9's
consolidation left off. Learners explain how personal history and culture shape the
way a person experiences and names emotion (D01.S1.10.01) and evaluate how technology
and media influence emotional life — online comparison, empathy, and emotional
contagion (D01.S1.10.02). They examine how identity is socially and historically
constructed, comparing examples across cultures (D01.S2.10.01), and develop a
considered position on what defines them, independent of external labels and
expectations (D01.S2.10.02). They direct their own learning by choosing goals,
resources, and ways to check their own progress (D01.S3.10.01), and balance
independence with knowing when and how to seek help, acting on that judgment
(D01.S3.10.02). Every self is honored as a whole in the making, never a label, and
every question is held as a question.

*Body, Wellness & Movement* (U.10.002, D03) reads the body as the somatic ground of
thought and feeling and as a system shaped by habit. Learners analyze how chronic
stress, sleep, and movement shape mood, thinking, and long-term health
(D03.S1.10.01), and explain how different cultures and times have understood the
body–mind connection, assessing what evidence supports each (D03.S1.10.02). They
apply principles of training — progressive effort and recovery — to build a safe and
sustainable movement plan (D03.S2.10.01), and evaluate how technology such as
fitness trackers and apps supports or distorts a healthy relationship with movement
and the body (D03.S2.10.02). They evaluate popular diets and nutrition claims using
scientific evidence, without shaming any body (D03.S3.10.01), and explain the
environmental footprint of different foods and diets and how food choices affect
climate and ecosystems (D03.S3.10.02). The body is taught with dignity and joy,
honoring the wide range of bodies and abilities.

*Relationships, Community & Mutual Aid* (U.10.003, D02) turns outward into the
practice of care in a connected world, where peers and belonging matter most.
Learners evaluate how technology and social media shape communication and use
digital tools respectfully (D02.S1.10.01), and handle conflict through
de-escalation, seeking mediation when a conflict is too big to solve alone
(D02.S1.10.02). They practice cross-cultural empathy by studying a community's
history and values from within (D02.S2.10.01), and evaluate how digital platforms
shape empathy, compassion fatigue, and echo chambers (D02.S2.10.02). They evaluate
how technology and media create and transform communities and belonging
(D02.S3.10.01), explain how local communities connect to regional, national, and
global ones (D02.S3.10.02), analyze how technology can enable or undermine
cooperation and mutual aid globally (D02.S4.10.01), and examine examples of
cooperative economies and mutual-aid networks across the world (D02.S4.10.02).

The heart of the year is given to the three great tools of school, now wielded at
full strength: language, number, and science. *Language, Literature & Argument*
(U.10.004, D04) grows from Grade 9's synthesis and authorship into *criticism and
craft*. Learners critique rhetoric in public speech and media, distinguishing
emotional appeal from reasoned argument (D04.S1.10.01), and speak to a public or
unfamiliar audience with confidence, adapting register and formality to the setting
(D04.S1.10.02). They critically evaluate digital and print media for bias, omission,
and the difference between evidence and belief (D04.S2.10.01), and read extended,
complex literary and informational texts, tracking themes and arguments across a
full work (D04.S2.10.02). They craft prose with deliberate style, varying sentence
structure, word choice, and tone to suit purpose and audience (D04.S3.10.01), and
compose multimodal texts that combine writing, image, sound, and design with
attention to accessibility (D04.S3.10.02). They analyze how language and power
interact — whose language is used in law, medicine, and school (D04.S4.10.01) — and
communicate respectfully in a multicultural setting, adapting to different cultural
norms of politeness and address (D04.S4.10.02), always home language first, with
multilingualism held as wealth. *Mathematics & Reasoning* (U.10.005, D05) grows from
Grade 9's functions and proof into the next rung of modeling. Learners use
properties of exponents and roots to simplify expressions and solve problems
involving exponential growth and decay (D05.S1.10.01), and extend number reasoning
to complex numbers, explaining why new kinds of numbers arise from real problems
(D05.S1.10.02). They build and interpret polynomial and rational functions that
model real relationships and describe their key features (D05.S2.10.01), and model
exponential growth and decay in population, finance, or environmental systems,
explaining the effect of the rate (D05.S2.10.02). They use trigonometric ratios and
the Pythagorean theorem to solve right-triangle problems in real contexts such as
surveying or navigation (D05.S3.10.01), and prove and apply theorems about circles
and use coordinate geometry to describe and analyze figures (D05.S3.10.02). They
understand independence and conditional probability and use them to interpret real
situations such as screening tests and risk (D05.S4.10.01), and use probability
rules to compute probabilities of compound events and interpret expected value in
everyday decisions (D05.S4.10.02). Mathematics remains sense-making with real
things, drawn from many traditions, for everyone.

*Science: Systems, Evidence & Our Origins* (U.10.006, D06) is the full pass through
Grade 10 science, carrying the evidence-and-source discipline of Grade 9 into the
three great bodies of the natural world. Learners analyze data using basic
statistics — mean, range, and trend — and assess how well the data support a
conclusion (D06.S1.10.01), and explain how peer review, replication, and open
sharing make scientific knowledge trustworthy, and where they fall short
(D06.S1.10.02). They explain how genetic information passes from parents to
offspring, how mutations arise, and how the environment shapes how genes are
expressed (D06.S2.10.01), and analyze how human activity — land use, pollution, and
introduced species — affects ecosystems and evaluate responses (D06.S2.10.02). They
explain motion, force, and energy quantitatively in everyday and engineered systems
such as work, power, and momentum (D06.S3.10.01), and explain how energy generation —
renewable and nonrenewable — works and evaluate its environmental and social
trade-offs (D06.S3.10.02). They explain the Big Bang model and the evidence that
supports it, distinguishing a model from revealed truth (D06.S4.10.01), and analyze
human impacts on Earth systems and evaluate responses to environmental change with
hope and agency (D06.S4.10.02). Origins are held as stories grounded in evidence,
among many others — never as dogma.

*Creative & Aesthetic Expression* (U.10.007, D07) gives craft and an ethical eye to
the imagination now that formal reasoning has matured. Learners develop a personal
style by making a body of work and explain how it reflects their ideas and influences
(D07.S1.10.01), and distinguish cultural appreciation from appropriation in art,
citing examples and explaining how to honor living traditions respectfully
(D07.S1.10.02). They compose and record an original piece that expresses a clear idea
or feeling and explain the techniques used (D07.S2.10.01), and distinguish respectful
engagement with a musical tradition from appropriation (D07.S2.10.02). They devise
and perform an original piece that develops a clear concept and demonstrates craft in
movement and expression (D07.S3.10.01), and distinguish respectful engagement with a
dance or dramatic tradition from appropriation (D07.S3.10.02). They design and make
something that serves a community need with attention to sustainability and fairness
and document the decisions (D07.S4.10.01), and distinguish responsible and
sustainable design from design that harms people or the environment (D07.S4.10.02).
The arts of other cultures are met as living traditions to honor with respectful
questions, never as specimens.

*Health, Sexuality & Safety* (U.10.008, D03) returns to the body at a turning point,
now with the honesty and agency high school demands. Learners analyze the risks and
responsibilities of sexual activity, including contraception and preventing
infections, using accurate information (D03.S4.10.01), and evaluate sources of health
information — online, peer, and marketing — for accuracy and bias (D03.S4.10.02).
Every body, every pace, and every family's way of living is honored; the body is
taught with dignity, never shame, and questions are met with accurate information and
care, not fear.

The final third steps into the full adult questions of power, justice, and the living
Earth. *Global History: Time, Culture & Atrocity* (U.10.009, D08) gives Grade 10 its
full pass through the long, many-voiced human story, deepening Grade 9's continuity
and liberation into the hardest and most careful questions. Learners compare how
different societies measured and recorded time and weigh how those systems shaped
their sense of the past (D08.S1.10.01), and argue for a way to divide a global era
into periods and defend where its turning points should fall (D08.S1.10.02). They
evaluate the ethics of sharing, borrowing, and taking knowledge and culture across
peoples, and who has been credited or harmed (D08.S2.10.01), and analyze how global
trade and technology have spread and transformed cultural traditions in the modern
world (D08.S2.10.02). They analyze how colonization reshaped populations,
environments, and borders across the world and its effects on Indigenous peoples
(D08.S3.10.01), and analyze modern migration and displacement driven by conflict,
economy, and climate, weighing their causes and human consequences (D08.S3.10.02).
They analyze a case of genocide or mass violence with care, examining its causes, the
responses of survivors, and how it is remembered (D08.S4.10.01), and analyze how
ordinary people have resisted oppression, sheltered others, and rebuilt after
atrocity, and what remembrance and justice require (D08.S4.10.02). History is told
from many perspectives, never as a single nation's triumph, and the voices of those
too often left out are centered with care and accuracy.

*Ethics, Justice & Political Economy* (U.10.010, D09) deepens Grade 9's reasoning
across frameworks into critique and action. Learners apply ethical reasoning to a
question about technology or the environment, weighing benefits and harms for people
and the planet (D09.S1.10.01), and critique a moral argument for hidden assumptions
or bias, rebuilding it more fairly (D09.S1.10.02). They analyze global justice
questions, such as who bears responsibility for climate harm or poverty, and weigh
competing answers (D09.S2.10.01), and evaluate how technology and data can expand or
limit rights, such as privacy, and argue for protections (D09.S2.10.02). They design
a plan for collective action on a real community or global issue, including goals,
allies, and steps, and evaluate its likely effects (D09.S3.10.01), and analyze the
ethics of civic technology and digital citizenship, including misinformation,
surveillance, and who is included or excluded (D09.S3.10.02). They design a
restorative process for a specific harm, including who should be heard, how
responsibility is taken, and how repair is made (D09.S4.10.01), and analyze
peacebuilding — preventing and transforming conflict nonviolently — and weigh its
strategies across different settings (D09.S4.10.02). Questions are held as questions,
never answered as dogma.

*Technology, Media & Our Earth Home* (U.10.011, D10) gathers the outward year into
critical judgment and stewardship — neither worship nor fear. Learners analyze how
data, surveillance, and automation affect privacy, freedom, and who has power in a
society (D10.S1.10.01), and evaluate an emerging technology, such as AI or genetic
tools, by weighing its promises and risks for humanity and the environment
(D10.S1.10.02). They apply a fact-checking method to a real claim, tracing evidence
and separating fact from interpretation (D10.S2.10.01), and analyze who owns the
media people consume and how concentrated ownership can shape what is reported and
left out (D10.S2.10.02). They explain what ecosystem services are — pollination,
clean water, and soil — and how biodiversity underpins them (D10.S3.10.01), and
explain what a tipping point is and why crossing one in an ecosystem or climate can
be hard to reverse (D10.S3.10.02). They evaluate a real sustainability solution, such
as a circular economy or a restoration project, for its benefits, limits, and
fairness (D10.S4.10.01), and explain the idea of a circular economy — designing out
waste — and compare it with a take-make-waste linear economy (D10.S4.10.02).

The year closes in *All of Me, All of Us* (U.10.012, D01 + D02), gathering every whole
person, the caring community they have built, and the living Earth — and looking ahead
to Grade 11, where the considered position becomes an articulated purpose and a
meaningful life, empathy extends to distant and future others, number grows into
sequences, series, vectors, and trigonometry of periodic phenomena, science deepens
into evolution, quantum scales, and coupled Earth systems, history deepens into long
transformations and the revitalization of marginalized knowledge, and the ethics of
technology and the environment mature into governance, solidarity, and stewardship
across generations.

Throughout, the four pillars are interwoven. Intellectual awareness (P3) carries the
most weight — criticism and craft in language, the next rung of modeling and proof in
mathematics, the systems-and-evidence discipline of science, and the creative and
critical work of the arts — while contextual and ecological awareness (P4) rises
further, for Grade 10 faces the hardest truths of history and the environment: the
ethics of knowledge and culture, genocide and remembrance, global justice, and the
deep effects of technology on humanity and the Earth. Emotional and social awareness
(P1) remains the ground: identity is examined and defended, and relationships and
belonging matter more than ever. A known, cared-for, and capable body-and-self (P2)
is the foundation all of it stands on. Science (D06), global history (D08), ethics
(D09), and technology and environment (D10) each now carry a full unit of the year,
and each spirals into still greater depth in Grades 11–12, where the full scientific,
historical, and ethical ladders unfold. The hardest truths of the world are faced
honestly — inequality, ecological crisis, atrocity, moral ambiguity — always met with
intellectual tools and a sense of agency, never nihilism. No high-stakes tests
anywhere: a learner's growth is seen through explanations that state their limits,
arguments that weigh conflicting evidence and critique hidden assumptions, reasoning
across more than one ethical framework and across cultures, modeling and proof with
real things, made and critiqued works, and a portfolio of what they say, make, and do
as co-authors of their own education.
