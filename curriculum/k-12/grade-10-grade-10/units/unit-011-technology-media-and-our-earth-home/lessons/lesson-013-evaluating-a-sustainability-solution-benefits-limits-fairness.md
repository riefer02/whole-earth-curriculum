# Lesson 13 — Evaluating a Sustainability Solution: Benefits, Limits, Fairness

## Summary

Learners evaluate a real **sustainability solution** — a restoration project, a circular scheme, or a
community-managed commons — for its **benefits**, **limits**, and **fairness**. They weigh who gains and
who pays, separate evidence from value, and reach a reasoned judgment about whether the solution is worth
supporting and how it could be made fairer.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a real sustainability solution for its benefits, limits, and fairness. _(D10.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

A plan to plant a million trees sounds unambiguously good — until you ask who owns the land, who gets the
shade, and whose grazing or fields the new forest replaces. Every real solution is like this: it does real
good, it has real limits, and it is fair or unfair to the people it touches. **Sustainability** — meeting
today's needs without robbing the future — is not a label to slap on; it is a balance to weigh. The skill
is not enthusiasm or cynicism, but the three questions: *what good does it do? what can't it do? and who
gains and who pays?*

## Materials

- Solution evaluation sheet
- Sustainability solution dossier

## Preparation

- Copy the evaluation sheet and the dossier.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 12, recall the circular economy and "designing out waste." Today we evaluate a
  real solution that tries to do that — and ask whether it is fair.
- Prepare one worked example of filling the three columns for a solution.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a sustainability solution is judged on
three questions — benefits (what good it does), limits (what it cannot do), and fairness (who gains and
who pays) — and fairness is where solutions most often fail.** The **environment** lens is the spine. The
**egalitarian** lens is decisive here: benefits and burdens rarely fall on the same people, and
environmental justice is the discipline of noticing that (S-483, S-484). The **global** lens: the same
solution (a plantation, a dam, a commons) succeeds or fails depending on local history and who holds the
land. The **ethics** lens: fairness is a value judgment, not a measurement — say so plainly. The
**technology** lens: high-tech and low-tech solutions both deserve the same three questions. Anchor the
examples: sustainability defined as meeting today's needs without compromising the future (S-395);
community-managed commons as a documented, effective pattern of stewardship (S-416); the Green Belt
Movement as a real restoration effort led by communities (S-059); indigenous cultural burning as
place-based stewardship (S-320). Distinguish evidence (what the solution does) from values (whether it is
fair) (philosophy §5). Preview: next lesson assembles the whole unit into one portfolio and one
synthesis.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12: what does "designing out waste" mean, and why is waste a design
   choice?
2. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** Look at the evaluation sheet. **Benefits** are the real goods a
   solution produces. **Limits** are what it cannot do or where it falls short. **Fairness** asks who
   gains and who pays — and whether that distribution is just. A **trade-off** is what you give up for
   what you get. **Stewardship** is caring for a resource so it lasts for others.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one solution evaluated step by step — a community tree-planting
   project: (a) benefits (shade, soil held, wood, income); (b) limits (trees take years; monoculture
   plantings can harm biodiversity if done wrong); (c) fairness (who owns the land, who gets the harvest,
   whose grazing is displaced — the Green Belt Movement succeeded by putting communities, especially
   women, at the center, S-059).
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, read the dossier — a real sustainability solution. Fill the
   three columns: at least two benefits, two limits, and a fairness analysis naming who gains and who
   pays.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your judgment: is this solution worth
   supporting, and how could it be made fairer? If you only listed benefits without the fairness question,
   mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one fairness finding. The lesson: a solution is as good as its most
   left-behind person.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled sheet (one benefit and one limit pre-listed) and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Weigh a benefit against a limit for the *same* community, and argue for the design that
  is both effective and fair.
- **Expression:** Learners may record their judgment aloud or have a partner read it — no step requires
  writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name benefits and limits of a solution and analyze its
  fairness — who gains and who pays?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed evaluation sheet and judgment, as the
  "sustainability-solution evaluation" for the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

With a family member, pick one local "green" effort — a recycling scheme, a new well, a planting drive —
and ask the three questions: what good does it do, what can't it do, and who gains and who pays?
