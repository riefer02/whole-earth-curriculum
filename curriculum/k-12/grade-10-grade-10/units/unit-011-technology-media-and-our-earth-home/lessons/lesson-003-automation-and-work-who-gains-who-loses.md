# Lesson 3 — Automation and Work: Who Gains, Who Loses

## Summary

Learners analyze **automation** — a machine doing work a person once did — through the lens of who gains
and who loses. They read cases from farming, retail, translation, and factory work, weigh productivity
against displacement and deskilling, and ask who holds the power to decide when and how automation
happens.

## Objectives

- Analyze how automation affects work, freedom, and who has power in a society. _(D10.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

A machine that threshes grain frees a farmer's back from weeks of bending. The same machine can put the
people who once did that threshing out of work. Automation is never just a gadget — it is a rearrangement
of *who does what* and *who gets paid for it*. When the cashier is replaced by a self-checkout, when the
translator is aided by software, when a tractor does the work of twenty hands, the gains are real and so
are the losses — and they rarely land on the same people. The question is not whether machines change
work (they always have); it is who benefits, who is left holding the cost, and who gets to decide.

## Materials

- Automation case cards
- Gains/losses chart

## Preparation

- Copy the case cards and the gains/losses chart.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall that the few who hold the data hold power. Today we ask what happens
  when machines *act* on that data, and on whose behalf.
- Prepare one worked example of filling the gains/losses chart for a single case.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **automation rearranges work — it raises
productivity and can free people from drudgery, but it can also displace and deskill workers, and the
gains and losses fall on different people; the decisive question is who holds the power to decide.** Keep
the facts hedged: automation's net effect on employment is debated and varies by sector and place, not a
single verdict. The **technology** lens is the spine — this is what technology does to work. The
**egalitarian** lens: the gains (profit, cheaper goods) often accrue to owners, while the losses (jobs,
skills, wages) fall on workers, and the digital divide shapes who can adapt (S-281). The **global** lens:
automation lands differently in a rice field, a garment factory, and an office — there is no single
story. The **ethics** lens: deciding how work changes is a moral choice, not just an efficiency
calculation. The **environment** lens: automation can use more energy and materials, or less — weigh both.
Distinguish evidence (what changed) from values (whether the change is fair) (philosophy §5). Preview:
next lesson we build a method for weighing an emerging technology's promises and risks.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: who watches, and who is watched, in your school or street? What
   does that asymmetry do to power?
2. **Meet automation (10 min).** **Automation** is delegating a task to a machine or software.
   **Productivity** is output per worker or per hour; **displacement** is a worker losing a task or job;
   **deskilling** is a task being simplified so it needs less judgment. These three travel together, and
   not evenly.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one case done step by step — a self-checkout at a store: (a) who
   gains (the owner saves wages; some customers are quicker); (b) who loses (cashiers lose hours; some
   customers do unpaid work); (c) who decides (the owner, not the cashier); (d) the environment angle
   (machines use power and are made of materials).
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, read two case cards — one where automation freed people from
   dangerous or exhausting work, one where it displaced them. Fill the gains/losses chart for each: who
   gains, who loses, who decides.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write: who *should* have a say when a machine
   takes over a job, and why? If you only described what changed without saying who should decide, mark
   "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one gains/losses contrast. Notice the recurring pattern: the decision power
   sits with the few, while the consequences spread to the many.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one pre-filled gains/losses chart and sentence starters ("The owner gains __; the
  worker loses __").
- **Extension:** Compare automation in two sectors and argue whether the gains outweigh the losses — and
  for whom.
- **Expression:** Learners may record their reasoning by voice, by diagram, or have a partner read it —
  no step requires writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name who gains and who loses in a case, and say who holds
  the decision power?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The gains/losses chart with one case, added to the "technology and
  power" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, find one task at home or work that has been automated (a machine, an app, a
self-service step). Ask who it helps and who it costs — and whether the people affected had a say.
