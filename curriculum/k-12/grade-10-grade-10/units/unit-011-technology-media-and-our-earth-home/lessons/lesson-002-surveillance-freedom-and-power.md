# Lesson 2 — Surveillance, Freedom, and Power

## Summary

Learners analyze **surveillance** as an asymmetry of visibility — many watched by few — and connect that
asymmetry to freedom and power. They read contrasting cases where watching is used for care and for
control, weigh who is protected and who is chilled, and distinguish the facts of surveillance from the
values of what *should* be watched.

## Objectives

- Analyze how surveillance affects privacy, freedom, and who has power in a society. _(D10.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Imagine a room where everyone can see everyone. Now imagine a room where one person sees everyone, but
no one sees the one. Those rooms feel different: in the first you speak freely; in the second you begin
to watch your own words, even when no one has threatened you. Surveillance is the second room. It is
not only cameras — it is the record of where you go, whom you call, what you read, and the power that
comes to whoever holds that record. The same watching that keeps a crowd safe at a festival can, in
another's hands, silence a person who would speak out. The question is not whether watching exists; it
is who watches whom, and what that does to freedom.

## Materials

- Asymmetry diagram
- Surveillance case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the asymmetry diagram and the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the data trail we leave and who collects it. Today we ask what the
  *concentration* of that collection does to freedom and power.
- Prepare one worked example of reading a case and naming who watches and who is watched.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **surveillance is an asymmetry of
visibility — the many are watched by the few — and that asymmetry redistributes power: it can protect
(the many can hold the few accountable) or control (the few can chill the many into self-censorship).**
Hold both directions honestly; surveillance is not one thing. The **ethics** lens: watching can be a
duty of care or a tool of domination, so we must ask *for what and by whom*. The **egalitarian** lens:
surveillance rarely falls evenly — the powerful are often the watchers and the marginalized the watched,
and the digital divide means some are over-exposed while others are invisible (S-281). The **global**
lens: states and communities differ sharply in how they watch and why — some watch for public health,
some for protest control; no one arrangement is the human default. The **technology** lens: cheap
sensors and storage made mass surveillance possible where it was once impossible. The **environment**
lens: surveillance infrastructure — cameras, servers, networks — carries material and energy costs.
Power here is the capacity to shape what others can do (S-275). Distinguish evidence (who is watched)
from values (who *should* be). Hold questions open (philosophy §11). Preview: next lesson turns to
automation — when machines act on that data, who gains and who loses.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what was the single most revealing mark on your day trail? Who
   could collect it?
2. **Meet the asymmetry (10 min).** Look at the diagram. **Surveillance** is systematic watching and
   record-keeping. The diagram shows **asymmetric visibility**: the many can be seen by the few, but the
   few are hard to see. Power is the capacity to shape what others can do (S-275) — and the record of a
   life is a form of power over that life.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one case read step by step — a transit system that tracks every
   rider's card: (a) who watches (the operator, and whoever they share with); (b) who is watched (every
   rider); (c) the protection it offers (faster service, finding a lost person); (d) the chill it can
   create (avoiding certain routes, altering behavior because the trip is recorded).
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, read two contrasting case cards — one where watching is
   used for care, one for control. For each, name who watches, who is watched, what freedom it protects
   or shrinks, and who holds the power.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, answer in three sentences: does surveillance
   change how freely *you* act when you know you are watched? If you wrote only "yes" or "no" without
   reasoning, mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one contrast. The line between protection and control is drawn by who
   watches, and by whether the watched can watch back — that is accountability.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one pre-annotated case (who watches / who is watched already labeled) and sentence
  starters.
- **Extension:** Find a case where surveillance both protects and chills the *same* people, and explain
  the tension.
- **Expression:** Learners may map the cases with arrows instead of writing, or have a partner read their
  reasoning aloud.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner identify who watches and who is watched in a case, name a
  freedom protected or shrunk, and say who holds power?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed asymmetry diagram with one annotated case, added to the
  "technology and power" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, list the places in your day where you are watched (a camera, a card, a phone). For
one, ask: does this watching protect us or shape us — and how would we know?
