# Lesson 4 — Weighing an Emerging Technology: Promises and Risks

## Summary

Learners build and apply a method for evaluating an **emerging technology** — weighing its promises and
risks for humanity and the environment, neither worshipping it nor fearing it. They read a balanced
dossier on generative AI and a genetic tool, separate evidence from value, and reach a reasoned, hedged
judgment about whether and how the technology should be developed and governed.

## Objectives

- Evaluate an emerging technology, such as AI or genetic tools, by weighing its promises and risks for
  humanity and the environment. _(D10.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Every powerful new tool arrives with two stories attached: the story of what it will fix, and the story
of what it might break. The same language model that writes a struggling student's first draft can flood
a town with convincing lies; the same gene-editing tool that could cure a disease could also be used to
alter what a future child is. Neither worship — "it will save us" — nor fear — "it will destroy us" — is
a method. A method is a balance: list the promises, list the risks, ask *for whom* and *for the Earth*,
and decide with your eyes open. That weighing, not a verdict, is the skill.

## Materials

- Promises/risks scale sheet
- Emerging-technology dossier

## Preparation

- Copy the scale sheet and the dossier.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–3, recall the data trail, the asymmetry of surveillance, and the gains and
  losses of automation. Today we assemble those threads into one weighing method.
- Prepare one worked example of listing a promise and a risk and stating for whom each is true.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a technology is judged by weighing its
promises and risks for humanity and the environment — not by worshipping it or fearing it — and the
weighing must ask *for whom* and *at what cost to the Earth*.** This is the unit's central stance
(neither worship nor fear). The **technology** and **environment** lenses are the twin pans of the
scale. The **egalitarian** lens: promises and risks are distributed unevenly — who gains access and who
bears harm is a question of justice (S-281). The **global** lens: an emerging technology lands
differently where infrastructure, law, and wealth differ. The **ethics** lens: whether a risk is
acceptable is a value judgment, not a measurement. Ground the examples in sources: generative AI's
real capabilities and limits (machine translation as a concrete case, S-350; synthetic media and
deepfakes, S-393), and genetic tools' real foundations in heredity and crop genetic resources (S-177,
S-178). Model the discipline of hedging — say "may," "can," "some evidence suggests," not certainty.
Distinguish evidence from values (philosophy §5). Preview: next lesson we turn to the media strand —
how to check a claim in the flood of information these very tools produce.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: who decided when a machine took over a job? What did that tell us
   about power?
2. **Meet the scale (10 min).** Look at the scale sheet. A **promise** is a claimed or real benefit; a
   **risk** is a possible harm; a **trade-off** is what you give up for what you get; an **unintended
   consequence** is an effect nobody planned. An **evaluation** weighs these honestly, for people and for
   the planet.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one weighing done step by step — a machine-translation tool:
   (a) promise (fast gist across languages, access for low-resource speakers, S-350); (b) risk (errors
   and flattening of nuance, dependence on a few large systems, S-350); (c) for whom (a traveler gains,
   a professional translator's craft is pressured); (d) the environment (training and serving models
   uses energy and water).
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, read the dossier — generative AI and a genetic tool. Fill
   the scale sheet: at least two promises and two risks for each, naming *for whom* and any environment
   cost.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your judgment in three sentences: should
   this technology be developed further, and under what conditions? If you only listed promises and
   risks without a judgment, mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one judgment. Notice the discipline: a hedged, "under these conditions"
   answer is stronger than worship or fear.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled scale sheet (one promise and one risk pre-listed) and sentence
  starters.
- **Extension:** Weight a promise against a risk for the *same* people (e.g., a farmer who both gains and
  loses), and argue which is heavier.
- **Expression:** Learners may record their judgment aloud, in a diagram, or have a partner read it — no
  step requires writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name promises and risks, say for whom each is true, note an
  environment cost, and reach a hedged judgment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed scale sheet and judgment, as the "emerging-technology
  evaluation" for the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

With a family member, pick one new technology in your home or region. Name one promise and one risk, and
ask: for whom is each true, and who would we want deciding whether to adopt it?
