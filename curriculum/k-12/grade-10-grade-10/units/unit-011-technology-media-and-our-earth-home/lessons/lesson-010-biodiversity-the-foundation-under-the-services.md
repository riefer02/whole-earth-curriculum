# Lesson 10 — Biodiversity: The Foundation Under the Services

## Summary

Learners explain how **biodiversity** — the variety of living things — underpins every ecosystem service,
and what happens when that variety thins. Using keystone-species cases and the idea of **redundancy**,
they see why a wide base of species makes services stable, and why losing species weakens the whole
system.

## Objectives

- Explain how biodiversity underpins ecosystem services. _(D10.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

A field with one kind of crop is one disease away from empty; a field with many kinds is a cushion. The
same is true of a forest, a reef, a river: the more kinds of life they hold, the more ways they have to
keep working when one part fails. If every pollinator were a single bee species and that bee vanished,
every flower would wait in vain; with many pollinators, one can fail and the rest carry the work. That is
**biodiversity as a foundation** — not decoration, but the load-bearing wall under every free service the
living world gives us.

## Materials

- Biodiversity pyramid sheet
- Keystone-species case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the pyramid sheet and the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, recall the four kinds of ecosystem services. Today we ask what they stand on:
  biodiversity.
- Prepare one worked example of showing how a keystone species' decline ripples through its services.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **biodiversity — the variety of living
things — underpins every ecosystem service, because more kinds of life mean more ways for the system to
keep working when one part fails (redundancy and resilience); thinning the base of species thins the
services.** The **environment** lens is the spine. The **egalitarian** lens: the loss of biodiversity
falls hardest on people who depend most directly on wild species for food, medicine, and livelihood
(S-437). The **global** lens: biodiversity is distributed unevenly across the planet, and the places
richest in species are often the places with the least money to protect them — a question of shared
responsibility (S-279). The **ethics** lens: other species have value beyond their usefulness to us —
a claim many traditions hold and one we can weigh alongside the services argument (S-427). The
**technology** lens: technology can mimic a few services but cannot rebuild a thinned web of life.
Anchor the science: a keystone species is one whose effect is large for its abundance (S-176); IPBES
documents that species decline is driven by land-use change, direct exploitation, climate change,
pollution, and invasive species (S-437). Keep the wolf/aspens example hedged — recovery has been complex
and contested (S-310). Preview: next lesson asks what happens at a *threshold* — the tipping point.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9: name one ecosystem service and its living provider.
2. **Meet biodiversity (10 min).** **Biodiversity** is the variety of living things. **Species richness**
   is how many kinds there are. A **keystone species** is one whose effect is large for its abundance. Two
   ideas matter: **redundancy** (many species doing similar jobs, so one can fail) and **resilience** (the
   ability to recover from disturbance). **Extinction** is the permanent loss of a kind of life.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one case traced step by step — many pollinator species in a healthy
   orchard: (a) the service (pollination → fruit); (b) the redundancy (bees, flies, moths, birds all
   pollinate); (c) what happens when pesticides thin them (fewer kinds → less redundancy → a bad year for
   one is a failed harvest); (d) the service now rests on a narrower base.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, read a keystone-species case card. Draw the pyramid for that
   ecosystem: list the services on top, the species in the base. Then show what happens when the base
   thins — which service topples first?
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, answer: why does a *variety* of species keep a
   service stable better than one species can? If you described variety without the "why," mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one toppling. The lesson: services do not float — they stand on biodiversity.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn pyramid with one service labeled, and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Explain how redundancy buffers an ecosystem, using a second example you find or know.
- **Expression:** Learners may draw the pyramid and have a partner read their reasoning — no step requires
  writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner explain how biodiversity underpins a service, using
  redundancy or a keystone example?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed biodiversity pyramid with one case, added to the
  "ecosystem-services and biodiversity" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, look at a garden, field, or green space. How many kinds of life do you count? What
does each kind do there — and what would be lost if one kind disappeared?
