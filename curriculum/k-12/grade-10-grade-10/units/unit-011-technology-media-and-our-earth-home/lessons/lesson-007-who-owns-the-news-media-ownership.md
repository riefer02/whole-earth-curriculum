# Lesson 7 — Who Owns the News? Media Ownership

## Summary

Learners analyze **who owns the media** people consume and how **concentrated ownership** can shape what
is reported and what is left out. They map outlets to owners, compare a concentrated market with a plural
one, and weigh the risks to a public's right to know against the realities of funding journalism.

## Objectives

- Analyze who owns the media people consume and how concentrated ownership can shape what is reported
  and left out. _(D10.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

When you read the news, you are reading someone's choices — an editor's, a publisher's, and behind them
an owner's. If a hundred different-looking outlets are all owned by the same few companies, the variety
is an illusion: the same hands decide what is worth covering and what is quietly dropped. That is what
**concentration** means — many names, few owners. The opposite is **plurality** — many independent
voices. Who owns the news shapes what a public can know, and what it never hears about at all. This is
not a conspiracy theory; it is how any gate works, whether the gate is a newspaper, a radio tower, or a
feed.

## Materials

- Ownership map sheet
- Media ownership case

## Preparation

- Copy the ownership map sheet and the case.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, recall how to trace a claim and separate fact from interpretation. Today
  we ask a step *before* the claim: who decided what you would even see to check?
- Prepare one worked example of tracing outlets to owners.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **media ownership can be concentrated —
many outlets, few owners — and that concentration shapes what is reported and left out, because owners
set priorities and resources; plurality of independent voices is what protects a public's right to know.**
Keep the analysis structural, not conspiratorial: owners influence through staffing, budgets, and
editorial direction, not usually by direct command. The **egalitarian** lens: whose stories get told and
whose are dropped tracks power — the voices that own the megaphone are heard; the rest are not (S-442,
S-417). The **global** lens: media systems differ — public-service broadcasting, community radio, and
press-freedom arrangements vary widely, and none is the human default (S-217). The **technology** lens:
digital platforms have become the new gatekeepers, and their ownership and algorithms shape the feed
(S-481, S-482). The **ethics** lens: journalism has a duty to inform fairly, and ownership that narrows
that duty is an ethical problem. The **environment** lens: media ownership also shapes *which* stories
are told — including environmental stories that threaten powerful interests. Distinguish evidence (who
owns what) from values (what a fair media system should look like) (philosophy §5). Preview: next lesson
analyzes what gets reported and what gets left out, as a portfolio artifact.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: what is the difference between a fact and an interpretation? Why
   does the distinction protect your thinking?
2. **Meet ownership (10 min).** **Media ownership** is who owns the outlets that gather and publish news.
   **Concentration** is many outlets under few owners. **Plurality** is many independent voices.
   **Independent media** are owned separately from government and from large chains; **public service
   media** are funded by the public and answer to it. The owner of a gate is a **gatekeeper** — deciding,
   by budget and priority, what passes through.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one market traced step by step: three newspapers, two radio
   stations, and a TV channel that all look different — but two of the papers, one station, and the
   channel share a single owner. (a) What gets covered well (the owner's interests); (b) what gets
   dropped (stories that embarrass the owner); (c) what plurality would look like instead.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, read the case — one concentrated market and one plural
   market. Draw the ownership map for each: which outlets trace to which owners. Discuss one story each
   market would likely report and one it would likely drop.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write: who owns the media you actually use, and
   what might that ownership leave out? If you only named owners without saying what is left out, mark
   "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one "left out" example. The question is not whether media should exist, but
   whether many independent voices — not a few owners — decide what a public knows.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-drawn ownership map (some outlets pre-traced) and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Compare concentration in two countries and argue how each shapes reporting, citing one
  real example.
- **Expression:** Learners may draw the map and have a partner read their reasoning — no step requires
  writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace outlets to owners, say how concentration shapes
  what is reported and left out, and name what plurality would add?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The ownership map with one "left out" example, as the opening page of
  the "media ownership analysis" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, list the news you both get. Try to name who owns each source. Ask: whose stories
do we never see, and how would we find them?
