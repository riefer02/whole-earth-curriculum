# Lesson 6 — Fact and Interpretation: A Real Claim, Checked

## Summary

Learners separate **fact** from **interpretation** within real claims, tracing evidence and flagging
where a meaning has been attached to a fact. They verify the checkable parts, identify the
interpretations, and practice the discipline of hedging — saying only what the evidence supports.

## Objectives

- Apply a fact-checking method to a real claim, tracing evidence and separating fact from
  interpretation. _(D10.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

"It rained for three days" and "the rains ruined our crops" can describe the same week. The first is a
**fact** — you can check it with a gauge and a calendar. The second is an **interpretation** — a meaning
placed on the fact, which may be true or may ignore that the crops were already failing from disease.
Much of what we read and hear is this mixture, stitched so tightly we mistake the meaning for the
measurement. Separating them is not pedantry; it is how you keep your thinking honest, and how you
notice when someone is smuggling a conclusion inside a fact.

## Materials

- Fact/interpretation claim set
- Two-column sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the claim set and the two-column sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, recall the four SIFT moves. Today we use them to split a single claim into
  its checkable facts and its interpretations.
- Prepare one worked example of separating a claim into fact and interpretation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a claim often bundles a checkable fact
with an interpretation — a meaning given to that fact — and the skill is to separate them, verify the
fact, and say plainly where the interpretation begins.** This extends Lesson 5 from "is it true?" to "which
part is true, and which part is a reading of the truth?" The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine. The
**ethics** lens: honest communication names its interpretations instead of hiding them inside facts
(S-442). The **egalitarian** lens: the habit of separating fact from interpretation protects ordinary
people from being led; it is a democratic skill, not an elite one. The **global** lens: different media
traditions blend fact and commentary differently — recognizing the blend, not imposing one style, is the
aim. The **technology** lens: algorithmically boosted feeds reward strong interpretation over careful
fact, which is why the split matters (S-482). The **environment** lens: environmental claims — a drought,
a harvest, a river's health — are where fact and interpretation most often tangle, so the split is a form
of care for the Earth too. Use real, hedged language and distinguish evidence from values (philosophy §5).
Model "the evidence shows X; whether that is good or bad is a judgment." Preview:
next lesson asks who owns the media that deliver these claims, and how that shapes what we even see.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: name the four moves and the one you are most likely to skip.
2. **Meet the split (10 min).** A **fact** is checkable — it can be confirmed or corrected with evidence.
   An **interpretation** is a meaning, judgment, or conclusion placed on facts. **Evidence** supports a
   fact; **inference** builds an interpretation on it. **Hedging** means saying only what the evidence
   supports ("the data suggest," not "this proves").
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one claim split step by step: "Because the factory cut jobs, the
   town is dying." (a) Fact: the factory cut a specific number of jobs — checkable. (b) Fact: the town's
   population changed by a checkable amount. (c) Interpretation: "the town is dying" — a meaning placed on
   those facts, which may be contested (new work, different causes).
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, sort the claim set into the two-column sheet: fact
   (checkable) on one side, interpretation (a meaning given) on the other. For each fact, name the
   evidence that would check it; for each interpretation, name the value or judgment it carries.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, rewrite one claim so the fact and the
   interpretation are visibly separated, and hedge the interpretation. If you only circled words without
   rewriting, mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one rewrite. The discipline: the fact stays; the interpretation is named as
   yours, not smuggled in.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one pre-split claim and sentence starters ("The fact is __; the interpretation is
  __").
- **Extension:** Find a real loaded claim and rewrite it in neutral terms, then in clearly-labeled
  opinion.
- **Expression:** Learners may speak the separation aloud or have a partner read their rewrite — no step
  requires writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner separate fact from interpretation in a claim, name the
  evidence that would check the fact, and hedge the interpretation?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column sheet and one rewrite, added to the "fact-check record"
  section.

## Home connection

With a family member, take one sentence from the news or a group chat and sort it: which words are
checkable facts, and which are a meaning placed on them?
