# Lesson 9 — Ecosystem Services: Nature's Free Labor

## Summary

Learners explain what **ecosystem services** are — the work living systems do for people for free — using
pollination, clean water, and soil as anchors. They classify services into **provisioning**,
**regulating**, **supporting**, and **cultural**, and connect them to the biodiversity that provides them,
previewing the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Explain what ecosystem services are, such as pollination, clean water, and soil. _(D10.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

A bee moves pollen and your food appears. A wetland holds water and filters it until it runs clean. Roots
grip soil so the rain does not wash a field into the river. None of this work appears on anyone's
payroll, yet every meal, every glass of clean water, and every stable field depends on it. We call this
**nature's free labor** — the services living systems provide. Because it is free, it is easy to forget;
because it is essential, forgetting it is dangerous. Seeing the work is the first step toward not
destroying it.

## Materials

- Ecosystem-services diagram
- Service-matching cards

## Preparation

- Copy the ecosystem-services diagram and the service-matching cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall the greenhouse effect and the carbon cycle connecting living things,
  oceans, atmosphere, and rock (D10.S3.09.01). Today we name the *benefits* those living systems provide.
- Prepare one worked example of matching a service to its provider.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **ecosystem services are the benefits
people receive from living systems — provisioning (food, water), regulating (climate, flood, disease),
supporting (soil, nutrient cycling), and cultural (meaning, recreation) — and they are provided for free
by biodiversity, so losing biodiversity loses the services.** The **environment** lens is the spine. The
**egalitarian** lens: the people who depend most directly on these services — smallholder farmers, fishers,
foragers — are often the least able to pay for a replacement, so their loss is a justice question
(S-437). The **global** lens: every place has its own services (a mangrove nursery, a mountain watershed,
a desert's shade), and no single ecosystem is the default. The **ethics** lens: treating free services as
worthless is a moral error — they have value beyond price. The **technology** lens: machines can
substitute for *some* services (a water-treatment plant for a wetland) but often at great cost and never
for all — the natural version is often irreplaceable. Anchor the science: pollination as the transfer of
pollen that makes seeds (S-330); soil as the living surface that supports growth (S-259); clean water
through the water cycle and filtration (S-212). Distinguish evidence (what services exist) from values
(what they are worth) (philosophy §5). Preview: next lesson asks how biodiversity underpins all of these.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what does the carbon cycle connect, and why does it matter that
   living things are in it?
2. **Meet the services (10 min).** Look at the diagram. An **ecosystem service** is a benefit people get
   from a living system. Four kinds: **provisioning** (things we take — food, water, wood, medicine);
   **regulating** (processes that keep conditions stable — climate, floods, disease, pests);
   **supporting** (the base that makes the rest possible — soil, nutrients, photosynthesis); and
   **cultural** (meaning, beauty, recreation, knowledge).
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one service traced step by step — a wetland's clean water:
   (a) the service (regulating: water filtration and flood control); (b) the providers (reeds, microbes,
   soil); (c) who relies on it (downstream communities); (d) what a treatment plant would cost to replace
   it.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, match the service-matching cards: each service to its
   provider and its kind (provisioning, regulating, supporting, or cultural). For two matches, name who
   relies on that service.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, name one ecosystem service you rely on this week,
   and what living thing provides it. If you named the service without its provider, mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one service. The pattern: every service rests on living things — which is
   exactly where the next lesson goes.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-matched set with one pair already done, and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Classify a list of services and argue which single one, if lost, would be hardest to
  replace by technology.
- **Expression:** Learners may act out a service or have a partner read their reasoning — no step requires
  writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name ecosystem services (pollination, clean water, soil),
  classify one by kind, and name its living provider?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The matched cards or a labeled diagram, as the opening page of the
  "ecosystem-services and biodiversity" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, find one thing your household relies on that a living thing provides for free —
shade, food, clean water, a pollinator. Ask: what would you do if it stopped?
