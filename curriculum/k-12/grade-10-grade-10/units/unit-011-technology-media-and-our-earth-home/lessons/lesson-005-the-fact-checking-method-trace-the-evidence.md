# Lesson 5 — The Fact-Checking Method: Trace the Evidence

## Summary

Learners meet and practice a **fact-checking method** — SIFT: **S**top, **I**nvestigate the source,
**F**ind better coverage, and **T**race to the original. They learn **lateral reading** (checking a
source by leaving it and looking at what others say) and **corroboration** (seeing whether independent
sources agree), then work a real claim through the method, recording where it came from and whether it
holds up.

## Objectives

- Apply a fact-checking method to a real claim, tracing evidence back to its source. _(D10.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

A claim reaches you wrapped in a feeling — outrage, hope, surprise — and the feeling travels faster than
the facts. Before you share it, you can ask four small questions that cost less than a minute: *Wait —
who is saying this? What do others say? Where did it first come from?* A person who asks those questions
is not cynical; they are careful. This matters everywhere people share news — a village noticeboard, a
family group chat, a city feed — because a lie can travel the world while the truth is still checking
its sources. The method is not about believing nothing; it is about knowing *why* you believe what you
believe.

## Materials

- SIFT method card
- Claim tracing worksheet

## Preparation

- Copy the SIFT method card and the worksheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall tracing a viral claim to its source and checking it against independent
  reporting (D10.S2.09.02). Today we formalize that habit into a reusable method.
- Prepare one worked example of running a claim through all four moves.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a fact-checking method is a habit, not
a talent — Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace to the original — and it works by
leaving the claim to see what others say (lateral reading) and by finding where it first came from.** This
is an explicit-skill lesson: model the method with a worked example, then guided practice, then
independent work (S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine. The **technology** lens: the flood
of information — and the AI that now generates it — is exactly why the method matters (S-393). The
**egalitarian** lens: everyone deserves the tool to tell a source apart from a rumor, not just
professionals; media literacy is a shared good (S-217). The **global** lens: checking practices and what
counts as a trustworthy source differ across cultures and media systems — no one tradition is the
default. The **ethics** lens: sharing an unchecked claim can harm real people, so checking is a duty, not
a chore. The **environment** lens: false claims about the environment — a broken dam, a fire, a drought,
a polluted river — spread fast and can drive real harm or delay real care, so checking them is care for
the Earth too. Note the method's roots in verification guides (S-394) and historical sourcing (S-316),
and hold questions open (philosophy §11). Preview: next lesson we separate fact from interpretation on a
real claim.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: think of a time a claim spread quickly. What, if anything, did you
   check before believing or sharing it?
2. **Meet the method (10 min).** Look at the SIFT card. Four moves: **Stop** (pause before you react or
   share); **Investigate the source** (who is this, and are they in a position to know?); **Find better
   coverage** (do other, independent sources report the same thing?); **Trace to the original** (where
   did this first appear, and was it altered on the way?). **Lateral reading** means checking a source by
   *leaving* it — opening new tabs, not scrolling deeper into the same page.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one claim run through all four moves step by step: a post claims a
   local dam has broken. Stop. Investigate: who posted it, and do they know? Find better coverage: is a
   news agency or the dam authority reporting it? Trace: the image in the post is actually from a flood
   years ago in another country. The claim fails the trace.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, run a second claim through the four moves on the worksheet.
   Record: the source, what others say, and the original. Agree on whether it holds up.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write the four moves in your own words, with one
   sentence each on what they protect you from. If you listed the moves without the "what they protect
   you from," mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one move you will actually use. The method is only as good as the habit of
   using it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the four moves with the "what it protects you from" half-filled, and sentence
  starters.
- **Extension:** Check a claim from a real feed and write a short verification note citing what you found.
- **Expression:** Learners may speak their moves aloud, or have a partner read their reasoning — no step
  requires writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name and apply the four moves, trace a claim to its source,
  and say whether it holds up?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed claim tracing worksheet, as the opening page of the unit's
  "fact-check record" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, take one claim from your shared feed or conversation and run it through the four
moves together. Which move changed your mind?
