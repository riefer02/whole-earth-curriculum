# Lesson 8 — What Gets Reported and What Gets Left Out

## Summary

Learners analyze how the **framing** and **selection** of a story — what is in the frame and what is left
out — shape what people come to believe. They compare two differently-framed reports of the same event,
identify each report's angle and omissions, and connect framing back to ownership and to their own habits
as readers.

## Objectives

- Analyze how media framing and selection shape what is reported and left out. _(D10.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

A photograph is a choice about where to point the lens. Point it at the crowd and you see a celebration;
point it at one angry face and the same crowd looks like a riot. News works the same way: every story is
a **frame**, and the frame decides what is inside — and, just as powerfully, what is outside. A flood
reported as "government failure" leaves out the river's neglected floodplain; a protest reported as
"violence" leaves out what the people were protesting. You cannot see everything at once; so the question
is never whether a frame exists, but whether you know you are looking through one — and what the frame
left out.

## Materials

- Framing comparison
- Frame/omission sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the framing comparison and the frame/omission sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall who owns the outlets you use and what that ownership might leave out.
  Today we analyze the *how* — the frame itself.
- Prepare one worked example of listing what is in and out of a frame.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **every report is framed — a selection of
what to show and what to omit — and the frame shapes what people conclude; media literacy is the habit of
noticing the frame and asking what it left out.** The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine. The
**ethics** lens: fair reporting names its angle and seeks what it might be missing (S-442). The
**egalitarian** lens: framing decides whose experience is centered and whose is omitted — a question of
who gets to be the "we" of a story (S-417). The **global** lens: the same event is framed differently by
different media systems and cultures, and reading across them widens the frame. The **technology** lens:
the feed amplifies the most engaging frame, not the most complete one (S-482). The **environment** lens:
environmental stories are especially vulnerable to framing — a drought framed as "weather" omits the
long human causes; notice what the frame hides. Use the event example with care and keep claims hedged.
Preview: we now pivot to the Earth itself — ecosystem services and tipping points — the environment half
of the unit.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: what is media concentration, and what can it leave out?
2. **Meet the frame (10 min).** **Framing** is choosing what to include and how to present it. **Selection**
   is what a story picks out; **omission** is what it leaves out. **Agenda setting** is the power to make
   some things seem important and others invisible. An **angle** is the particular point of view a story
   takes.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one frame analyzed step by step: two reports of a market fire — one
   framed as "looters run wild," another as "families lose their stalls." (a) What is inside each frame
   (who is named, what words are used); (b) what is outside (the cause, the history, the people affected);
   (c) what a reader of each would conclude.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a group, compare the two reports in the framing comparison. Fill the
   frame/omission sheet: for each, list what is in the frame and what is out, and name the angle.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, answer: what is one thing *both* frames leave out,
   and how would you find it? If you only described the frames without the omission, mark "not yet."
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one omission. The reader's power is the question: *what is outside this frame?*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one report pre-annotated (in-frame / out-of-frame labeled) and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Identify the audience each report targets, and argue who benefits from each framing.
- **Expression:** Learners may draw the frame and list items, or have a partner read their reasoning — no
  step requires writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner identify what is in and out of a frame, name the angle, and
  say how framing shapes what a reader concludes?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The frame/omission sheet with one report comparison, completing the
  "media ownership analysis" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, take one news story and ask: what is inside this frame, and what is left out? Where
would you look to fill the gap?
