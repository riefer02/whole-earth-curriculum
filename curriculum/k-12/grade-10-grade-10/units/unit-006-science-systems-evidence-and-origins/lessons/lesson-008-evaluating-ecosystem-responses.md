# Lesson 8 — Evaluating Responses to Ecosystem Harm

## Summary

Learners evaluate four kinds of response to ecosystem harm — **restoration** (repairing
damaged systems), **protected areas**, **regulation** (rules and limits), and
**biosecurity** (keeping new invaders out) — using a simple rubric: is it effective,
fair, affordable, and does it have side effects? They practice weighing trade-offs and
learn that "does it work?" is inseparable from "works for whom?"

## Objectives

- Analyze how human activity (such as land use, pollution, and introduced species)
  affects ecosystems and evaluate responses. _(D06.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

When a field is degraded, a river polluted, or an invader spreading, people do not just
stand by — they fence land, pass rules, replant trees, and guard borders. But every
response has a cost, and some "solutions" help one group while hurting another. Judging
a response well means asking not only "does it fix the problem?" but "who benefits, who
pays, and what else breaks?" That is the difference between a gesture and a genuine
repair.

## Materials

- Response-evaluation sheet
- Science journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the response-evaluation sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, the three human drivers; from Grade 9, biodiversity and why
  it matters (D06.S2.09.02). Today we turn from the problem to the response.
- Prepare the four response cards and the rubric.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **responses to
ecosystem harm — restoration, protected areas, regulation, and biosecurity — can be
evaluated on effectiveness, fairness, cost, and side effects, and every response is a
trade-off; good evaluation asks "works for whom?" not just "does it work?"** Model the
scoring of one response (S-011), then let learners score the rest and defend their
judgments. Keep hope attached: the point is agency, not despair — responses exist and
can work, especially when they are chosen *with* the people who live there.

The **ethics** lens: a response that saves a species but displaces a community is not a
clean win — evaluation must include human consequences. The **egalitarian** lens: the
people who depend on an ecosystem must have a voice in deciding its fate; top-down
"conservation" that fences people out of their own land has a painful history and a
better alternative in community-based stewardship. The **environment** lens: responses
are real and can succeed — damaged systems do recover, often surprisingly, when given
time and protection. The **global** lens: every region has its own versions of these
responses, from village-managed forests to international treaties, and we can learn
across them. Preview: Lesson 9 moves to the physical world — force, work, and the
quantitative language of motion.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, name the three human drivers of ecosystem harm.
   What is one response you have heard of — a rule, a fence, a replanting?
2. **Meet the four responses (15 min).** Read the four cards:
   - **Restoration:** replanting, reintroducing a species, cleaning a river.
   - **Protected area:** setting land aside from development.
   - **Regulation:** limits on pollution, harvest, or trade.
   - **Biosecurity:** quarantine and border checks against invaders.
   For each, note the aim and the likely cost.
3. **Score one together (10 min).** Score "restoration" with the rubric: effective?
   fair? affordable? side effects? Model the judgment aloud, then note the trade-off —
   restoration is slow and costly but can bring a system back.
4. **Score the rest (15 min).** With a partner, score protected area, regulation, and
   biosecurity. For each, name who benefits and who pays. Then pick one response and
   write a one-paragraph recommendation with reasons.
5. **Close (5 min).** In one sentence: what four questions should you ask before
   trusting that a response "works"?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a simple "helps or hurts, and for whom?" two-column note per
  response instead of the full rubric.
- **Extension:** Research one documented restoration or reintroduction project and
  evaluate it against all four criteria with evidence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner score a response on the four criteria,
  name a trade-off, and say who benefits and who pays?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The scored response sheet with the written
  recommendation, added to the living-systems section.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a rule or project in your area meant to protect land, water,
or wildlife. Evaluate it together with the four questions.
