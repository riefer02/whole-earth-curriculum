# Lesson 15 — Origins as Stories: Model, Evidence, and Many Ways of Knowing

## Summary

Learners close the unit by holding the scientific accounts of origins — the Big Bang
model and the evidence for how life diversified — alongside the many cultural and
spiritual stories people tell about beginnings. They practice distinguishing **evidence**
from **values**, ranking no account above the others, and synthesizing the unit's
learning into a portfolio and a considered position.

## Objectives

- Explain the Big Bang model and the evidence that supports it, and distinguish a model
  from revealed truth. _(D06.S4.10.01)_
- Analyze human impacts on Earth systems and evaluate responses to environmental change
  with hope and agency. _(D06.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

Almost every people on Earth has asked, "where did we come from?" — and almost every
people has answered with a story. The scientific story (a universe expanding from a hot
dense state; life diversifying over deep time) is one of these, distinguished by being
built from publicly checkable **evidence**. A spiritual story answers a different kind
of question — not "how?" but "what does it mean, and how should we live?" These are not
rivals for the same seat; they are different kinds of answers to different questions.
Holding them side by side, without ranking, is the mark of a mature mind.

## Materials

- Origins-stories sheet
- Unit portfolio (the artifacts gathered across the unit)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the origins-stories sheet.
- Retrieval (spaced, across the whole unit): from Lesson 13, the Big Bang model and its
  evidence; from Lessons 4–5, inheritance and variation; from Lessons 7–8 and 14, human
  impacts and responses. Today we synthesize and hold accounts in relation.
- Prepare the side-by-side frame and the portfolio list.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is the unit's capstone, and its
heart is philosophy §11: **origins are held as stories grounded in evidence, among many
others — never as dogma.** The idea to land: **the scientific account is a model
grounded in evidence (testable and revisable), not a revealed truth; cultural and
spiritual accounts answer meaning-and-value questions that evidence alone cannot; and
a mature person holds them side by side, distinguishing evidence from values without
ranking one above another.** Model that holding, gently, and let learners articulate
their own considered position — there is no single "correct" answer to require, only
thoughtfulness to encourage.

The **global** lens: every culture has origin stories; the point of the lesson is
plurality, not a contest. The **ethics** lens: treating another person's sacred story
with respect is itself an ethical act; so is not pretending evidence and belief are the
same thing. The **critical-thinking** lens: the disciplined distinction — "this is
evidence; this is a value" — is the unit's capstone skill. The **egalitarian** lens: no
tradition outranks another by birth or geography; knowledge belongs to everyone. Close
by returning to the unit's first question — how do we know, and why should we trust it —
now answered with the whole toolkit.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** In a circle or alone, name one story of beginnings you know —
   scientific, cultural, or spiritual. There are no wrong answers.
2. **Side by side (15 min).** On the sheet, place the scientific model on one side and
   a cultural or spiritual account on the other. For each, note: what question does it
   answer (how? or what does it mean?), what evidence or authority grounds it, and what
   it offers a person. Notice they are not answering the same question.
3. **Evidence and values (15 min).** With a partner, mark each statement you wrote as
   *evidence* (checkable, revisable) or *value* (about meaning, worth, or how to live).
   Discuss: why is it honest to keep these two separate, and why is it also fine to
   hold both at once?
4. **Synthesize (15 min).** Gather your unit portfolio — the data-and-evidence toolkit,
   the living-systems and physical-systems sheets, and the Earth-and-origins
   reflections. Write a short closing statement: *how do I now understand where
   everything came from, and why do I trust — and question — what I know?* Hold the
   scientific account and the stories you grew up with side by side, and say how they
   relate for you.
5. **Close (5 min).** Share one sentence of your synthesis, or keep it in your journal.
   The unit ends with wonder, not a verdict.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the two-column sheet and fill only "what question does it answer?"
  and "what does it offer?"
- **Extension:** Write a short essay articulating how scientific and other ways of
  knowing can relate respectfully, citing one piece of evidence and one value.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner place two accounts side by side,
  distinguish evidence from value, and articulate a considered position without ranking?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed origins-stories sheet and the closing
  synthesis statement, as the capstone of the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you their story of beginnings, and share yours. Wonder
together how the scientific story and theirs can both be true in different ways.
