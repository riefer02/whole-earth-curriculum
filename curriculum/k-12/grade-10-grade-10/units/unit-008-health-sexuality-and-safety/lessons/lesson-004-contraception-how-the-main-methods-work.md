# Lesson 4 — Contraception: How the Main Methods Work

## Summary

Learners learn the main methods of contraception grouped by how they work —
**barrier, hormonal, long-acting, and emergency** — and the key fact that **condoms
are the method that also protects against sexually transmitted infections**, then
reflect on unequal access as a justice issue.

## Objectives

- Analyze the risks and responsibilities of sexual activity using accurate
  information, specifically how the main methods of contraception work and what each
  protects against. _(D03.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

Contraception is not one thing — it is a set of tools that work in different ways,
like the tools in a toolbox. A lock and a key both secure a door, but differently.
Some methods stop an egg and sperm from meeting; some change the body's monthly
cycle; some last for years; some are for right after an unexpected situation. Knowing
the *toolbox* — not just one tool — is what lets two people choose together, with
care, what fits their lives.

## Materials

- Contraception methods chart
- Scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the contraception methods chart and scenario cards.
- Retrieval (spiral): from Lesson 3, how pregnancy begins (egg + sperm + implantation);
  from Lesson 2, consent. Today: the methods that let people decide if and when.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Land the accurate method facts**
(from WHO, S-453): methods fall into **barrier** (external and internal condoms),
**hormonal** (pills, injections, patches, vaginal rings), **long-acting** (implants,
intrauterine devices/IUDs, and permanent methods like sterilization), and
**emergency contraception** (used soon after unprotected sex, not for routine use).
The one fact to make unforgettable: **condoms are the only method that also protects
against sexually transmitted infections, including HIV** — so "dual protection"
(condom plus another method) is how couples get both pregnancy prevention and
infection protection. Condoms are effective at preventing pregnancy **if used
correctly**; effectiveness depends on correct and consistent use. For relationships
where pregnancy is not a possibility (for example, two people who cannot conceive
together), condoms and testing still matter for infection prevention — the skills
apply to every kind of intimacy, and no kind of couple is assumed or ranked.

**Sensitive-topic guidance:** teach methods factually, not as a recommendation to be
or not be sexually active — the decision to be active or abstinent is the learner's
own, and both are treated with respect. Do not distribute or demonstrate physical
contraceptives unless your setting and local law permit it; the chart is enough.
Learners may **opt out** of discussion. If a learner discloses pregnancy risk or
unsafe circumstances, respond privately and follow your safeguarding procedure to a
**trusted adult or health service**.

The **ethics** lens: honest information lets people decide with care rather than by
accident. The **egalitarian** lens: access to contraception is unequal — WHO notes
barriers including age, income, and misinformation — and that unequal access is a
justice issue, not a personal failing. The **global** lens: availability, cost, and
law differ enormously across countries and communities — some provide contraception
free through public health services, while in others cost, distance, or legal limits
put it out of reach (a difference in *access*, never in people's worth). The **technology** lens:
contraception is a technology, old and new (from barrier methods to long-acting
devices), and its *access* — not just its existence — shapes health.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve (5 min).** What must meet for a pregnancy to begin? What is the one
   word that means "deciding if and when"?
2. **Meet the four groups (15 min).** Study the chart. For each group, say what it
   does in one sentence and name one or two methods: barrier (blocks the meeting),
   hormonal (changes the cycle), long-acting (works for months or years), emergency
   (used soon after, not routine).
3. **The key fact (5 min).** Say it together: **condoms are the method that also
   protects against STIs, including HIV.** Practice "dual protection": condom plus
   another method covers both pregnancy and infections.
4. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** In small groups, read each fictional situation.
   Which method group fits? What extra protection should be considered? Check your
   answers against the chart.
5. **Correct use matters (5 min).** A method only works as well as it is used.
   Correct and consistent use is part of responsibility — both partners share it.
6. **Reflect (5 min).** Write one sentence: "The fact I most want to remember is…"
   and one: "A question I still have is…"
7. **Close (5 min).** Preview: pregnancy is one risk; infections are another. Next
   lesson, how to prevent them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on the four group names and the single condom key fact; use the
  drawn chart and a physical sort. For learners who cannot see the chart, read each
  method aloud and sort by hearing, or use four distinct found objects (a stone, a
  leaf, a seed, a stick) to stand in for the four groups.
- **Extension:** Compare two methods on how they work, typical use, and whether they
  protect against STIs, then write a short "what fits which life" note.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four method groups, one
  method in each, and the fact that condoms also protect against STIs including HIV?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "fact I most want to remember / question I still
  have" reflection.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, ask a trusted adult or a health worker how people in your community
access health information about contraception. If that is not comfortable, write the
question for your wonder box. No home conversation is required.
