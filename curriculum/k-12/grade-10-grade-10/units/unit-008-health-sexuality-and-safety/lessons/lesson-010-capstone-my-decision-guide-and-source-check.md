# Lesson 10 — Capstone: My Decision Guide and Source Check

## Summary

Learners assemble the unit's capstone: a **risks-and-responsibilities analysis**
using accurate information about contraception and preventing infections, and a
**source-evaluation** of a real health claim, gathered into the portfolio with a
self-check against both objectives.

## Objectives

- Analyze the risks and responsibilities of sexual activity, including contraception
  and preventing infections, using accurate information. _(D03.S4.10.01)_
- Evaluate sources of health information, including online, peer, and marketing
  sources, for accuracy and bias. _(D03.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

You now hold two tools that most adults never learned together: the ability to weigh
a real decision with accurate information, and the ability to check whether the
information in front of you is even true. Together they are a kind of freedom — the
freedom to decide for yourself, on evidence and your own values, instead of being
decided *for* by rumor, pressure, or an algorithm. This capstone is you proving to
yourself that you can do both.

## Materials

- Capstone template
- Portfolio folder

## Preparation

- Copy the capstone template and self-check rubric.
- Retrieval (spaced, interleaved): the whole unit — the four commitments; the five
  consent conditions; conception; the four method groups and the condom key fact;
  prevent–test–treat; the four-step decision frame; the five source questions; the
  four lateral-reading moves; the three motives.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **The capstone has two parts,** each
mapped to one objective: **(1) A risks-and-responsibilities analysis** — take a
fictional scenario and name at least one risk (pregnancy, infection, emotional or
relational outcome), one responsibility (consent, communication, protection, care),
and one evidence-based option using accurate information about contraception and
infection prevention (S-453, S-454). **(2) A source evaluation** — take a real health
claim (online, peer, or marketing) and run it through the five questions and the four
lateral-reading moves, naming its source, motive, evidence, and bias. Both are
assessed on *reasoning and accuracy*, never on the learner's values or conclusion
(inquiry over creed).

**Sensitive-topic guidance:** learners complete the capstone **privately** and share
**only by choice**. Use fictional scenarios and pre-selected (or self-found, with
personal info removed) claims; no learner discloses their own experience. Normalize
delay and abstinence as complete, valid options. If any work or question suggests a
learner is unsafe or at risk, respond privately and follow your safeguarding
procedure to a **trusted adult or health service**. Celebrate mastery of the *skill*,
never any particular choice.

The **ethics** lens: the capstone is the unit's evidence that a learner can reason
with honesty and care. The **egalitarian** lens: these two skills are distributed
unequally in the world, and learning them is a small equalizer. The **global** lens:
the same skills apply to a claim in any language, any market, any family. The
**technology** lens: the source-check half exists because the internet made both
truth and falsehood cheap to publish. The **critical-thinking** lens: the capstone is
the unit's whole skill, shown.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve, spaced (5 min).** One line each, fast: the four commitments; the five
   consent conditions; the condom key fact; prevent–test–treat; the four-step
   decision frame; the five source questions; the four lateral-reading moves; the
   three motives.
2. **Meet the template (5 min).** Study the two sections and the self-check rubric.
   You will complete both, then check your own work.
3. **Part 1 — Risks and responsibilities (20 min).** Take a fictional scenario.
   Write: one risk, one responsibility, and one evidence-based option, naming at
   least one accurate fact about contraception or infection prevention.
4. **Part 2 — Source evaluation (15 min).** Take a health claim. Write: its source,
   its motive (help, belong, sell), what evidence it offers, what is left out, and
   your verdict — accurate, biased, or false — with the reason.
5. **Self-check (5 min).** Use the rubric: did I name a risk and a responsibility
   with accurate information? Did I identify the claim's source, motive, and bias?
   Mark each "not yet / with help / on my own / can teach."
6. **Share by choice (5 min).** If you want, share one section with a partner or the
   group. No one is required to share anything.
7. **Close and file (5 min).** Add both parts to your portfolio. Write one sentence
   on the cover: what these two skills are for, in your own words.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each section and read the rubric aloud;
   allow the source check to be done on a clearly labeled example.
- **Extension:** Add a third line to Part 1 — "what I would want a partner to know" —
   and a "trace to the original source" step in Part 2 with a link or citation.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio + self + peer):** Does Part 1 name a risk, a
  responsibility, and an evidence-based option using accurate information
  (D03.S4.10.01)? Does Part 2 identify a claim's source, motive, evidence, and bias
  (D03.S4.10.02)? Assessed on reasoning and accuracy, never on values.
- **Performance (by choice):** Explaining one section aloud to a peer or group.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, show a trusted adult your *source check* (not the personal
scenario) and teach them the five questions. If not comfortable, keep the capstone
private in your portfolio. No home conversation is required.
