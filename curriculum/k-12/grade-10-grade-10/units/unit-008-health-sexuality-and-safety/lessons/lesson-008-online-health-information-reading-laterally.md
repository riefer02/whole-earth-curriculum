# Lesson 8 — Online Health Information: Reading Laterally

## Summary

Learners learn to **read laterally** — leave the page and check the source, find
better coverage, and trace claims to their origin — and learn the **red flags** of
online health misinformation, applying the moves to a real claim.

## Objectives

- Evaluate sources of health information, including online sources, for accuracy and
  bias. _(D03.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

When you are told something surprising, a wise move is to ask a second person — not
just the person who told you. Reading online is the same, except the "second person"
is *other pages*: open a new tab and see what the rest of the web says about the
source and the claim. A page can look polished and still be wrong; the test is not
how it looks, but what other sources say about it. This move — called **lateral
reading** — is how fact-checkers separate signal from noise.

## Materials

- Lateral-reading worksheet
- Red-flags list

## Preparation

- Copy the worksheet and red-flags list.
- Retrieval (spiral): from Lesson 7, the five questions (who, why, for whom, what
  evidence, what is left out). Today: the four moves that answer them online.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Land the four moves** (lateral
reading; the verification habit documented by First Draft, S-394, and framed for
learners by Common Sense, S-063): **(1) Stop** — pause before believing or sharing;
**(2) Investigate the source** — open a new tab and find out who is actually behind
the page (not what the page says about itself); **(3) Find better coverage** — see
whether trustworthy sources report the same thing; **(4) Trace claims** — click
through to the original study or quote rather than trusting the summary. A memory aid
calls these **SIFT** (Stop, Investigate, Find, Trace). **Red flags:** miracle cures,
fear and urgency, no author or date, anonymous sources, "doctors hate this," a page
that is really an ad (sponsored content), and claims that only one site makes.

**Sensitive-topic guidance:** some health misinformation targets sexual health and
bodies; keep examples non-graphic, treat claims as material to *verify* (never to
try), and let learners **opt out**. If a claim raises a real concern, respond
privately and follow your safeguarding procedure to a **trusted adult or health
service**. The point is protection through skill, not fear of the internet.

The **ethics** lens: sharing only what you have checked is a form of honesty to
everyone who trusts your feed. The **egalitarian** lens: misinformation spreads
fastest where trust in institutions is lowest — checking is a skill that protects the
most vulnerable first. The **global** lens: the same false health claim can be
translated and re-posted across countries; the moves work in any language. The
**technology** lens: recommendation feeds rank by engagement, not accuracy, so a
compelling falsehood can out-travel a dull truth — exactly why *we* must be the
filter.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve (5 min).** The five questions from Lesson 7 — say them fast. Today we
   learn the four *moves* that answer them online.
2. **Meet the four moves (10 min).** Learn them: **Stop, Investigate the source,
   Find better coverage, Trace the claim.** Say them as a chant. Notice the central
   idea: **leave the page** — the truth is rarely found on the same page that made
   the claim.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Follow a model: a site claims a herb "cures" an
   infection. *Stop.* *Investigate*: the site is anonymous, sells the herb, and is
   not a health authority. *Find better coverage*: trusted health sources say no
   such cure exists. *Trace*: the "study" links back to the same site. The claim
   fails every move.
4. **Practice lateral reading (15 min).** In groups, take a claim. Run all four
   moves — using devices if you have them, or the printed examples. Write what each
   move revealed.
5. **Name the red flags (5 min).** Which flags appeared in your claim? Say them:
   miracle cure, fear, no author, no date, selling, one-sided.
6. **Reflect (5 min).** Write: "Before I share a health claim, I will…"
7. **Close (5 min).** Next: the same skill, applied to the people and ads around us —
   peer pressure and marketing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on the first two moves (Stop, Investigate) with clearly flagged
  examples; offer a "red flag or green flag" sort.
- **Extension:** Take a real claim from your own feed (personal info removed), run
  all four moves, and write a short "verified or not" brief with your evidence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four moves and use them to
  find a source's identity, locate better coverage, and spot at least one red flag?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "before I share, I will…" reflection.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, fact-check one health claim together with a trusted adult using
the four moves. If not comfortable, practice alone on any surprising claim you see.
No home conversation is required.
