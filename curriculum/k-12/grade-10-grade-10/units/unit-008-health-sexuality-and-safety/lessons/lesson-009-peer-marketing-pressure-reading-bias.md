# Lesson 9 — Peer, Marketing, and Pressure: Reading Bias

## Summary

Learners see that health information comes with a **motive** — to *help*, to
*belong*, or to *sell* — and practice detecting bias in peer advice and marketing,
including sponsored content and influencer posts, so they can listen without being
steered.

## Objectives

- Evaluate sources of health information, including peer and marketing sources, for
  accuracy and bias. _(D03.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

A friend who says "everyone's doing it" is usually trying to make you feel *normal*,
not to give you facts — and "everyone" is often fewer people than it sounds. An ad
that shows beautiful, confident people using a product is selling a *feeling*, not a
fact. Neither is lying exactly; each has a motive. Reading the motive — help, belong,
or sell — is how you keep your decisions yours, made on evidence and your own values
rather than on someone else's purpose.

## Materials

- Motive cards
- Bias-detector worksheet

## Preparation

- Copy the motive cards and bias-detector worksheet.
- Retrieval (spiral): from Lesson 7, the five questions; from Lesson 8, the four
  moves and red flags. Today: the same tools aimed at *people* — peers and
  marketers.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Land the three motives** (using
NAMLE, S-151, and WHO's marketing framing, S-312): **to help** (a clinic, a health
authority, a professional — motive: your wellbeing), **to belong** (a peer or social
group — motive: connection, fitting in, sometimes shared pressure), and **to sell**
(a company, a sponsored influencer — motive: profit). Teach learners to ask **"what
does this person gain if I believe them?"** A marketer gains money; a friend gains
your company or agreement; an expert gains nothing from your choice — which is a clue
to trust, though even experts can be wrong, so verify anyway. Show how **sponsored
content and influencer posts** blur "help" and "sell" on purpose, which is why
disclosure labels and the five questions matter.

**Sensitive-topic guidance:** peer-pressure examples may touch on sex and bodies;
keep them non-graphic and use fictional scenarios. Explicitly normalize resisting
pressure — saying no, waiting, or checking facts — as strength, never weakness.
Learners may **opt out**. If a message or disclosure suggests coercion or unsafe
circumstances, respond privately and follow your safeguarding procedure to a
**trusted adult or health service**.

The **ethics** lens: persuading with facts is honest; persuading with manufactured
fear or false "everyone does it" is not. The **egalitarian** lens: marketing
disproportionately targets the young, the uncertain, and the under-served — reading
bias is a shield for those with the least to spare. The **global** lens: the same
product is sold with different emotional appeals in different cultures, but the
motive — sell — is the same. The **technology** lens: algorithms place marketing
beside genuine help so they look identical, which is precisely why motive-reading is
a survival skill online. The **social-emotional** lens: wanting to belong is human
and good — the skill is noticing when that want is being used.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve (5 min).** The five questions (Lesson 7) and the four moves (Lesson 8)
   — say them fast. Today we aim them at *people*.
2. **Meet the three motives (10 min).** Learn them: **help, belong, sell.** For each,
   ask: what does this person *gain* if I believe them? A seller gains money; a peer
   gains company; an expert gains nothing — a clue worth noticing.
3. **Role-play the voices (10 min).** Three volunteers read the same health advice
   as a caring expert, a friend ("everyone's doing it"), and a marketer ("change
   your life"). The group names each motive and the tell-tale sign.
4. **Sort the messages (15 min).** In small groups, sort the message cards into
   help, belong, or sell — and for each, name the tell: a source? a fear? an
   "everyone"? a price? Check your sorting against the key.
5. **Spot the blur (5 min).** Look at a sponsored post or influencer message. Where
   does "sell" disguise itself as "help"? What label gives it away?
6. **Reflect (5 min).** Write: "When a friend or an ad tells me something about my
   health or body, I will ask…"
7. **Close (5 min).** Tomorrow we bring it all together into your own decision guide
   and source check.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on help vs. sell with clearly labeled examples; use the
  three-column sort.
- **Extension:** Find a real influencer post or ad (personal info removed), identify
  undisclosed sponsorship, and write a short "what they gain" analysis.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three motives, sort messages
  by motive, and identify the "what do they gain" question for a sponsored post?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "when a friend or ad tells me…" reflection.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, look at a product ad with a trusted adult and name its motive
together. If not comfortable, practice on any ad you see. No home conversation is
required.
