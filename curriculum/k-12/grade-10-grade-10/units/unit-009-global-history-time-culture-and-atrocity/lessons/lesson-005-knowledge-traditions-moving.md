# Lesson 5 — Knowledge Traditions Moving Across the World

## Summary

Learners trace real routes by which knowledge moved across the world — paper from
China, the decimal system and zero from India, and scholarly manuscripts preserved at
Timbuktu — and weigh where credit was given and where it was erased in translation.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the ethics of sharing, borrowing, and taking knowledge and culture across
  peoples, and who has been credited or harmed. _(D08.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

The number you write as "0" and the paper you write it on both traveled thousands of
kilometers, through many hands and languages, to reach you. Along the way, each idea
changed and — sometimes — its makers' names were forgotten while the idea kept their
stamp. Tracing how knowledge actually moves is how we see both the brilliance of
exchange and the quiet erasure that can ride with it.

## Materials

- Knowledge-journey map
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the knowledge-journey map.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall the difference between sharing, borrowing, and
  taking (consent, credit, power, harm). Today we look at real journeys and ask
  where credit held and where it dropped.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **knowledge moves
through trade, translation, and apprenticeship — and while exchange enriches
everyone, the record has often named the receiver and not the maker, which is a
question of credit and power, not of accuracy.** Teach the three journeys as worked
examples before learners weigh credit on their own (S-011).

The **global** lens is the spine: paper was invented in China and refined around
105 CE, carried along the Silk Roads through Central Asia to Baghdad's paper mills
and into Europe by the 1100s (S-264); the decimal place-value system and zero were
developed in India and transmitted through Arabic/Islamic scholars to Europe,
changing calculation everywhere (S-272, S-023); and Timbuktu under Songhai rule
(1464–1591) kept a great manuscript tradition of astronomy, mathematics, medicine,
and law, preserved by families for centuries (S-268, S-201). The **ethics** and
**egalitarian** lenses: ask learners *who is named* in each story — too often the
last receiver, not the first maker — and what that erasure costs. The **technology**
lens: paper and the decimal system are *technologies of knowledge* whose spread
remade what people could record and compute. Preview: Lesson 6 turns to how global
trade and technology transform cultural *traditions* today.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what separates taking from sharing? (consent
   and credit.) Now: where did the numeral "0" come from? Keep the question open.
2. **Meet the map (15 min).** Trace three journeys on the map:
   - **Paper:** invented in China, refined c. 105 CE; carried along the Silk Roads to
     Baghdad's mills, then into Europe by the 1100s (S-264).
   - **Decimal system and zero:** developed in India; transmitted through
     Arabic/Islamic scholars (e.g., al-Khwarizmi's 9th-century work) to Europe,
     where they transformed calculation (S-272, S-023).
   - **Timbuktu's manuscripts:** a scholarly tradition of astronomy, mathematics,
     medicine, and law preserved by families through centuries of trade and empire
     (S-268, S-201).
   Watch one worked example: at each step of paper's journey, ask — *is the maker
   still named, or only the receiver?* Notice where "paper" loses its Chinese origin
   in the telling.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take the zero's journey. At each step,
   name who made it and who is usually credited in a textbook you have seen. Compare
   and agree on where credit held and where it dropped.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph: how can an
   idea's origin be lost as it travels, and why does naming the maker matter?
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: what does translation and trade carry,
   besides the idea itself? Next time: how trade and technology transform traditions
   today.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Follow only paper's journey and answer two guided questions about who
  is named.
- **Extension:** Research one more transmission (e.g., the spread of printing, or of
  a medical tradition) and trace its credit trail with a source.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe each route as a spoken journey (paper:
  China → Baghdad → Europe; zero: India → Baghdad → Europe; manuscripts: kept at
  Timbuktu), and lay yarn on a flat surface for each route so the learner can trace
  it with a finger while you name each stop.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace one journey and name where
  credit held and where it was erased?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The credit-trail paragraph, added to the unit's
  "knowledge and culture" section.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *is there something we use every day (a number, a food, a tool) whose
origin we do not know?* Together, find its origin story and name the people who made
it.

## Resources

- On the non-European roots of mathematics: George Gheverghese Joseph, *The Crest of

- On Timbuktu's manuscript tradition: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Timbuktu"
