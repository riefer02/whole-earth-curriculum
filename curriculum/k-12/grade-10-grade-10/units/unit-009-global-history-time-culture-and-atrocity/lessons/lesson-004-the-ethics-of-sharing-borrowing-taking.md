# Lesson 4 — The Ethics of Sharing, Borrowing, and Taking

## Summary

Learners evaluate the ethics of how knowledge and culture move between peoples. Using
consent, credit, power, and harm as criteria, they sort cases as *sharing*,
*borrowing*, or *taking*, and examine what happens when knowledge is suppressed and
later revived.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the ethics of sharing, borrowing, and taking knowledge and culture across
  peoples, and who has been credited or harmed. _(D08.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Imagine a friend shows you a song they wrote, and you sing it at a gathering. If you
said "this is my friend's song," you *shared* it with credit. If you sang it without
naming them, you *took* it — and they might feel erased. The same question runs
through history, across whole peoples: when knowledge and culture move from one
people to another, who is credited, who consents, and who is harmed?

## Materials

- "Share / Borrow / Take" case cards
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or write the three case cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall that knowledge traditions can be suppressed,
  marginalized, or lost through conquest, and that peoples work to preserve and
  revive them (S-474, S-424).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the ethics of how
knowledge and culture move turns on consent, credit, power, and harm — sharing is
given and credited, borrowing is taken with credit and a continuing relationship, and
taking is done without consent or credit, at the cost of the source people.** Model
the three-way sort with a worked case before learners rule on their own (S-011).

The **ethics** lens is the spine: the same object (a song, a medicine, a script) can
be shared, borrowed, or taken depending on consent and credit. The **egalitarian**
lens: power decides whose knowledge is valued and whose is taken as "raw material" —
colonizers suppressed Maya codices while keeping what they learned from them, and
renamed medicines and foods without the peoples who stewarded them (S-474, S-427).
The **global** lens: examples span continents — the Maya bark-paper codices, African
and Indigenous ecological knowledge, and languages under pressure to assimilate. The
**critical-thinking** lens: "credit" and "harm" are judgments — name your criterion
and test it against a hard case. Preview: Lesson 5 traces how knowledge actually
*moved* across the world, and who got written out of the record.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: name one way a people's knowledge has been
   suppressed, and one way it has been revived. (e.g., Spanish authorities destroyed
   most Maya codices; communities and scholars now study and revive Maya astronomy.)
2. **Meet the sort (15 min).** Look at the three-column chart.
   - **Sharing:** given with consent, credited, and kept in relationship.
   - **Borrowing:** taken with credit and a continuing relationship — the borrower
     names the source and the exchange continues.
   - **Taking:** done without consent or credit, at the cost of the source people —
     often by a more powerful party from a less powerful one.
   Watch one worked case: a colonial authority copies a people's calendar and
   medicine, bans the people from practicing them, and publishes them under its own
   name — sort it as *taking*, and say why (no consent, no credit, harm).
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, sort the three case cards. For each, name
   the criterion that decided it: consent, credit, power, or harm. Compare and agree
   before moving on.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph ruling on a
   hard case: when is borrowing also taking? State your criterion and apply it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: what separates taking from sharing?
   Next time: the real routes knowledge traveled.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only two cards (sharing and taking) and a two-column sort.
- **Extension:** Draft a short "ethics of exchange" rubric (consent, credit, power,
  harm) and apply it to a current cultural or knowledge dispute you know of.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** The three columns are already spoken in the
  Procedure (sharing, borrowing, taking), so the sort works by voice alone; offer a
  paper folded into three sections, or simply sort the cases aloud.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner sort a case correctly and name the
  criterion (consent, credit, power, harm) that decided it?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-paragraph hard-case ruling, kept in the
  unit's "knowledge and culture" section.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *is there a recipe, song, or skill in our family that came from
another people — and do we know who to credit?* Together, find one thing and name
whose it was.
