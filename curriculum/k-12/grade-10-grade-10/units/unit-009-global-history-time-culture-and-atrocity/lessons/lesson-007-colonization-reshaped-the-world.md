# Lesson 7 — Colonization Reshaped the World

## Summary

Learners analyze how colonization reshaped populations, environments, and borders
across the world, and its effects on Indigenous peoples. They work with three
documented reshapings — epidemic and displacement, extraction and introduced
species, and borders drawn across peoples' lands — with care and accuracy.

## Objectives

- Analyze how colonization reshaped populations, environments, and borders across
  the world, and its effects on Indigenous peoples. _(D08.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Look at a map of almost any continent and you will see lines that cut straight
through mountains, rivers, and the lands of a single people. Those lines were often
drawn far away, by people who had never walked the land. Colonization — the rule of
one people over another's land and lives — reshaped not just maps but populations and
environments, and its effects are still lived today. Studying it honestly means
seeing both what was taken and how peoples endured.

## Materials

- "Three reshapings" chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-column chart.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall that European colonization spread globally from the
  late 1400s, taking land and suppressing languages and practices (S-326). Today we
  analyze its *scale*: populations, environments, borders.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **colonization
reshaped the world along three axes — populations (through epidemic and
displacement), environments (through extraction and introduced species), and borders
(drawn across peoples' lands) — and its effects on Indigenous peoples are real,
documented, and still lived.** Model the three columns with worked examples before
independent analysis (S-011).

**Emotional safety:** this is difficult but not graphic content. Give a brief warning
at the start ("today covers real harms — loss of land, life, and self-rule"), tell
learners they may step out or pass at any time, name a trusted adult or counselor for
anyone who needs it, and ground the class afterward (a slow breath, a quiet minute).
Do not show graphic images; keep numbers brief and human. Always pair the loss with
agency and endurance — peoples survived, resisted, and rebuilt (S-274, S-273).

The **egalitarian** lens is the spine: colonization is a system of power — who gained
and who was dispossessed is the question. The **global** lens: this happened across
the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania, not in one place (S-326). The **technology**
and **environment** lenses: ships, firearms, and introduced species (the Columbian
Exchange moved crops, animals, and disease in both directions, devastating Indigenous
peoples while remaking landscapes, S-273); and the transatlantic trade forcibly
displaced millions of African people (S-274, S-322). The **ethics** lens: naming the
harm accurately is a duty to the truth, not a verdict on any person today. Preview:
Lesson 8 centers Indigenous effects and endurance in depth.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: when did European colonization spread globally,
   and what did it take? (Land, self-rule, languages — from the late 1400s.)
2. **Meet the chart (15 min).** Look at the three-column chart.
   - **Populations:** epidemic disease and displacement devastated Indigenous
     peoples; the transatlantic trade forcibly moved millions of African people
     (S-273, S-274).
   - **Environments:** extraction (minerals, forests, soils) and introduced species
     (crops, animals, and pathogens) remade landscapes in both hemispheres (S-273).
   - **Borders:** lines drawn far away — often at conferences with no one from the
     land present — cut through peoples, languages, and ways of life (S-326).
   Watch one worked example: trace one region through all three columns, and name
   both the harm and the endurance of the people there.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take one column. Give one documented
   example and one effect on Indigenous peoples. Compare and agree before moving on.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph on the
   reshaping that lasted longest — population, environment, or borders — and why.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: what three things did colonization
   reshape, and what must we also remember? Next time: Indigenous effects and
   endurance.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one column (borders) with a guided example and two questions.
- **Extension:** Trace one colonial-era border to a present-day consequence, with a
  source, and explain the link.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** The three reshapings (populations, environments,
  borders) are fully spoken in the Procedure, so the lesson works by voice; use a
  paper folded into three sections, or read each example aloud with no chart at all.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three reshapings and give a
  documented example with an effect on Indigenous peoples for one of them?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-paragraph "longest-lasting reshaping"
  analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *do we know whose land our region was, and how its borders were drawn?*
Together, find one border or place name and trace where it came from.
