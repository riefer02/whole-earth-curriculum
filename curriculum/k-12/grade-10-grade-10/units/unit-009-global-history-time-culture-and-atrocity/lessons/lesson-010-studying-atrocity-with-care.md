# Lesson 10 — Studying Atrocity with Care: A Case Study

## Summary

Learners establish how to study genocide and mass violence with care. They meet the
concept of genocide as a defined international crime, learn a three-part framework
(causes, survivors, remembrance), and commit to care rules that keep human beings —
never numbers — at the center. This lesson opens the unit's most careful section.

## Objectives

- Analyze a case of genocide or mass violence with care, examining its causes, the
  responses of survivors, and how it is remembered. _(D08.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

When a terrible thing happens to one person, we can hold that one person's name and
face. When it happens to millions, our minds can go numb — research shows compassion
can *decrease* as the number of victims grows, and a single named person moves us
far more than a statistic (S-414). That is why studying atrocity must begin with a
rule: translate statistics back into people, one name at a time. That is the only
honest and humane way to look.

## Materials

- "Studying atrocity with care" framework
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the framework.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall that learners analyze empire, liberation, and
  forced migration with care and respect, and that dignity and agency are centered
  even in the hardest histories.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **genocide is a
defined international crime — the attempted destruction of a people — and studying
it with care means examining causes, centering survivors, and asking how it is
remembered, while never flattening victims or reducing anyone to a number.** Teach
the framework explicitly before any case detail (S-011).

**Emotional safety (non-negotiable for this section):** (1) **Warning and opt-out:**
state clearly that these lessons cover genocide — real mass killing and its causes —
and that any learner may step out, pass, or write privately instead of speaking at
any time, with no penalty. (2) **Referral:** name a trusted adult, counselor, or
support line available if feelings rise. (3) **Grounding:** open and close with a
slow breath or a quiet minute; keep the room calm and unhurried. (4) **No graphic
images or gratuitous detail:** describe events in brief, dignified summary; do not
show photographs of bodies or violence. (5) **Agency and hope:** every lesson in this
section ends by turning to survival, resistance, shelter, and rebuilding — the truth
is never only the horror. These rules follow the consensus guidance of memorial
institutions on teaching about genocide (S-495).

The **ethics** lens is the spine: how we study the worst of humanity is itself an
ethical act — with dignity or without. The **egalitarian** lens: genocide targets a
people *as a people*; understanding that requires seeing every person as equal in
worth. The **global** lens: genocide is not one place's history — the term was coined
by the lawyer Raphael Lemkin (1944) and defined in the 1948 UN Convention, and it
applies to cases across eras and continents, including colonial contexts (S-491,
S-494). The **critical-thinking** lens: the framework (causes, survivors,
remembrance) is a way to think, not a script to memorize. Preview: Lessons 11–13
move through survivors, remembrance, and the ordinary people who resisted and
rebuilt.

## Procedure

1. **Ground (3 min).** Sit quietly. Breathe once, slowly. Know that this is a hard
   and important thing to study, and that you may pause or step away at any time.
2. **Meet the framework (20 min).** Look at the care framework.
   - **What "genocide" means:** a defined international crime — the attempted
     destruction of a people, in whole or in part, *as a people*. The word was
     coined by Raphael Lemkin (1944) and defined in the 1948 UN Convention (S-491).
   - **Three questions to study a case:** What were its *causes* (how did it
     escalate)? Who were its *survivors* (how did they respond and endure)? How is
     it *remembered* (memorials, testimony, justice)?
   - **The care rules:** no graphic detail; name individuals; never flatten victims
     or reduce people to numbers; hold agency and hope alongside the truth (S-495).
   Watch one worked example: meet the concept, then a single named survivor's story
   told in brief, dignified summary — not a statistic.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, ask only: *why does "name individuals,
   not numbers" matter?* and *why must we study causes, not just events?* Compare
   one answer each.
4. **Independent work (10 min).** In your journal, write three lines: one thing you
   will remember about *how* to study this, one question you hold, and one name or
   story you want to honor carefully.
5. **Close and ground (5 min).** Breathe once more. Remember: the truth is never
   only the horror — the next lessons turn to survival, resistance, and rebuilding.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work only with the care rules and one named individual's story; skip
  the causes/stages detail for now.
- **Extension:** Read the definition of genocide in the 1948 Convention and explain,
  in your own words, why "as a people" is central.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Read the three spokes (causes, survivors,
  remembrance) and the care rules aloud in order — the framework is a list, not a
  picture — and offer a circle of yarn with three spokes to feel, if wanted.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state the three study questions (causes,
  survivors, remembrance) and at least two care rules, in their own words?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-line journal reflection, kept private
  unless the learner chooses to share it.

## Home connection

Choose a trusted adult. Tell them one thing you learned about *how* to study hard
history with care, and ask what they think "remember with dignity" means. (No one is
required to discuss the events themselves outside class.)
