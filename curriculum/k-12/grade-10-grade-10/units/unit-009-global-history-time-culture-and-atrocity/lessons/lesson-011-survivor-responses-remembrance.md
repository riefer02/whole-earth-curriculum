# Lesson 11 — Survivor Responses and Remembrance

## Summary

Learners examine how survivors respond and endure, and how a genocide is remembered
— through testimony, memorials, museums, days of remembrance, and justice. They
practice holding remembrance with accuracy and dignity, and meet survivors as
agents, not only as the harmed.

## Objectives

- Analyze a case of genocide or mass violence with care, examining its causes, the
  responses of survivors, and how it is remembered. _(D08.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

Think of how your own family keeps someone who has died: a photograph, a story told
at a certain table, a name spoken on a certain day. Remembrance is how the living
refuse to let the dead and the harmed be forgotten. For a genocide, that work is
done at the scale of a whole people — through testimony and memorials and museums and
days of remembrance — and it is always, at bottom, the same human act: keeping a name.

## Materials

- "Remembering with dignity" sheet
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or write the remembrance sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, recall the care rules and the three study questions
  (causes, survivors, remembrance). Today we dwell on the last two — survivors and
  remembrance — with care.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **survivors respond
and endure in countless ways — through witness, rebuilding, and carrying memory —
and remembrance takes many forms (testimony, memorials, museums, days of
remembrance, justice), all of which exist to keep individuals and peoples from being
forgotten.** Model the forms of remembrance with a worked example before learners
reflect (S-011).

**Emotional safety (non-negotiable):** (1) **Warning and opt-out:** re-state that
learners may step out, pass, or write privately at any time. (2) **Referral:** name
the trusted adult or support line again. (3) **Grounding:** open and close with a
slow breath or a minute of quiet. (4) **No graphic images or gratuitous detail:** use
brief, dignified summaries and one survivor's words chosen for dignity, never for
shock. (5) **Agency and hope:** survivors are met as agents — people who bore
witness, rebuilt families and communities, and chose how to carry memory (S-495).

The **ethics** lens is the spine: remembrance is a duty to the truth and to the
dignity of the dead and the harmed. The **egalitarian** lens: memorials and justice
exist because every person's life counts equally. The **global** lens: remembrance
takes many forms across peoples — days of remembrance, museums, and testimony exist
for cases across continents, and the duty to remember is not one people's alone
(S-491, S-494). The **critical-thinking** lens: different forms of remembrance serve
different purposes — testimony preserves a voice, a museum teaches, a court seeks
accountability — and none replaces the others. Preview: Lesson 12 turns to the
ordinary people who resisted and sheltered others.

## Procedure

1. **Ground (3 min).** Breathe once, slowly. Recall that you may pause or step away
   at any time.
2. **Meet remembrance (20 min).** Look at the remembrance sheet.
   - **How survivors respond:** bearing witness, rebuilding family and community,
     teaching, and carrying memory forward.
   - **How it is remembered:** testimony (a survivor's own words), memorials and
     museums, days of remembrance, and justice (courts and accountability).
   Watch one worked example: read one named survivor's words, in brief and dignified
   summary, and notice what testimony preserves that a statistic cannot.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, ask: *what does testimony preserve that a
   number cannot?* and *why do we need both a memorial and a court?* Share one
   answer each.
4. **Independent work (10 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph: how would
   you want a harmed people to be remembered — and what would you do to help carry
   that memory?
5. **Close and ground (5 min).** Breathe once more. Next time: the ordinary people
   who resisted and sheltered others — the hope inside the truth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one remembrance practice and one survivor's words.
- **Extension:** Compare testimony and a court of justice as two forms of
  remembrance — what each can do, and what each cannot.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the symbol once aloud (a steady flame
  above a book of names), then teach from the spoken list of remembrance forms; the
  image is illustrative, and the list is the lesson.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name at least three forms of remembrance and
  say, in their own words, what testimony preserves that a number cannot?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The remembrance paragraph, kept private unless
  shared by choice.

## Home connection

With a trusted adult, talk about one way your family or community remembers people
who are gone — and one reason remembrance matters. (No one is required to discuss
the events themselves outside class.)
