# Lesson 3 — Periodizing a Global Era: Arguing for Turning Points

## Summary

Learners treat periodization — dividing history into periods — as an *argument*, not
a fact. Using a worked example, they divide a global era into periods, choose turning
points, and defend them with evidence, then practice answering a counterclaim.

## Objectives

- Argue for a way to divide a global era into periods and defend where its turning
  points should fall. _(D08.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Every time you tell a story you divide it: "before," "when everything changed," and
"after." You might say a friendship changed "when we moved," or "when the drought
came." Historians do the same thing at the scale of the whole world, calling the
dividing lines "turning points." But two people can pick different turning points for
the same story — and both can be reasonable. Periodization is an argument you defend,
not an answer you find.

## Materials

- Periodization worked example
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the periodization worked example.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, recall that time is counted many ways and that its
  shape changes what is remembered (S-315). Today we argue about *where to draw the
  lines*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **periodization is a
claim about where the most important changes fall, and it is defended with evidence
and by answering a counterclaim — there is no single "correct" dividing line.** This
is a foundational skill, so teach it with an explicit worked example and guided
practice before independent argument (S-011).

The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: a turning point is a *judgment about
significance*, so two defensible periodizations can disagree. The **global** lens:
the worked example divides a *global* era (roughly 1450–1800), so a turning point
must matter across regions — the Columbian Exchange that joined the hemispheres and
began colonizing the Americas (S-273, S-326) is a strong candidate, but so is the
later industrial take-off, and a learner may defend a different one. The
**egalitarian** lens: *whose* turning point counts? A line that marks "discovery" for
one people marks invasion for another — ask learners to name for whom the turning
point turned. The **ethics** lens: dividing the past is never neutral; it is a choice
about what matters, so say what you are counting as significant. Preview: Lesson 4
moves to the ethics of how knowledge and culture move between peoples.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier: what is one thing a calendar or a shape of time
   changes about memory? Now think of one event in your life that "changed
   everything" — that is your turning point.
2. **Meet the worked example (15 min).** Look at the periodization example. To divide
   a global era (say 1450–1800) into periods:
   - **Propose turning points:** where did change run deepest across regions? (e.g.,
     c. 1492, the Columbian Exchange joining hemispheres; c. 1760s–1800s, the
     industrial take-off.)
   - **Give evidence for each:** what actually changed, and for whom? (new crops,
     peoples, and power moving across oceans; machines remaking work and cities.)
   - **Defend against a counterclaim:** "but 1492 didn't change everyone at once" —
     answer it (changes were uneven, yet the *system* of connected hemispheres dates
     from then).
   - **State what you are counting as significant:** movements of people, power, and
     environment — not just one region's events.
   Watch this worked example once, step by step.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take the same era. Choose one turning
   point you would move or replace, give one piece of evidence, and answer one
   counterclaim. Compare and agree on what makes a defense strong.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write a three-part periodization
   of a global era you choose (1450–1800, or 1800–present). For each turning point:
   one evidence sentence and one counterclaim you can answer.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: why is periodization an argument, not a
   fact? Next time: the ethics of sharing and taking knowledge and culture.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a half-built periodization with the turning points given and the
  evidence blank, to fill in.
- **Extension:** Defend a *competing* periodization — argue that a commonly accepted
  turning point should be moved or that a quieter one matters more.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Lay a rope straight as the timeline, with knots
  or stones at each turning point, and read the dates and evidence notes aloud in
  order; the argument (propose → evidence → answer a counterclaim) is fully spoken,
  so no seeing is required.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner propose a turning point, support it
  with evidence, and answer one counterclaim?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written periodization with evidence and a
  counterclaim, as the unit's "defended argument" artifact.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *where does our family's story "turn"?* (A move, a birth, a season, a
decision.) Together, write that turning point, one piece of evidence it mattered, and
one reason someone else might place it differently.
