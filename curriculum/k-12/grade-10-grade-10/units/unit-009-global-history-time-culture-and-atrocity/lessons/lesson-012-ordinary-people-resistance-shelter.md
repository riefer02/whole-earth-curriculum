# Lesson 12 — Ordinary People: Resistance and Shelter

## Summary

Learners analyze how ordinary people resisted oppression and sheltered others during
genocide and mass violence. They meet rescuers and resisters as agents who chose
courage at great risk, and begin to ask what remembrance and justice require of us.

## Objectives

- Analyze how ordinary people have resisted oppression, sheltered others, and
  rebuilt after atrocity, and what remembrance and justice require. _(D08.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

Most of the people who resisted or sheltered others in the worst of times were not
heroes from a story — they were neighbors, farmers, teachers, and students who made a
choice at a moment when choosing was dangerous. Their acts were often small: a door
left open, a child hidden, a warning passed, a truth told. Studying them reminds us
that courage is not a rare gift but an ordinary human possibility, taken up one
choice at a time.

## Materials

- "Ordinary people" case cards
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or write the three case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 10–11, recall the care rules and the three study
  questions. Today we turn to the hope inside the truth: the people who resisted and
  sheltered.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **ordinary people —
neighbors, strangers, whole communities — resisted and sheltered others during
genocide, and their choices show that agency and courage lived even inside atrocity;
remembrance and justice honor that agency.** Model one case before learners study the
rest (S-011).

**Emotional safety (non-negotiable):** (1) **Warning and opt-out:** re-state that
learners may step out, pass, or write privately at any time. (2) **Referral:** name
the trusted adult or support line. (3) **Grounding:** open and close with a slow
breath. (4) **No graphic images or gratuitous detail:** tell the cases in brief,
dignified summary, focused on the *choice* and its cost, never on violence. (5)
**Agency and hope:** this lesson *is* the agency-and-hope turn — center the ordinary
people who chose courage (S-495).

The **ethics** lens is the spine: sheltering is an ethical act — choosing another's
life over one's own safety — and studying it asks what we ourselves would count as a
duty. The **egalitarian** lens: rescuers came from every background, which teaches
that dignity and courage are not the property of any rank or class. The **global**
lens: ordinary people sheltered and resisted in many places and eras — the Righteous
Among the Nations, honored by Yad Vashem, are non-Jews across Europe who risked
their lives to rescue Jews (S-492), and Rwandans who sheltered neighbors are honored
in Rwanda's remembrance (S-494). The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *why* some
chose courage and others did not — not to judge, but to understand how choices are
made under pressure. Preview: Lesson 13 turns to rebuilding and what justice
requires.

## Procedure

1. **Ground (3 min).** Breathe once, slowly. Recall you may pause or step away.
2. **Meet the cases (20 min).** Look at the case cards — three ordinary people who
   resisted or sheltered others. For each, notice: the *choice* they made, the *risk*
   they took, and the *ordinary* fact of who they were. Watch one worked example:
   a neighbor who hid a family in a small room, or passed a warning, or refused an
   order — a choice made at great cost, by someone no more extraordinary than anyone
   else.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take one case. Name the choice, the risk,
   and one reason the person might have given for acting. Compare and agree.
4. **Independent work (10 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph: what makes
   an "ordinary" act extraordinary, and what would you hope you would choose?
5. **Close and ground (5 min).** Breathe once more. Next time: rebuilding, and what
   justice requires.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one case and two questions (what did they choose, what did
  it cost).
- **Extension:** Compare two forms of resistance — sheltering another and openly
  refusing an order — and analyze the different risks and effects of each.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the scene once aloud (a family welcomed
  through an open door, hands passing food), then teach from the spoken cases; the
  image illustrates, the cases carry the lesson.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name an ordinary person's choice to
  resist or shelter, the risk, and why it mattered?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The "ordinary and extraordinary" paragraph.

## Home connection

With a trusted adult, talk about one ordinary act of courage or kindness you have
seen or heard of — and what made it brave. (No one is required to discuss the events
themselves outside class.)
