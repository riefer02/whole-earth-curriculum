# Lesson 2 — Time and the Sense of the Past

## Summary

Learners weigh how the *shape* a society gives to time — a returning circle or a
forward arrow, a spoken story or a written record — changes what it remembers as its
past. They compare oral traditions, dynastic annals, and calendars, and ask what each
keeps and what each lets go.

## Objectives

- Compare how different societies measured and recorded time and weigh how those
  systems shaped their sense of the past. _(D08.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Ask yourself what you remember about last year. You probably remember it as a line —
"first this happened, then that" — moving from your birth toward now. But imagine
remembering instead as a circle: the same rains, the same festival, the same
ancestors returning each year, so that "now" always touches "then." The shape you
give to time decides what you remember. Different peoples have chosen different
shapes.

## Materials

- "Cycle and arrow" diagram
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the cycle-and-arrow diagram.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall that a calendar matches the Sun's year and the
  Moon's month to a people's seasons, and that each system watches something
  different (S-315).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the shape a people
gives to time — a returning cycle that keeps ancestors near, or a forward line from a
fixed beginning — shapes what its history remembers, and both shapes are real, living
ways of holding the past.** Model the contrast with two worked examples before
independent work (S-011).

The **global** lens: many Indigenous peoples hold time as cycles that carry ancestors
and land forward together (a living, recurring relationship rather than a finished
line); Chinese dynastic historiography recorded time as repeating dynastic cycles
rise-and-fall while keeping continuous written annals; the Islamic Hijri calendar
marks a community's shared era from the Hijra (622 CE); a linear "era" that counts
forward from one founding moment is itself a choice, not a universal (S-315, S-378).
The **technology** lens: writing changes memory — an oral tradition lives by
re-telling and shifts with each teller, while a written annal fixes one version and
can be kept for centuries, each with its own losses and gains. The **ethics** lens:
whose record was written down and kept, and whose was carried in speech and often
ignored, is a question of power. The **critical-thinking** lens: neither shape is
"better"; each preserves something and lets something else fade — hold both.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: name one calendar and what it watches. Now
   think: does your memory of your own life feel like a line or a circle?
2. **Meet the diagram (15 min).** Look at the cycle-and-arrow diagram.
   - **Cyclical time:** time returns — seasons, festivals, ancestors; the past is
     always near, renewed each cycle. Many Indigenous calendars and oral traditions
     work this way.
   - **Linear time:** time moves forward from a fixed beginning toward now; the past
     is "behind" us. Era-dated calendars and written annals work this way.
   - **Spoken vs. written:** an oral tradition is re-told and stays alive and
     shifting; a written annal fixes a record that outlives any single voice.
   Watch one worked example: the same event (a founding, a harvest, a ruler's reign)
   told as a returning story versus recorded as a dated entry — notice what each
   preserves.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, answer: what does a cyclical record keep
   that a linear one might let fade? What does a linear record keep that a cyclical
   one might not? Agree on one answer for each before moving on.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write two short entries: one
   memory told as a circle (something that returns each year), one as a line
   (something that happened once, in order). Then write one sentence on how the
   *shape* changed what you chose to include.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: how does the shape of time change the
   sense of the past? Next time: how we divide the long human story into periods.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the circle/line contrast, with a half-written example to
  finish.
- **Extension:** Compare a cyclical and a linear record of the same real event (e.g.,
  a dynastic annal vs. a seasonal/ceremonial account) and argue what each loses.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Give the learner a closed loop of string
  (cyclical — seasons return, the past always near) and a straight string (linear —
  a line from a fixed beginning to now) to hold while you describe each; the
  contrast is tactile, not only drawn.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name one thing cyclical time keeps and
  one thing linear time keeps, and link each to a society's way of recording?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two journal entries (circle and line) with the
  one-sentence reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *in our family, what returns every year that keeps the past close — a
holiday, a visit, a food, a story?* Write it as a circle, then as a line, and notice
the difference.
