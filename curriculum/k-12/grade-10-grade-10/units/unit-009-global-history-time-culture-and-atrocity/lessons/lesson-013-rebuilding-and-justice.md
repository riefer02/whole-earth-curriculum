# Lesson 13 — Rebuilding and What Justice Requires

## Summary

Learners analyze how communities rebuild after genocide and mass violence, and what
justice requires — truth, accountability, repair, and remembrance. They weigh what
each path can and cannot do, and close by turning toward the unit's synthesis.

## Objectives

- Analyze how ordinary people have resisted oppression, sheltered others, and
  rebuilt after atrocity, and what remembrance and justice require. _(D08.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

After a fire, a family does not only mourn the house — it also clears the ash, tells
the truth about what happened, holds anyone responsible accountable, and rebuilds so
that life can continue. After atrocity, whole communities face the same four tasks at
a vast scale: tell the truth, seek accountability, repair what can be repaired, and
remember. Justice is not one act but these four things held together.

## Materials

- "After atrocity" chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four-path chart.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 10–12, recall the care rules, the study questions, and the
  ordinary people who resisted and sheltered. Today we ask: what comes after, and
  what does justice require?

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **after atrocity,
communities rebuild through four interlocking paths — truth, accountability, repair,
and remembrance — and justice is not a single verdict but the holding of these
together, each with its own reach and its own limits.** Model the four paths with a
worked example before learners weigh them (S-011).

**Emotional safety (non-negotiable):** (1) **Warning and opt-out:** re-state that
learners may step out, pass, or write privately at any time. (2) **Referral:** name
the trusted adult or support line. (3) **Grounding:** open and close with a slow
breath. (4) **No graphic images or gratuitous detail:** discuss the *paths*, not the
violence. (5) **Agency and hope:** this lesson is the turn toward repair — end by
centering what communities *built*, not only what they suffered (S-495).

The **ethics** lens is the spine: justice is a moral question — what is owed to the
harmed and to the truth. The **egalitarian** lens: justice exists because every life
counts equally, and repair seeks to restore dignity, not merely to punish. The
**global** lens: truth commissions, courts, and reparations have been attempted in
many places, and repair is often understood not as revenge but as restoring
relationship — a principle shared by restorative-justice traditions (S-022) and
echoed in Indigenous peacemaking and other world traditions. The
**critical-thinking** lens: weigh what each path *can* and *cannot* do — truth
without accountability can ring hollow, accountability without repair can feel
empty, and remembrance without the others can fade into ceremony. Preview: Lesson 14
synthesizes the whole unit into one many-voiced story.

## Procedure

1. **Ground (3 min).** Breathe once, slowly. Recall you may pause or step away.
2. **Meet the chart (20 min).** Look at the four paths.
   - **Truth:** naming what happened honestly — testimony, records, truth
     commissions.
   - **Accountability:** holding those responsible to answer — courts of law, legal
     process (S-491).
   - **Repair:** restoring what can be restored — reparations, rebuilding, care for
     survivors and communities (S-022).
   - **Remembrance:** keeping memory alive — memorials, days of remembrance,
     teaching (S-490).
   Watch one worked example: a community that told the truth, sought accountability,
   repaired what it could, and built a memorial — and notice how the four paths
   support one another.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, ask: *what can truth do that a court
   cannot?* and *what can repair do that remembrance cannot?* Share one answer each.
4. **Independent work (10 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph: what do you
   think justice requires after atrocity, and which of the four paths matters most
   to you, and why?
5. **Close and ground (5 min).** Breathe once more. Next time: the whole unit, woven
   into one many-voiced story.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on two paths (truth and remembrance) and one question each.
- **Extension:** Compare a truth commission and a court of law as responses to the
  same atrocity, and argue what each is best suited to do.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Name the four paths aloud — truth,
  accountability, repair, remembrance — meeting in "rebuilding," and offer four
  overlapping loops of yarn meeting at a center knot so the interlock can be felt.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four paths and say what one
  path can do that another cannot?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The "what justice requires" paragraph.

## Home connection

With a trusted adult, talk about what your family or community does to *repair*
after a hurt — and what "making it right" means to you. (No one is required to
discuss the events themselves outside class.)
