# Lesson 9 — Modern Migration and Displacement

## Summary

Learners analyze modern migration and displacement driven by conflict, economy, and
climate, and weigh their causes and human consequences. They distinguish voluntary
from forced movement and learn to see people, never just numbers.

## Objectives

- Analyze modern migration and displacement driven by conflict, economy, and
  climate, and weigh their causes and human consequences. _(D08.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

People have always moved — for family, for work, for safety, for the land's health
(S-086). What changes is *why* and *how freely*. A person who chooses a new city for
work moves differently from a person who must flee a war, a failed harvest, or a
rising sea. The same word — "migration" — covers both, but the human weight is not
the same. Reading the difference is how we see people, not statistics.

## Materials

- "Why people move" chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-driver chart.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the difference between voluntary and forced
  migration, and that environmental change (drought, flood, sea-level rise) has
  driven people to move, past and present (S-383, S-005).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **modern migration
is driven by three forces — conflict, economy, and climate — and the difference
between moving by choice and being forced to flee changes everything about the human
weight of the story.** Model the three drivers with worked examples before
independent analysis (S-011).

**Emotional safety:** this lesson touches real hardship (war, displacement, climate
loss) but without graphic detail. Give a brief warning, allow stepping out, name a
trusted adult, and ground afterward. Keep the human consequences concrete and
dignified — a family, a place, a choice or a forced flight — never only counts.

The **global** lens is the spine: displacement is worldwide — UNHCR documents tens of
millions of forcibly displaced people (refugees, asylum-seekers, and internally
displaced people), driven primarily by conflict and persecution, with climate a
growing driver (S-493, S-383, S-005); IOM documents that migration is longstanding
human behavior with economic, family, safety, and environmental drivers (S-086). The
**egalitarian** and **ethics** lenses: who can move safely and legally, and who is
turned away or trapped, is a question of power and justice — hold it as a question.
The **technology** lens: climate-driven displacement is partly a consequence of
technologies and energy systems that warmed the planet, which places the question of
responsibility across borders. Preview: Lesson 10 begins the unit's most careful
section — studying atrocity and remembrance.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between voluntary and
   forced migration? Name one environmental driver of movement.
2. **Meet the chart (15 min).** Look at the three-driver chart.
   - **Conflict:** war and persecution force people to flee as refugees,
     asylum-seekers, or internally displaced people — the largest driver of forced
     displacement today (S-493).
   - **Economy:** people move for work and opportunity, often by choice, but poverty
     can make "choice" narrow (S-086).
   - **Climate:** drought, flood, and sea-level rise are growing drivers, within and
     across borders (S-383, S-005).
   Watch one worked example: one family forced by conflict and one person choosing a
   job abroad — name the driver and the different human weight of each.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take one driver. Give one human-scale
   example and name whether the movement is voluntary or forced. Compare and agree.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph weighing
   causes and human consequences for one driver, naming at least one person-scale
   detail.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: why does "voluntary or forced" matter to
   how we see migration? Next time: studying atrocity with care.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one driver (conflict) with a guided example and two questions.
- **Extension:** Compare two drivers (e.g., climate vs. conflict) and argue which
  shapes more lives today, citing a source.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Name the three drivers aloud — conflict, economy,
  climate — and show push versus pull with two sticks or fingers (pull toward the
  new place, push away from home); the human-scale story carries the lesson by
  voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the three drivers, give a
  human-scale example for one, and say whether it is voluntary or forced?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-paragraph causes-and-consequences analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *has anyone in our family or community moved for work, safety, or the
weather?* Together, name the driver and whether the move was chosen or forced.

## Resources

- On why people move: International Organization for Migration, "Who is a migrant?"
  (S-086).
- On forced displacement today: UNHCR, *Global Trends* (S-493).
- On climate as a driver: IOM, "Climate Action" (S-383), and the IPCC (S-005).
