# Lesson 6 — Global Trade and Technology Transform Traditions

## Summary

Learners analyze how global trade and technology spread and transform cultural
traditions in the modern world. Using foods, music, language, and dress, they
distinguish *fusion* (new, credited blends) from *homogenization* (thinning into
sameness), and see that the home tradition keeps living through both.

## Objectives

- Analyze how global trade and technology have spread and transformed cultural
  traditions in the modern world. _(D08.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

Think of a dish your family loves. Its ingredients may have crossed oceans: the
potato began in the Andes, the tomato in the Americas, spices along Indian Ocean and
Silk Road routes (S-054, S-323). And think of a song you stream: it may have left its
home, met other sounds, and come back changed. Trade and technology do not just move
things — they *change* the traditions they carry, in two different directions at
once.

## Materials

- "Tradition in motion" examples
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or write the four examples.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, recall that knowledge moves through trade and
  translation, and that credit can be lost. Today we see traditions *transform* as
  they move, not just arrive.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **global trade and
media technology spread traditions, and they transform them in two ways at once —
fusion creates new, credited blends, while homogenization thins distinct traditions
toward sameness; the home tradition also keeps living and adapting.** Model the two
outcomes with one worked example before learners analyze their own (S-011).

The **global** lens is the spine: Haitian Creole is a full language born of contact
between enslaved Africans and French colonists, now spoken by millions — not a broken
version of anything (S-475); a food like the potato moved from the Andes into
countless cuisines, becoming "native" to new tables (S-054); music, dance, and craft
travel through recordings and screens, protected as living heritage by UNESCO's
intangible-heritage framework (S-056). The **technology** lens: the printing press,
radio, recording, and streaming each changed *how* a tradition spreads — faster and
further, but also more filtered through whoever controls the channel. The **ethics**
and **egalitarian** lenses: fusion with credit is exchange; fusion without credit is
taking (recall Lesson 4); and homogenization often favors the powerful. Preview:
Lesson 7 turns to how colonization reshaped populations, environments, and borders.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: name one idea that traveled and whose maker we
   must remember to credit. Now: name one food or song in your life that came from
   far away.
2. **Meet the examples (15 min).** Look at the four "tradition in motion" examples.
   For each, mark what *fused* (a new, credited blend) and what *thinned* (sameness):
   - a food whose ingredients crossed oceans and became a new dish;
   - a music that left home, met other sounds, and returned changed;
   - a language born of contact between peoples (like Haitian Creole, S-475);
   - a garment or craft that traveled through trade and media.
   Watch one worked example: trace a song from its home form, through a recording,
   to both a credited remix (fusion) and a stripped-down version built for a global
   market (homogenization) — and notice the home form is still sung.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take one example each. Name one fusion
   and one homogenization, and say who is credited in the fusion. Compare and agree.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** In your journal, choose a tradition you know. Write
   one paragraph on how trade or media has transformed it — one fusion, one
   thinning, and one thing that stayed.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say in one sentence: what two directions can a tradition change
   as it travels? Next time: how colonization reshaped the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the food example with two guided questions (what fused, what
  thinned).
- **Extension:** Compare two media (print vs. streaming) and argue their different
  effects on one tradition, with a source.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the branch aloud — one home tradition
  forks into fusion (a credited blend) and homogenization (a thinned copy) while the
  home form keeps living — and model the fork with three strands of yarn held apart.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name one fusion and one
  homogenization in a traveling tradition, and say who is credited in the fusion?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-paragraph transformation analysis, added to
  the unit's "knowledge and culture" section.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *which food, song, or saying in our family came from another people —
and what changed when it arrived here?* Write one fusion and one thing that stayed.
