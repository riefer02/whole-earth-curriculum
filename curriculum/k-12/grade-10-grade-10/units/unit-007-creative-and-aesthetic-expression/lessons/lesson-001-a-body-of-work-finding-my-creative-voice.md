# Lesson 1 — A Body of Work: Finding My Creative Voice

## Summary

Learners start the unit's **visual portfolio** by choosing one recurring idea or
motif and drawing it several ways, discovering that a **personal style** grows out of
a *body of work* — many versions of a recurring concern — rather than from any single
piece. This lesson opens the whole unit: every strand that follows (music, movement,
design) builds the same habit of making many things around one idea.

## Objectives

- Develop a personal style by making a body of work, and begin explaining how the
  work reflects ideas and influences. _(D07.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

A cook does not invent a dish once and call it their style; their style is the dozen
meals they return to, each a little different, shaped by who taught them and what they
love. A runner's stride, a gardener's rows, a storyteller's favorite phrases — all are
recognizable because they *repeat and vary*. Your creative voice works the same way:
it is not hidden inside one perfect drawing waiting to be found; it shows up across
the many things you make, again and again. Today you start the record of yours.

## Materials

- Sketchbook or bound paper journal
- Drawing tools (pencil, pen, one color)
- Portfolio folder or sleeve

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall making a digital or mixed-media artwork and
  reflecting on the choices technology made possible. Today we add the next step —
  making a *series*, not a single piece.
- Prepare one worked example: a single simple motif (a leaf, a hand, a house, a wave)
  drawn three ways, so learners see "same idea, different decisions."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a personal style is
not a flash of genius; it is a pattern that appears when you make many works around a
recurring idea or concern, and it reflects your influences and choices.** Style is
*observable* — in the motifs you return to, the marks you make, the questions you keep
asking — so learners can notice their own style forming rather than waiting to be
"talented" enough to have one. This is the unit's through-line: every strand asks
learners to make a *body* of work, not a one-off.

The **ethics** lens appears as the honesty of the artist's record — keeping every
version, not hiding the "failed" ones, because the arc is the point. The
**egalitarian** lens: there is no gate to having a voice; style belongs to everyone,
not to those with expensive materials (hence the low-tech path). The **global** lens:
across cultures, artists have always worked in series — repeated motifs in Akan
adinkra and kente cloth (S-084), Māori kōwhaiwhai rafter patterns (S-115), and Jacob
Lawrence's 60-panel *Migration Series* (S-363) all show how a body of work carries a
vision. The **technology** lens: digital tools let learners keep every variation and
watch their style emerge side by side — but paper does too. The **environment** lens:
a sketchbook made of reused paper is itself a small act of care; art materials carry a
footprint worth noticing. Preview: Lesson 2 turns to the ethics of engaging with
*other* people's visual traditions.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back: what did you make in Grade 9, and what did you
   notice about your own choices? Today you stop making one-off pieces and start a
   *body of work*.
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** A *body of work* is many pieces around a recurring
   idea. Watch a worked example: one leaf drawn three ways — large and rough, small
   and careful, colored and not. The leaf is the *motif*; the differences are
   *variation*. Notice what stays and what changes.
3. **Choose your motif (5 min).** Pick one thing you are drawn to — a shape, an
   object, a place, a person, a feeling, a question. Write it at the top of a
   sketchbook page: "I keep returning to ______."
4. **Make variations (20 min).** Draw your motif at least three times, each version
   making a *different decision* — bigger or smaller, light or dark, one color or
   many, close-up or far away, calm or restless. Keep every version; none are
   mistakes.
5. **Notice your style (10 min).** Look at your three versions. Write one sentence
   naming what *stayed the same* across them and one sentence naming what *changed*.
   That recurring sameness is the first glimpse of your style.
6. **Close (5 min).** Add the page to your portfolio. Remember: one drawing is an
   attempt; many drawings around one idea are a *voice*. You will return to this
   motif all unit long.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the motif already chosen (a hand, a house) and ask only for
  two variations of size or darkness.
- **Extension:** Look up Jacob Lawrence's *Migration Series* (S-363) or an artist you
  admire, and name the recurring motif that runs through their body of work.
- **Non-visual path (blind or low vision):** Make the motif and its variations from
  found objects — stones, leaves, string, cloth, seed pods — arranged by touch so the
  "same idea, different decisions" pattern is felt, then describe each version in one
  spoken sentence. A partner can record the descriptions in words; the body of work is
  the spoken-and-arranged record, not the drawing.
- **Motor path:** Build the motif by placing and moving objects (no fine drawing
  required), or direct a partner who draws while you name each change.
- **Neurodivergent support:** Offer the motif choice as a short visual or spoken menu
  rather than an open prompt, keep the routine predictable (recall → example →
  choose → make → notice → close), and let learners work with a repeated, calming
  mark or movement if novelty is overloading.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner make three variations of one motif and name,
  in a sentence, one thing that stayed the same and one that changed?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The first sketchbook page (motif + three variations
  + a reflection sentence), added as the opening entry of the visual body of work.

## Home connection

Ask a grown-up at home what they make, cook, or grow again and again, and what makes
their way of doing it recognizable. Tell them the motif you chose and why.
