# Lesson 9 — Honoring Living Practitioners: Movement and Drama

## Summary

Learners sort movement-and-drama scenarios to distinguish **respectful engagement**
with a dance or dramatic tradition from **appropriation**, learning to seek
invitation, learn the context, respect what is sacred, and credit living
practitioners. Capoeira, haka, and tinikling show that movement forms are held by
living communities — so borrowing them carries an ethical responsibility.

## Objectives

- Distinguish respectful engagement with a dance or dramatic tradition from
  appropriation, and explain how to honor living practitioners. _(D07.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

If a guest at your home watched one dance you do at a family occasion, then performed
a half-remembered, joking version of it on a public stage for money, you would feel
something was taken. It is not that the dance cannot be shared — it is that *how* it
is taken matters: were they invited, did they learn what it means, did they name you?
Today you learn to be the guest who asks.

## Materials

- Engagement-or-appropriation scenario cards
- Two-column reflection sheet

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lessons 2 and 6, recall the four questions — ask, context, credit,
  benefit. Today you run them on *movement and drama*, with one addition: is the form
  sacred or ceremonial, and does that close it to outsiders?
- Prepare the four scenarios (see Facilitator note) and the reflection sheet.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the same four
questions govern movement and drama, with one more layer — some forms are sacred,
ceremonial, or owned by a specific community, and for those, respect may mean *not*
performing them at all.** Capoeira is an Afro-Brazilian art of movement and song that
developed among enslaved Africans in Brazil and carries strength and identity (S-325).
Haka is a family of Māori posture dances — not one single "war dance" — each with its
own words, meaning, and iwi (tribe), performed on real occasions (S-447). Tinikling is
a Philippine folk dance in which dancers step between bamboo poles imitating the
tikling bird (S-116). Each is *alive* — owned, taught, and performed by people now.

Keep it concrete and never flatten a tradition. The distinction is about power and
consent: learning a form from its practitioners, on their invitation, honoring its
context, and crediting them is engagement; performing a sacred or ceremonial form
without invitation or understanding, mocking or simplifying it, or profiting while the
community earns nothing is appropriation. UNESCO's living-heritage frame (S-056) and
the Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (S-389) support treating these forms
as held by communities, not free material.

The **ethics** lens is the spine. The **egalitarian** lens: the question "who is
allowed, who is invited, who profits" is a question of power — respect corrects an
imbalance. The **global** lens: forms are *people's*, alive now, not costumes from a
"world culture" bin. The **technology** lens: video made any dance one click from any
stage, so permission and credit are *more* urgent, not less. The **environment**
lens: a form often grows from its place — the bamboo poles of tinikling, the roda
(circle) of capoeira — and stripping the place strips the meaning. Preview: Lesson 10
turns to making — designing for a community need.

**Four scenarios** (adapt to your place; keep specific and respectful): 1. A dancer
learns a form from its own teacher, by invitation, learns its meaning, and credits the
community in the program. 2. A performer copies a ceremonial dance from a video,
performs it as entertainment, and never asks or credits anyone. 3. A theater group
invites a practitioner to teach and co-direct, and shares the ticket money. 4. A film
uses a people's sacred gesture as a joke, flattening a living tradition into a punch
line.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 2 and 6: four questions — **ask, context, credit,
   benefit**. Today add a fifth for the body: **is it sacred, and is it mine to do?**
2. **Meet the distinction (10 min).** Watch a worked example: a dancer who learns from
   a teacher by invitation, learns the meaning, and credits the community — versus a
   performer who copies a ceremonial dance from a video for laughs and profit. One
   *honors*; one *takes*.
3. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, read the four cards. Place each
   under "Honors living practitioners" or "Takes from them," and write one reason
   using the five questions.
4. **Meet three living forms (10 min).** Consider capoeira (S-325), haka (S-447), and
   tinikling (S-116). Why does each one's *context* — its history, its occasion, its
   community — matter so much to how we engage it?
5. **Write your rule (10 min).** Write your own two-sentence rule for engaging with
   another culture's movement or drama. Start: "I will honor living practitioners by
   ______."
6. **Close (5 min).** Add the sheet to your portfolio. Next: design.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give the five questions as a checklist and ask only "invited or not"
  and "credited or not" for each scenario.
- **Extension:** Research one real case of a dance or drama form taken out of context,
  and write a short proposal for a fairer path (invitation, credit, shared benefit).
- **Non-visual / low-literacy path:** The four scenarios and the three living forms
  (capoeira, haka, tinikling) can be read as text rather than viewed on video, and
  learners answer aloud or in voice-recorded form instead of writing.
- **Language:** Learners may write or speak their rule in their home language first,
  then translate to a shared classroom language; the five questions (ask, context,
  credit, benefit, is-it-sacred) translate cleanly across languages.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner sort each scenario and give one reason
  (invitation, context, sacredness, credit, or benefit) for at least two of them?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column reflection with the learner's own
  two-sentence rule, added to the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a dance, gesture, or ceremony your family or community does,
and whether an outsider would need permission to join or perform it.
