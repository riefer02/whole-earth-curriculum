# Lesson 2 — Appreciation, Not Appropriation: Honoring Living Visual Traditions

## Summary

Learners sort four scenarios to tell **cultural appreciation** from **appropriation**
in visual art. They learn that honoring a living tradition means *asking*, *learning
the context*, *crediting the makers*, and *sharing benefit* — while appropriation
takes a people's meaningful or sacred forms as decoration, costume, or profit, with
no permission, credit, or return. The arts of other cultures are met as living
traditions to honor with respectful questions, never as specimens.

## Objectives

- Distinguish cultural appreciation from appropriation in art, citing examples, and
  explain how to honor living traditions respectfully. _(D07.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Imagine someone copied the family recipe your grandmother taught only you, sold it as
their own, and never mentioned her. You would not object to their *liking* the food;
you would object to their taking the thing that carried your family's story, stripping
it of where it came from, and profiting alone. That feeling is the seed of the whole
distinction today. Loving another culture's art is not the problem — *how* you take
it, credit it, and share it is.

- Appreciation-or-appropriation card set
- Two-column reflection sheet
- Portfolio folder

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall your motif and what you noticed about your own
  style. Today we look outward — at how to engage with *other* people's visual
  traditions.
- Prepare the four scenarios (see Facilitator note) and the two-column sheet.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **appreciation and
appropriation differ in permission, context, credit, and benefit — not in whether you
admire something.** Appreciation means learning *from* the living people who make the
art — asking when something is owned or sacred, understanding what it means, naming
the makers, and sharing benefit (paying, supporting, or amplifying them).
Appropriation takes *without* those things: copying a form while stripping its
meaning, wearing sacred or ceremonial designs as fashion, or profiting while the
community who created it earns nothing. Because the difference is about power and
consent, it is not a neat two-column rule but a set of respectful questions.

Keep this concrete and specific, never a cartoon of any culture. Ground examples in
real, living traditions and their documented meanings: Akan kente and adinkra cloth of
Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, whose named patterns and colors encode proverbs and values
and are worn at important occasions (S-084); Māori kōwhaiwhai painted rafter patterns,
whakairo carving, and the koru (unfurling fern) spiral, tied to whakapapa — belonging
through ancestry (S-115); and Mexico's Day of the Dead, an Indigenous festivity that
commemorates the return of deceased loved ones and fuses pre-Hispanic rites with
Catholic feasts — not a costume theme (S-448). The UNESCO Universal Declaration on
Cultural Diversity (S-389) and the living-heritage frame (S-056) support treating arts
as practices communities own and pass on, not free material.

The **ethics** lens is the spine: what is right when we borrow? The **egalitarian**
lens: appropriation usually flows *uphill* — from the less powerful to the more
powerful — so credit and shared benefit matter. The **global** lens: traditions are
*people's*, not "the world's"; they live in communities who still make them. The
**technology** lens: copying and remixing are one click away now, which makes
permission and credit *more*, not less, important. The **environment** lens: a
tradition often carries knowledge of materials and place (plant dyes, local fibers)
that mass copying erases. Preview: Lesson 3 asks learners to write the artist's
explanation that names influences honestly — the skill they are building here.

**Four scenarios to use** (adapt to your place; keep them specific and respectful):
1. A designer visits a weaving community, learns the patterns' meanings from the
   weavers, pays them, and credits them on the label. 2. A brand prints a sacred
   ceremonial design on T-shirts without asking and sells them without credit or
   payment. 3. A student paints a motif inspired by another culture, names the culture
   and the artist they learned from, and says "I am a beginner borrowing with
   permission." 4. A shop sells "tribal-style" stickers made by no one from that
   culture, flattening many distinct peoples into one stereotype.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: your motif is *yours*. Now ask — what is *not*
   mine, and how do I honor it?
2. **Meet the distinction (10 min).** Watch a worked example: a designer who visits a
   weaving community, learns the meanings, pays, and credits them — versus a brand
   that prints the same pattern on T-shirts with no permission or payment. One
   *honors*; one *takes*. Notice the four questions: did they **ask**? did they learn
   the **context**? did they **credit**? did they **share benefit**?
3. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, read the four cards. Place each
   under "Honors the living tradition" or "Takes from it," and write one reason using
   the four questions.
4. **Name the pattern (10 min).** What do the "takes from it" cases have in common?
   (No permission, no context, no credit, no shared benefit — often taking sacred or
   ceremonial forms as decoration or profit.) What do the "honors" cases share?
5. **Write your rule (10 min).** On your reflection sheet, write your own two-sentence
   rule for engaging with another culture's visual art respectfully. Start: "I will
   honor a living tradition by ______."
6. **Close (5 min).** Add the sheet to your portfolio. Remember: admiration is not the
   problem; *how* you take, credit, and share is. Next time, you write the artist's
   explanation for your own body of work.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give the four questions as a checklist to run on each scenario, and ask
  only "asked permission — yes or no?"
- **Extension:** Research one real instance of a tradition copied without credit and
  write a short proposal for a fairer path (permission, credit, shared profit).
- **Non-visual / low-literacy path:** Read each scenario aloud (or play it from the
  facilitator's voice) and have learners answer aloud — "honors" or "takes," plus one
  reason spoken — instead of reading and writing. The reflection can be recorded in
  voice or transcribed by a partner rather than written.
- **Language:** Learners may write or speak their two-sentence rule in their home
  language first; a translation to a shared classroom language can follow. The four
  questions (ask, context, credit, benefit) translate readily and can be posted in
  multiple languages.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner sort each scenario correctly and give one
  reason (permission, context, credit, or benefit) for at least two of them?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column reflection sheet with the learner's
  own two-sentence rule, added to the portfolio.

## Home connection

With someone at home, name one thing your family or community makes that carries
meaning. Ask: what would need to happen for an outsider to use it *respectfully*?
