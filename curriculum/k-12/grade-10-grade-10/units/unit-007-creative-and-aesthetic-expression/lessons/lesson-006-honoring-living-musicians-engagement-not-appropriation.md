# Lesson 6 — Honoring Living Musicians: Engagement, Not Appropriation

## Summary

Learners sort music scenarios to tell **respectful engagement** with a musical
tradition from **appropriation**, learning to ask, learn the context, credit, and
share benefit — and to honor the *living* musicians and communities who made the
music. Reggae, spirituals, and the griot tradition show how music carries a
community's history, so using it carries an ethical responsibility.

## Objectives

- Distinguish respectful engagement with a musical tradition from appropriation, and
  explain how to honor living musicians and communities. _(D07.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

You would not want someone to take a song *you* wrote, perform it for money, and never
say your name. That instinct — credit the maker, share the benefit — is the whole
lesson. Music is never just sound; it is made by people in places, carrying their
history. Engaging with it respectfully means treating those people as *the point*, not
as a free library of sounds.

## Materials

- Engagement-or-appropriation scenario cards
- Two-column reflection sheet

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall your rule for honoring living *visual* traditions.
  Today you apply the same four questions — ask, context, credit, benefit — to music.
- Prepare the four scenarios (see Facilitator note) and the reflection sheet.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the same four
questions from visual art govern music — did they ask? learn the context? credit? share
benefit? — because music, too, is a living tradition made by living people.** Reggae
originated among marginalized groups in western Kingston, Jamaica, and became "a voice
for all" on injustice, resistance, love, and humanity (S-450; S-445). African American
spirituals are religious folk songs born of enslavement that carry a people's history
(S-385). West African griots are hereditary oral historians and musicians who *keep* a
community's memory — their music is not decoration but archive (S-446). Because music
travels and is sampled in one click, credit and permission are *more* urgent, not
less.

Keep it concrete and never flatten a tradition into a stereotype. The ethical
distinction is about power and consent: learning a tradition from its practitioners,
crediting composers and performers, paying royalties where due, and amplifying the
community's own voices is engagement; sampling or performing without permission or
credit, claiming others' forms as your own "discovery," or profiting while the makers
earn nothing is appropriation. UNESCO's living-heritage frame (S-056) and the
Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity (S-389) support treating music as owned
and passed on by communities.

The **ethics** lens is the spine. The **egalitarian** lens: musical appropriation
almost always flows from the less powerful to the more powerful — credit and shared
benefit are how the balance is corrected. The **global** lens: traditions are
*people's*, alive now, not "world music" free for the taking. The **technology** lens:
sampling and streaming made reuse effortless, so the *ethical* work (permission,
credit, royalty) is the part a machine cannot do. The **environment** lens: honoring a
tradition includes honoring the places and materials it grew from, not extracting the
sound and discarding the rest. Preview: Lesson 7 turns to the body — devising a
movement piece.

**Four scenarios** (adapt to your place; keep specific and respectful): 1. A musician
studies with a community's players, learns the meaning of the form, credits them, and
shares the earnings. 2. A producer samples a traditional recording without permission,
lists no composer, and keeps the profit. 3. A choir learns a spiritual from recordings
and from a living singer of that tradition, and names the tradition in the program.
4. A brand uses a people's ceremonial song in an advertisement without asking or
paying, turning a sacred sound into a jingle.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: four questions — **ask, context, credit,
   benefit**. Today you run them on *music*.
2. **Meet the distinction (10 min).** Watch a worked example: a musician who studies
   with a community, credits them, and shares earnings — versus a producer who samples
   without permission, lists no composer, and keeps the profit. One *honors*; one
   *takes*.
3. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, read the four cards. Place each
   under "Honors living musicians" or "Takes from them," and write one reason using
   the four questions.
4. **Hear the history (10 min).** Consider three real traditions: reggae as the voice
   of Kingston's marginalized communities (S-450), spirituals born of enslavement
   (S-385), and griots as keepers of memory (S-446). Why does *context* matter so much
   for music like this?
5. **Write your rule (10 min).** Write your own two-sentence rule for engaging with
   another culture's music. Start: "I will honor living musicians by ______."
6. **Close (5 min).** Add the sheet to your portfolio. Next: the moving body.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give the four questions as a checklist and ask only "credited — yes or
  no?" for each scenario.
- **Extension:** Look up who wrote and who owns a familiar song, and trace where the
  money from one stream goes — then propose one fairer arrangement.
- **Non-hearing / low-literacy path:** The four scenarios and the histories of reggae,
  spirituals, and griots can be read as text rather than listened to; the "hear the
  history" step becomes "read the history" with no loss. Learners answer aloud or in
  writing, and the reflection may be recorded in voice.
- **Language:** Learners may write or speak their rule in their home language first,
  then translate to a shared classroom language; the four questions carry cleanly
  across languages.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner sort each scenario and give one reason
  (permission, context, credit, or benefit) for at least two of them?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column reflection with the learner's own
  two-sentence rule, added to the portfolio.

## Home connection

Pick a song you love. Find out who wrote it, who performs it, and where it comes from.
Tell someone at home one thing you learned about its makers.
