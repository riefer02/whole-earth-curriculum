# Lesson 4 — Composing an Idea: Sound That Says Something

## Summary

Learners **compose** an original short piece that expresses a clear idea or feeling,
choosing melody, rhythm, dynamics, and texture deliberately and notating the result on
a **graphic score** so another person could follow it. This is the first half of the
music objective — the second (recording craft) comes in Lesson 5.

## Objectives

- Compose and record an original piece that expresses a clear idea or feeling, and
  explain the techniques used. _(D07.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

You already compose every time you hum a tune that is yours, tap a rhythm to match how
you feel, or make up a song for a child. The difference between that and *composing*
is only intention: deciding on an idea or feeling, then choosing each sound because it
serves that idea. Today you make the intention explicit — and write the piece down so
someone else could play it.

## Materials

- Graphic-score sheet
- A sound source (voice, clap, instrument, or found object)

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–3, recall how a *body of work* grows around one idea.
  Music works the same way — one idea, developed.
- Prepare one worked example: a four-bar phrase expressing "calm" (slow, soft, a
  falling melody) marked on a graphic score.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **composing is
choosing sound on purpose — a clear idea or feeling, expressed through deliberate
choices of melody, rhythm, dynamics, and texture, and notated so the choice is
visible.** A graphic score (any drawn map of sound over time) is a universal, low-tech
notation that needs no staff-reading, so every learner can compose and *document*,
not just perform. The unit's through-line holds: a motif (a short musical idea)
developed across the piece is the sonic twin of the visual motif from Lesson 1.

The **ethics** lens: composing your own idea (rather than only copying) is the honest
starting point — originality here means your *choice*, made visible. The
**egalitarian** lens: the voice and body are instruments; no purchased instrument or
training is required to be a composer. The **global** lens: every musical tradition
has its own ways of organizing sound and of writing it down — the graphic score is one
such system, alongside staff notation, Indian raga frameworks (S-366), and the oral
transmission of West African griots (S-446); none is "the" one true system. The
**technology** lens: recording and notation tools let a composer hear and revise
instantly — but a pencil works too. The **environment** lens: found-object
instruments remind us that sound comes from the world around us, with no footprint at
all.

Model one worked example (worked example → guided practice → independent composition;
S-011). Keep it short — a piece can be eight seconds and still express a clear idea.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: you built a visual *body of work* around one
   motif. Today you build a *piece* around one musical idea.
2. **Meet the elements (10 min).** Watch a worked example: a four-bar phrase that
   says "calm" — slow, soft, with a melody that falls. See how it is marked on a
   graphic score: a time line, a rising/falling line for melody, dots for rhythm, a
   swelling shape for loud and soft.
3. **Choose your idea (5 min).** Pick one idea or feeling — sunrise, a long wait, a
   chase, arriving home. Write it at the top of your score: "This piece is about
   ______."
4. **Build the piece (15 min).** Make a short musical idea (a motif) and repeat or
   change it. Decide: is the melody high or low? The rhythm fast or slow, even or
   uneven? The dynamics loud or soft? The texture one voice or many? Mark each choice
   on the score.
5. **Notate it (10 min).** Draw your piece on the graphic score so clearly that a
   friend could follow the shape without hearing you first.
6. **Close (5 min).** Play or speak your score once for a partner. Keep the score in
   your portfolio. Next time, you record it and explain your techniques.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compose a rhythm-only piece with a single clear feeling, and mark only
  fast/slow and loud/soft on the score.
- **Extension:** Add a contrasting B section and explain, in a sentence, how the
  contrast serves the idea.
- **Non-hearing path (deaf or hard of hearing):** Compose through what is felt and
  seen rather than heard — feel the beat through a drum skin, a balloon held against
  the chest, or the floor under bare feet, and use the graphic score (a visual map)
  plus hand signals for loud/soft and fast/slow. The score itself is the document of
  the piece; a hearing partner can perform it while the composer watches and adjusts.
- **Neurodivergent support:** The graphic score gives a predictable, concrete frame for
  a task that can otherwise feel open-ended; allow repetition and a fixed structure
  (one idea, then variations), and let learners choose a single element (rhythm or
  dynamics) to focus on at a time.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state the idea in one sentence, make a short
  piece, and point to at least two deliberate choices (melody, rhythm, dynamics, or
  texture) on their score?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The graphic score with the idea statement, added to
  the portfolio as the music-composition draft.

## Home connection

Listen for one piece of music at home (a song, a ringtone, a lullaby) and name the
single feeling it is built to carry — and one choice (speed, loudness, shape) that
creates it.
