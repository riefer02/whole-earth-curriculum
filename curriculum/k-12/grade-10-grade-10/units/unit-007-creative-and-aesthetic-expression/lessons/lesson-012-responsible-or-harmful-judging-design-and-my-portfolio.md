# Lesson 12 — Responsible or Harmful? Judging Design — and My Portfolio

## Summary

Learners judge designs against four questions — who it helps, who it harms, what it
costs the Earth, and whether it could be done better — distinguishing **responsible**
from **harmful** design, then review their full creative portfolio and write a closing
reflection. This closes the making-and-design strand and the whole unit.

## Objectives

- Distinguish responsible and sustainable design from design that harms people or the
  environment, citing examples. _(D07.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

A thing can look beautiful and work perfectly *for you* and still be a bad design if
it poisons a river downstream or is made by someone who is not paid. Judging design is
not a matter of taste — it is a matter of *consequences*, which you can look up,
measure, and name. Today you become the judge, then step back and look at everything
you made this unit.

## Materials

- Design-judgment sheet
- Your full unit portfolio

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, recall the life cycle and your sustainability-and-fairness
  rationale. Today you turn that into a general *judgment* — and then review the whole
  unit.
- Prepare one worked example of judging a design with evidence (e.g., a single-use
  item versus a repairable one).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **responsible design
is judged by consequences, not appearance — four questions: who does it help, who does
it harm, what does it cost the Earth, and could it be done better.** The same evidence
base from Lesson 11 applies: e-waste toxicity (S-397), cobalt supply-chain abuses
(S-398), the circular-economy alternative (S-452), universal design (S-369), and the
Brundtland definition of sustainability (S-395). A judgment that names *evidence* is
the difference between an opinion and a defended conclusion — the unit's capstone in
critical thinking.

Then the lesson turns inward: the portfolio is the unit's real product, and the
closing reflection lets each learner see their own arc across all four strands — a
body of visual work and an artist's explanation, a composed-and-recorded piece, a
devised-and-performed piece, and a documented design — each one carrying the
appreciation-not-appropriation and responsible-not-harmful distinctions they practiced.

The **ethics** lens: judging design is an ethical act — naming harm is the first step
to preventing it. The **egalitarian** lens: always ask who is helped and who is left
out; that question is the fairness test. The **global** lens: consequences cross
borders — the harm and the benefit are distributed unevenly across the world. The
**technology** lens: technology can serve or harm, and the designer (and the
judge) decides which. The **environment** lens is the spine: the Earth's cost is real
and measurable, and it is always on the sheet. The **community** lens: the portfolio
sharing is a community act — each learner's work seen and honored by peers who want
them to succeed. This is a summative close, kept low-stakes and celebratory (mastery,
not ranking).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** You have designed for a need and asked what it costs. Today you
   judge design in general — and then look back at everything you made.
2. **Meet the four questions (10 min).** Watch a worked example: a single-use item
   judged against a repairable one. The four questions: **who does it help? who does
   it harm? what does it cost the Earth? could it be done better?** Notice that every
   judgment names *evidence*.
3. **Judge two designs (15 min).** On your sheet, judge your own design from Lessons
   10–11 and one other real design you have seen — an object, a building, a product.
   Answer all four questions with at least one concrete fact or observation each.
4. **Write the verdict (5 min).** For each, write one sentence: responsible, harmful,
   or in between — and the single strongest piece of evidence.
5. **Review your portfolio (15 min).** Lay out your full portfolio: visual body of
   work and statement, composed-and-recorded piece, devised-and-performed piece, and
   documented design. Notice the one idea or concern that runs through all four.
6. **Write the closing reflection (5 min).** Finish this sentence and keep it in your
   portfolio: "Across this unit, I kept returning to ______, and I learned to honor
   other people's work by ______."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Answer only "who does it help" and "who does it harm" for one design,
  and write a one-sentence portfolio reflection.
- **Extension:** Evaluate the full life cycle of a designed object and propose three
  concrete improvements, with evidence for each.
- **Non-writing / non-visual path:** The judgment and the closing reflection can be
  spoken and recorded in voice or transcribed by a partner; the four questions are the
  same whether answered aloud or in writing, and the portfolio can be described and
  presented orally.
- **Language:** The closing reflection may be written or spoken in the learner's home
  language first, then translated to a shared language for the portfolio sharing;
  naming one's own through-line and rule for honoring others is the point, in whichever
  language carries it most truly.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio + self):** The learner judges at least one design against all
  four questions with a concrete fact or observation for each, states a
  responsible/harmful verdict with evidence, and writes a closing reflection naming
  their through-line and their rule for honoring others' work.
- **Self-assessment:** The learner reviews the mastery checklist for all eight unit
  objectives, marking each "on my own" or "can teach someone else," and notes anything
  still "not yet."

## Home connection

Show someone at home your full portfolio — the art, the music, the movement, the
design — and tell them the one idea that runs through all of it.
