# Lesson 3 — The Artist's Explanation: Style, Influences, and Honest Credit

## Summary

Learners assemble their visual **body of work** and write an **artist's explanation**
that names what they keep returning to, how the work changed, who influenced them —
with honest credit — and how any living tradition they engaged was honored. This
completes the unit's visual-arts strand: a personal style, explained and credited.

## Objectives

- Develop a personal style by making a body of work and explain how it reflects their
  ideas and influences. _(D07.S1.10.01)_
- Distinguish cultural appreciation from appropriation in art, citing examples, and
  explain how to honor living traditions respectfully. _(D07.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Every maker you admire was shaped by other makers — teachers, family, artists they
studied — and the honest ones say so. A carpenter names the person who taught them the
joint; a poet names the poet they imitate while finding their own voice. Naming your
influences is not admitting you lack originality; it is how originality is honestly
made. Today you write that public, honest explanation for your own work.

## Materials

- Artist-statement frame
- Your growing visual portfolio
- Drawing tools

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, recall your motif and your two-sentence rule for
  honoring living traditions. Today you put them into one written explanation.
- Prepare the artist-statement frame and one worked example statement.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a body of work plus
an honest explanation is what turns "I drew some things" into a personal style with a
traceable history.** The artist's explanation has four moves — the recurring idea, the
change across the work, the influences (named and credited), and how any living
tradition was honored. The fourth move is the ethics check from Lesson 2, made
routine: an artist *says* where their forms came from.

The **ethics** lens: honest credit is a habit, not a penalty — it is how a community
of makers stays fair. The **egalitarian** lens: anyone can have a style and an
explanation; the statement needs no expensive materials, only honest looking at your
own work. The **global** lens: real artist statements across cultures name lineage and
place — the makers they learned from — rather than claiming to have invented
everything alone; kente and adinkra carry meaning *because* their makers name what
each pattern means (S-084), and Māori arts tie form to whakapapa, belonging through
ancestry (S-115). The **technology** lens: a digital record lets learners see their
whole series at once, which is exactly what makes the through-line visible. The
**environment** lens: a body of work made from reused materials is a statement of care
too. Preview: Lesson 4 moves to sound — composing an idea in music.

Model the four moves once (worked example → guided practice → independent writing;
S-011). Keep this low-stakes: the statement is a draft the learner can revisit all
unit. Assessment is about whether the four moves are *present and honest*, never about
ranking the art.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Lay out every visual page you have made so far. You now have a
   *body of work* — more than one piece around one idea.
2. **Meet the four moves (10 min).** Watch a worked example artist's statement. It
   does four things: names **what I keep returning to**, describes **what changed**
   across the work, names **who influenced me** with credit, and says **how I honored
   any living tradition** I engaged.
3. **Look at your own arc (10 min).** Study your pages. What stays the same? What
   changed? Who taught you or inspired you — a family member, a friend, an artist you
   studied? Write notes for each move.
4. **Write the statement (15 min).** Using the frame, write four to six sentences: the
   idea, the change, the influences (named), and the honor (if you engaged another
   tradition, how you asked, learned, and credited).
5. **Read it back (10 min).** Read your statement aloud once, alone or to a partner.
   Does it name at least one influence honestly? Does it say how you honored any
   tradition you touched? Revise anything unclear.
6. **Close (5 min).** Add the statement to your portfolio beside your body of work.
   You have now done what every strand in this unit asks: made a series and explained
   it. Next: sound.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each move ("I keep returning to ______
  because ______").
- **Extension:** Add a fifth move — a one-sentence note on what you want the work to
  say to an audience, previewing Grade 11's art-with-a-message.
- **Non-visual / non-writing path:** Speak the four-move statement aloud and record it
  in voice (or have a partner transcribe it); the explanation is judged on the four
  moves being *present and honest*, not on handwriting or spelling.
- **Language:** The statement may be composed in the learner's home language and
  translated to a shared language for the portfolio, keeping the credit of influences
  intact in whichever language names them most accurately.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The learner presents a body of at least three related
  works and a statement that (a) names the recurring idea, (b) names the change, (c)
  credits at least one influence, and (d) explains how any living tradition was
  honored — observable across the four moves of the frame.
- **Self-assessment:** The learner self-places on the mastery checklist for
  _D07.S1.10.01_ and _D07.S1.10.02_ and notes their next step.

## Home connection

Show your body of work and statement to someone at home. Ask: what did you already
know about my style that I only just noticed?
