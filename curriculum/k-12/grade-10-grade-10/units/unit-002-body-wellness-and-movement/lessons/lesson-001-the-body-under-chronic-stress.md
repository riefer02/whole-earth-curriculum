# Lesson 1 — The Body Under Chronic Stress

## Summary

Learners distinguish **acute stress** — a useful alarm that readies the body for a
challenge and then turns off — from **chronic stress**, an alarm that stays on and
slowly wears on **mood, thinking, and long-term health**. They trace the physiology
(sympathetic "alarm" and parasympathetic "calm" branches) and close with a
body-based calm-down: slow breathing and a tense-and-release practice.

## Objectives

- Analyze how chronic stress, sleep, and movement shape mood, thinking, and long-term
  health — beginning with the stress side of the picture. _(D03.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Think of a moment you had to act fast — a sudden noise, a near miss, a big test
announcement. Your heart sped, your muscles tensed, your attention narrowed. That is
your body's **alarm system**, and it is a good thing: it got you ready. The problem is
when the alarm never turns off — when the pressure of bills, exams, caregiving,
housing, or a hard home never lets up. Then the same system that was meant to save you
for a moment begins to wear on you for months. Whether you live in a busy city, a
quiet farm, a coast, or a mountain town, every human body runs this same alarm — and
every body can learn to help it settle.

## Materials

- Stress-response diagram (acute alarm vs. chronic wear-and-tear)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the stress-response diagram.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall the **somatic basis of emotion** — that body state
  shapes mood and thinking (D03.S1.09.01). Today we go one layer deeper: what happens
  when the body's stress state never turns off.
- Preview the closing calm-down practice so you can lead it smoothly.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **stress is a
physiological alarm, and the harm is not in feeling it once but in never turning it
off — the "wear-and-tear" of chronic stress.** The physiology is well documented: an
acute stressor activates the sympathetic branch (fast heart, adrenaline/cortisol,
tensed muscles), and the parasympathetic branch returns the body to calm; when the
response stays on, the costs accumulate as muscle tension, cardiovascular strain,
digestive and sleep disruption, and mood effects (S-338, S-339). Slow, deliberate
breathing — especially a longer exhale — is one evidence-backed way to help the body
return to calm (S-340), and tense-and-release practice has been used for a century to
do the same (S-227). Present these as *descriptive science*, not as a verdict: feeling
stressed is a real signal, never a personal failing.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **ethics** lens: the goal is *understanding and agency*,
never blame — a learner under chronic stress is not "doing it wrong." The
**egalitarian** lens: stress is not distributed fairly — poverty, displacement,
discrimination, and unsafe conditions impose chronic stress on whole communities, so
stress is a justice question as much as a personal one. The **global** lens: every
culture has both words for distress and ways of settling the body; the science here is
one account among many, and the calm-down we practice draws on breathing traditions
held side by side with respect (S-340). The **technology & environment** lens: our
environments and devices can keep the alarm ringing — constant notifications, a
never-ending feed, an always-on world — and noticing that is part of the analysis. The
**social-emotional** lens: this topic can touch real hardship; invite, never force, and
let learners choose what to share. Keep the tone gentle and forward-looking: the point
is a learner who feels they can *do something* for their own body.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back to Grade 9: a feeling has a home in the body — worry
   can knot a stomach, joy can lift a chest. Notice: that is the body and mind talking
   to each other. Today we ask what happens when the body's alarm stays on too long.
2. **Meet the alarm (10 min).** Look at the two-panel diagram. On the left, the
   **acute alarm**: a fast heart, quick breath, tensed muscles, narrowed attention —
   the body getting you ready to act. On the right, the **chronic wear-and-tear**: the
   same alarm, but it never turns off — clouded mood, tired eyes, a knotted stomach,
   trouble sleeping. Learn the two branches: the **sympathetic** branch rings the
   alarm; the **parasympathetic** branch settles the body again (S-338, S-339).
3. **Feel it in your body (8 min).** Watch a worked example, then try it. Sit still and
   quietly notice: where is your body carrying tension right now — shoulders, jaw,
   hands, stomach? Now take one slow breath in through the nose, and a longer, slower
   breath out through the mouth. Notice the tiny shift toward calm on the exhale (S-340).
4. **Trace acute to chronic (12 min).** In your journal, list two or three real
   stressors in your life. Mark each: is it a **passing alarm** (an exam, a race, a
   performance — it fires, then fades) or a **staying alarm** (something that presses
   for weeks or months)? Write one sentence about how a *staying* alarm shows up in
   your body or mood — tension, tiredness, low mood, a racing mind.
5. **Practice settling (12 min).** Learn a tense-and-release: squeeze your hands into
   fists as you breathe in, hold for a count, then let go completely as you breathe
   out (S-227). Move through two or three spots — hands, shoulders, jaw — letting each
   one soften. Notice the contrast between "on" and "off."
6. **Draft (5 min).** Write or record one sentence: *stress is a useful alarm; the harm
   is when it never turns off — here is one thing I can do to help my body settle.*
7. **Close (3 min).** Remember: you are not failing when you feel stressed. You are a
   body with an alarm system — and you are learning, slowly, to help it rest.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a one-signal version ("one thing that alarms me, one thing that
  calms me") with a sentence starter.
- **Extension:** Explain, in your own words, how an alarm that never turns off could
  gradually affect sleep and mood — and preview what you already know about sleep.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name the difference between acute and chronic
  stress and give one body signal and one calm-down?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The acute-to-chronic journal entry,
  added to the unit portfolio as the "body under chronic stress" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *what is one thing in your life that has been a "staying"
pressure, and what helps you settle your body when it builds up?* Listen for their
calm-down. Then try one slow exhale together.
