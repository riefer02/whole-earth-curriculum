# Lesson 4 — Reading My Own Body's Signals

## Summary

Learners **bring the unit's first three forces together** and read them in their own
lives. Using a three-dial tracker, they observe **stress, sleep, and movement** beside
**mood and focus**, name a pattern, and practice the honest distinction between a
**correlation** and a **causal claim**. This is the "analyze" step of the objective:
the learner becomes a careful observer of their own body-mind system.

## Objectives

- Analyze how chronic stress, sleep, and movement shape mood, thinking, and long-term
  health — by reading the pattern in one's own life. _(D03.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

You already have more data about your own body than any tracker could buy. How you
slept last night, what is pressing on you this week, whether you moved today — these
things you can notice, and they tell a story about your mood and your focus. The trick
is learning to *read* the story instead of just living it. A sailor reads the wind and
the tide; a farmer reads the soil and the sky; you can learn to read the three forces
that shape your own mind. No special tool is needed — only attention, honesty, and a
little patience.

## Materials

- Three-dial pattern tracker (stress, sleep, movement → mood and focus)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-dial tracker.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–3, recall the three forces — **chronic stress** wears on
  mood and health; **sleep** consolidates memory and steadies mood (8–10 hours);
  **movement** lifts mood and sharpens focus. Today we read all three *together*.
- Prepare one worked example of reading a pattern so learners can see the skill modeled.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **stress, sleep, and
movement are not separate; they act together on mood and thinking, and a learner can
read the pattern in their own life — while staying honest about what the pattern does
and does not prove.** This is the unit's retrieval-and-application pass (spacing and
retrieval practice are among the most effective learning techniques, S-039): the three
lessons just learned are pulled back together and *used*.

The key intellectual move is **correlation vs. causation.** A learner might notice
"short sleep goes with a foggy morning," but a careful observer does not leap to "sleep
caused the fog" without more data — many things change together (S-039 supports
retrieval practice; the correlation/causation distinction is a general reasoning skill).
Model it: *"On days I slept under six hours, I also felt low — but those were also the
days I was most stressed, so I can't say sleep alone did it. That is a correlation, not
yet a cause."*

The **ethics** lens: self-observation is for *care and agency*, never for self-punishment
or a grade on oneself; a learner who finds no pattern yet has still done the work.
The **egalitarian** lens: what a learner can observe is bounded by what life allows —
work, caregiving, housing, and safety all shape the data; a "bad" pattern is often a
*context*, not a *character flaw*. The **global** lens: people have tracked body rhythms
by season, moon, and labor for thousands of years; this is an ancient human practice in
modern form. The **technology & environment** lens: apps and wearables can help — or can
turn self-observation into self-surveillance; we return to that question in Lesson 9. The
**social-emotional** lens: noticing mood can bring up real feeling; invite, never force,
and let learners share only what they choose. Keep the tone curious and kind.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From memory — no notes — name the three forces and one thing
   each does to mood or thinking: **stress**, **sleep**, **movement**. This is retrieval
   practice: pulling it back up is what makes it stick (S-039).
2. **Meet the tracker (8 min).** Look at the three dials: **stress, sleep, movement** —
   and the strip below for **mood and focus**. Watch a worked example: a day with high
   stress, six hours of sleep, and no movement, marked with low mood and foggy focus;
   a day with low stress, nine hours of sleep, and a long walk, marked with steady mood
   and clear focus. Notice the *pattern* — but also notice what we cannot yet claim.
3. **Track your last few days (15 min).** Fill the tracker for the last three or four
   days you can remember. For each, mark roughly: how stressed were you (low/medium/
   high)? How many hours did you sleep? Did you move much? Then mark your mood and
   focus. Be honest, and be kind — these are observations, not grades.
4. **Find a pattern (10 min).** Read across your rows. Ask: *what travels together?*
   Write one sentence naming a pattern you notice — for example, "on short-sleep days
   my focus drops" or "movement days feel steadier."
5. **Name the limit (8 min).** Learn the difference: a **correlation** is two things
   that travel together; a **causal claim** says one *causes* the other. Write one
   sentence that honestly names the limit of your pattern: *"I see ___ and ___ together,
   but I can't be sure one caused the other, because ___ might also be at work."*
6. **Close (4 min).** Remember: reading your body is a skill, and every careful
   observer starts where you are — with attention and honesty, not with certainty.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-day tracker and a sentence starter ("When I ___, my mood and
  focus tend to ___.").
- **Extension:** Write a cautious causal claim and list the *specific data* you would
  need (more days? a comparison? a control?) to test it more fairly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one pattern across stress, sleep, and
  movement — and state its honest limit (correlation, not proof of cause)?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed tracker and pattern
  reflection, added to the unit portfolio as the "reading my own body's signals"
  analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *do you notice that your sleep, stress, or movement affects
your mood — and which one matters most for you?* Compare their pattern with yours.
