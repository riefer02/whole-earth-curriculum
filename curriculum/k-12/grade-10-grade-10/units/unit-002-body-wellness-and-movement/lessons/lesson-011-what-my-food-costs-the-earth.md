# Lesson 11 — What My Food Costs the Earth

## Summary

Learners explore the **environmental footprint of different foods and diets** — the
greenhouse gases, land, and water behind what we eat — and how **food waste** multiplies
the cost. They trace one meal from field to plate and name one change, held as a
*choice*, never a guilt. This closes the unit by connecting the body to the planet it
eats from.

## Objectives

- Explain the environmental footprint of different foods and diets and how food choices
  affect climate and ecosystems. _(D03.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

Every meal is also a message to the planet. The rice, the beans, the bread, the fish,
the meat, the fruit — each came from somewhere: soil and water, sun and rain, hands and
machines and fuel, and a journey to your plate. Some foods ask a great deal of the Earth
to produce; some ask far less. This is not about guilt — food is a joy, a culture, a
right — it is about *knowing*, so that the choices we do have can be made with our eyes
open. Whether you eat from a market, a farm, a garden, or a food bank, the same truth
holds: what we eat shapes the climate and the living world, and the world's choices are
not all ours to make alone.

## Materials

- Food-footprint diagram (relative land, water, and greenhouse gases; food-waste note)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the food-footprint diagram.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, recall the no-shame line — we judge *claims*, never bodies.
  Today we extend that care to the planet: we weigh *footprints*, never *people*. From
  Grade 9, recall the local food system from farm to table to waste (D03.S3.09.01).
- Prepare one worked example of tracing a meal's footprint.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **different foods and
diets carry different environmental footprints — greenhouse gases, land, and water —
and food waste multiplies the cost, so food choices are also Earth choices.** The
science is anchored by the IPCC, the authoritative intergovernmental summary of climate
science, which documents that agriculture and food systems are a significant source of
greenhouse gases and that land use is a major driver of ecosystem change (S-005). The
**relative** footprint pattern is well established and stated with care: plant foods
(grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits) generally carry *smaller* footprints than animal
foods, and among animal foods, **ruminant meat** (beef, lamb, goat — animals that digest
with methane-producing fermentation) generally carries the *largest* footprint per unit
of food. Hold these as *general, well-documented patterns with real variation* (place,
method, and transport all matter), not as precise numbers. The Ecological Footprint
concept frames a footprint as the land and resources a product *demands* (S-277), and
food waste — roughly a fifth of food available to consumers worldwide — wastes all the
land, water, and energy that went into producing it (S-410).

The **environment** lens is the spine: food is a link between our bodies and the living
Earth. The **global** lens: what people eat is shaped by place, climate, and culture —
a herding community, a rice-growing region, and a coastal fishing town all eat what
their land offers, and no diet is "more moral" by geography alone. The **egalitarian**
lens is essential: *choice* about food is not equally shared — hunger affects roughly 1
in 11 people worldwide, and food insecurity constrains millions of choices (S-409) — so
this lesson names footprint as a *systems* question, never a lecture aimed at the
person with the fewest choices. The **ethics** lens: we weigh footprints of *food*, not
worth of *people*; and those with the most choice carry the most responsibility to use
it well. The **critical-thinking** lens: footprints vary by method and place, so beware
oversimplified "eat this, never that" rules — the honest picture is comparative, not
absolute. The **social-emotional** lens: this topic can stir anxiety; always connect it
to *agency and hope*, never despair — one real change, not a whole life rewritten.
Close the unit gently: the body and the Earth are one system, cared for together.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10, recall the no-shame line: judge claims, never
   bodies. Today we extend that care outward — we weigh *footprints*, never *people*.
   Ask: *what did the food I ate today ask of the Earth to reach me?*
2. **Meet the footprint (12 min).** Look at the diagram. Three costs stand behind every
   food: **greenhouse gases** (warming the climate), **land** (fields, pasture, forest
   cleared), and **water**. Notice the *pattern*: plant foods — grains, beans,
   vegetables, fruit — generally sit low; dairy sits higher; and **ruminant meat**
   (beef, lamb, goat) sits highest, because those animals digest in a way that releases
   methane, and they need a great deal of land (S-005 for the climate-and-land science;
   the relative pattern is the standard, well-documented one).
3. **Trace one meal (15 min).** Choose a meal you know — breakfast, lunch, or dinner.
   List its main foods. For each, ask: *did it come from a plant or an animal? How much
   land, water, and travel might it have needed?* Mark a rough footprint (low/medium/
   high) for each. This is a *trace*, not a precise calculation.
4. **Meet the waste (8 min).** Learn this: roughly **a fifth of the food available to
   consumers worldwide is wasted** — and wasted food wastes all the land, water, and
   energy that made it (S-410). Ask: *where does food get wasted in my own life — and
   what is one small way I could waste less?*
5. **Name one change, with hope (10 min).** In your journal, write one change you could
   actually make — eating a little more plant food, wasting less, choosing local or
   seasonal when you can, learning where your food comes from. Frame it as *choice and
   care*, not guilt: one real step, not a perfect life.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember the whole unit: your body is shaped by stress, sleep, and
   movement; it is understood by many cultures; it can be trained with care; and it eats
   from a living Earth. Caring for your body and caring for the planet are the same act,
   done a little at a time.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-food comparison (beans vs. beef) and one change to choose
  from a short list.
- **Extension:** Compare two whole diets (e.g., plant-heavy vs. meat-heavy) across land,
  water, and greenhouse gases, and weigh one *fairness* consideration — whose choices
  are limited, and who carries the most responsibility to choose well.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner explain the relative footprint of plant foods
  versus ruminant meat, state how food waste multiplies the cost, and name one change
  held as choice, not guilt?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The meal trace and one-change
  reflection, added to the unit portfolio as the "what my food costs the Earth" entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *where did the food we eat this week come from, and is there one
way our home could waste less or choose a little lighter?* Decide on one shared, doable
change together.
