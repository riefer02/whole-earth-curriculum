# Lesson 2 — Sleep: The Brain's Night Shift

## Summary

Learners explore what sleep does for **mood and thinking** — it consolidates memory,
helps regulate emotion, and restores the body — and meet the teen sleep-need range of
**about 8–10 hours per night** (S-050). They map their own night against the sleep
cycle and design **one small, dignified sleep-hygiene change**.

## Objectives

- Analyze how chronic stress, sleep, and movement shape mood, thinking, and long-term
  health — focusing on the sleep side. _(D03.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Every animal sleeps — birds on a wire, fish drifting in the current, humans in beds
and mats and hammocks across the world. Sleep is not laziness and not lost time; it is
the brain's **night shift**, the hours when it sorts the day, files what you learned,
and lets your body repair. When you cut it short — for homework, a feed that never
ends, a job, or a restless home — you borrow against yourself, and the interest shows
up in your mood, your memory, and your health. Whether your night is quiet or noisy,
long or interrupted, understanding what sleep is *for* is the first step to protecting
it.

## Materials

- Sleep-need and cycle diagram (light → deep → REM; 8–10 hours)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the sleep-cycle diagram.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the two branches of the nervous system — the alarm
  and the calm. Sleep is one of the body's most powerful "calm" states; today we see
  what it does.
- Preview the sleep-hygiene change so you can model one.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **sleep is an active
state that consolidates memory, regulates mood, and restores the body — and teens need
about 8–10 hours a night** (S-050). The 8–10 hour figure is the standard international
benchmark for adolescents (13–18 years) from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine
consensus (S-050); hold it as a *general guide*, not a rule — family life, work,
caregiving, and living conditions all shape real sleep, and no learner should be shamed
for a short night. For the memory-consolidation and mood-regulation claims, speak
accurately and with hedging: sleep researchers have found that during deep sleep and
REM the brain strengthens and sorts what was learned, and that short sleep is linked to
lower mood and poorer focus — these are *general, well-replicated findings*, offered as
description, not as a score on any learner.

Model once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **egalitarian** lens: sleep is not
distributed equally — shift work, crowded housing, light and noise, and caregiving
responsibilities all cut into sleep, and those burdens fall unevenly, so sleep is a
fairness question as well as a personal habit. The **global** lens: people sleep
differently everywhere — some in one long night, some with a midday rest, some in
shared rooms — and no pattern is more "correct" than another. The **technology &
environment** lens: screens, light, and the always-on feed are among the loudest
thieves of sleep; noticing them is part of the analysis. The **ethics** lens: the goal
is agency and dignity, never blame — a tired learner is not failing. The
**social-emotional** lens: sleep and mood are tightly linked; invite, never force
sharing, and hold this gently. Preview the retrieval pass: this lesson will be recalled
in Lessons 4 and 11.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: the body has an alarm branch and a calm branch.
   Notice that sleep is the deepest, most natural "calm" the body knows. Ask yourself:
   *how many hours did I actually sleep last night?*
2. **Meet the night shift (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Sleep is not one flat state;
   it cycles — **light sleep**, then **deep sleep** (when the body repairs and the
   brain files facts), then **REM** (rapid-eye-movement, when we dream and the brain
   sorts emotion) — and the cycle repeats through the night (S-050 for the need range;
   the cycle is the standard, well-established picture). Notice the band at the bottom:
   teens need about **8–10 hours**.
3. **Map your night (12 min).** Draw a timeline of last night. Shade in roughly when
   you fell asleep, when you woke, and any waking in between. Compare your total
   against the 8–10 hour band. Do this without judging yourself — it is *information*,
   not a grade.
4. **Follow the links (12 min).** Learn the two links. **Memory:** during deep sleep and
   REM the brain strengthens what you learned that day — sleep is when studying
   "settles in." **Mood:** short sleep is linked to lower mood, irritability, and poorer
   focus. In your journal, write one sentence about how *your* focus or mood tends to
   go when you sleep too little.
5. **Design one change (10 min).** Choose one small, dignified **sleep-hygiene** step
   you could actually take: a steadier bedtime, a screen-free half hour before bed,
   dimmer light, a cooler room, less late caffeine, a quiet wind-down. Pick one that
   fits *your* life — not an idealized one.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: sleep is not lost time; it is the night shift your
   brain works for you. Protecting it, even a little, is an act of care for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a simplified night map (asleep → woke) and a menu of two hygiene
  options to choose from.
- **Extension:** Explain, in your own words, how cutting sleep could interact with
  chronic stress (Lesson 1) to weigh on mood — and name the *most* realistic change you
  could make.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state the teen sleep-need range, one thing
  sleep does for the brain, and one hygiene change that fits their life?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The night-map and one-change plan,
  added to the unit portfolio as the "sleep: the brain's night shift" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *what was sleep like when you were my age — and what is one
thing you do now to protect your sleep?* Listen, then share the one change you chose.
