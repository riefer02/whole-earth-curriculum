# Lesson 10 — Diets and Claims: Reading the Evidence

## Summary

Learners build a **four-question checklist** for evaluating popular diets and nutrition
claims against **evidence** — and do it in a **body-neutral** way that shames no one.
They distinguish a promise from proof, notice who profits, and hold every body with
dignity, treating nutrition claims as things to weigh, never people to judge.

## Objectives

- Evaluate popular diets and nutrition claims using scientific evidence, without
  shaming any body. _(D03.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Everywhere you look, someone is selling a way to eat: a tea that "melts fat," a plan
that "resets your metabolism," a diet that "everyone" swears by. Some of these rest on
real science; many rest on marketing, a single anecdote, or a whisper. The skill that
protects you is the same one from Lesson 6 — reading the evidence — now aimed at food.
And here is the part that matters most: the worth of a body is never on trial when we
judge a diet. A claim can be weak without the person believing it being foolish, and a
body can be healthy at many sizes, in many ways of eating, across many cultures. Today
you learn to judge the *claim* — and to refuse, firmly and kindly, to judge the *person*.

## Materials

- Claim-vs-evidence checklist (what it promises, what evidence, who benefits, what it leaves out)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall the evidence ladder (anecdote → correlation → trial →
  systematic review). Today we apply it to food. From Lesson 3 and the WHO healthy-diet
  frame, recall that health comes from *variety and balance*, not from any single
  "superfood" or ban (S-038).
- Prepare one worked example of a real nutrition claim run through the checklist.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a nutrition claim is
weighed against evidence — and the weighing is always done without shaming any body, at
any size, in any way of eating.** The evidence baseline is the WHO healthy-diet fact
sheet: health is supported by *variety* across food groups, and by limiting free sugars,
salt/sodium, and saturated fat — not by any single magic food or by banning a whole
category (S-038). Energy and nutrient needs **differ among bodies** by age, sex, size,
and activity, and rise during the adolescent growth spurt (S-285) — which is *why*
one-size-fits-all diet rules mislead. Marketing aimed at children and teens for foods
high in fat, salt, and sugar is something the WHO explicitly recommends *limiting*
(S-312) — a signal that much of what learners see is persuasion, not evidence.

Hold the **no-shame line** as a values commitment, distinct from the facts (philosophy
§5): a learner's body and their family's way of eating are honored, always. The
**ethics** lens: judging food claims is legitimate; judging bodies is not — and pushing a
restrictive diet onto a growing body can itself cause harm, so "evidence-based" also
means "does not harm." The **egalitarian** lens: what a person can eat is shaped by
budget, access, culture, and food security (S-409); a "healthy" claim that assumes wealth
and choice is a narrow claim. The **global** lens: every culture has its own healthy
foods and ways of eating, and none is "correct" — the WHO's variety principle shows up
in dishes around the world (S-038). The **technology & environment** lens: diet claims
travel fastest through feeds, ads, and influencers whose incentives are engagement and
profit, not health — and every food behind a claim also carries an environmental
footprint, which Lesson 11 will trace. The **critical-thinking** lens: run each claim
through the four questions — *what does it promise? what evidence backs it? who
benefits? what does it leave out?* The **social-emotional** lens: diet talk can sting,
especially for a body still growing; keep it gentle, and never let the discussion slide
into commenting on anyone's body. Preview: Lesson 11 turns from the *body* to the
*planet* — the environmental footprint of food.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6, name the evidence ladder. From Lesson 3, recall
   that health comes from variety and balance, not one magic food. Now aim that skill at
   the loudest voice in the room: the diet claim.
2. **Meet the checklist (10 min).** Look at the four questions:
   - **What does it promise?** ("Lose weight fast," "detox," "reset your metabolism.")
   - **What evidence backs it?** (An anecdote? a trial? a systematic review?)
   - **Who benefits?** (The seller? The influencer? Or your actual health?)
   - **What does it leave out?** (Cost, culture, sustainability, whether it harms a
     growing body.)
   Watch a worked example: "This tea flushes toxins and melts fat" — *promises* a quick
   fix; *evidence*: anecdote only; *who benefits*: the seller; *left out*: your liver and
   kidneys already "detox" you, and no tea replaces whole-food variety (S-038).
3. **Evaluate a real claim (15 min).** Choose a diet or nutrition claim you have seen —
   a "keto," "detox," "intermittent fasting," or "superfood" claim, or a local remedy.
   Run it through all four questions. Write what you find, even when the answer is "no
   trial exists."
4. **Hold the no-shame line (10 min).** Learn to separate two sentences: *"this claim is
   not well supported"* (about the claim) and *"this body is wrong"* (about a person —
   never said). Write one sentence of each kind about your claim, so the difference is
   in your own words. Remember: energy and nutrient needs differ among bodies (S-285),
   and a growing body needs *enough*, not a ban.
5. **Draft (7 min).** Write or record: *the claim I evaluated, what the evidence
   actually shows, and how I would explain it without shaming anyone.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: judge claims, not people. A body is not a problem to be
   solved — it is a home to be fed, with dignity, in the way that fits its life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a single claim pre-chosen and a two-question version (promise vs.
  evidence).
- **Extension:** Trace *who profits* from a claim — seller, sponsor, platform — and write
  one sentence on how marketing hides the "what it leaves out" question.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner run a nutrition claim through the four questions
  and state, in their own words, why a weak claim never makes a body wrong?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed checklist and no-shame
  reflection, added to the unit portfolio as the "diets and claims" analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust about a food rule or remedy your family has heard of. Together,
gently run it through the four questions — and notice that asking never means judging
anyone's body.
