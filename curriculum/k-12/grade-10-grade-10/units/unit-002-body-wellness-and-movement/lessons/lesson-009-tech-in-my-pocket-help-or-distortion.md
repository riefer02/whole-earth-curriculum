# Lesson 9 — Tech in My Pocket: Help or Distortion?

## Summary

Learners **evaluate fitness trackers and apps** with a critical eye: what they measure
and miss, how they motivate, and how the same tool can **support or distort** a healthy
relationship with movement and the body. They draw their own line between help and
distortion, grounded in what healthy movement actually is.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology, such as fitness trackers and apps, supports or distorts a
  healthy relationship with movement and the body. _(D03.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

A device on your wrist now counts your steps, scores your sleep, and reminds you to
move — and millions of people wear one, hoping it will make them healthier. Sometimes
it does: a nudge to walk, a record of progress, a little encouragement. Sometimes it
strains: a red ring of "not enough," a broken streak that stings, a number that becomes
a judgment on your worth. The tool is not good or bad on its own — it is a mirror with
a shape, and the shape matters. Whether you live with a screen in your pocket or none
at all, learning to *read* these tools is a skill you will use for the rest of your
life, because the question is never just about the tracker — it is about who decides
what your body is for.

## Materials

- Help-or-distortion two-column sheet
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall what healthy movement actually is — aerobic plus
  muscle-strengthening activity, done with joy and recovery (S-341); and from Lesson 7,
  the principles of progressive effort and recovery. Today we ask: *does the tech in my
  pocket serve that picture, or distort it?*
- Prepare one worked example of a feature as help and one as distortion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a fitness tracker or
app is a tool that can support or distort a healthy relationship with movement and the
body — and the learner can evaluate which, feature by feature, against what healthy
movement actually is.** The anchor for "healthy" is the WHO picture of balanced activity
(S-341); the cautionary anchor is the well-documented reality that media and technology
can shape body image and self-worth (S-226 documents the research program on media and
tech's impact on adolescent development; S-411 documents how media and social messages
shape body image). Hold the *specific* claims about trackers honestly and with hedging:
the research on wearables is **real but mixed and maturing** — devices can help some
people build consistent habits, while their step counts, calorie estimates, and sleep
scores are only *approximate*, and their streaks and comparisons can feed anxiety and
negative body image for others. Present these as tendencies to evaluate, not as a
verdict that "trackers are bad."

The **technology** lens is the spine: read the tool as a *designed object* — every
prompt, streak, and score was chosen by someone to shape behavior, sometimes helpfully,
sometimes for engagement or profit. The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *what does it
measure, what does it miss, and how accurate is it?* — a step count is not a life, and a
calorie number is an estimate, not a fact. The **ethics** lens: is the tool nudging me
toward care or toward shame? Tools that punish with "you're behind" deserve the same
scrutiny as tools that encourage. The **egalitarian** lens: trackers are not equally
available or equally fair — they assume a wrist, a phone, a budget, and a body that can
step — and their "norms" (10,000 steps, closing rings) are not universal truths, just
targets someone set. The **social-emotional** lens: numbers about our bodies can sting;
invite, never force, and let learners share only what they choose. Preview: the same
"claim vs. evidence" eye returns for diets in Lesson 10.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, what is healthy movement? (Aerobic plus
   muscle-strengthening activity, with joy and recovery.) From Lesson 7, progress comes
   from *small steps plus rest*. Hold that picture up — today we test the tech against
   it.
2. **Meet the two sides (10 min).** Look at the two-column sheet. Watch a worked
   example: a tracker that **helps** — it reminds you to move, records a walk, cheers a
   rest day as part of the plan. A tracker that **distorts** — it breaks your streak with
   a red "you failed," compares you to strangers, and turns a number into a verdict on
   your body. Notice: the *same tool* can do both.
3. **Examine a real tool (15 min).** Choose a tracker, smartwatch, or fitness app you
   know — or imagine one. List its features in the two columns: which ones serve *your*
   healthy movement (nudges, records, rest), and which ones strain it (streaks,
   comparisons, "not enough" scores, calorie guilt)? Be specific.
4. **Ask the sharp questions (10 min).** For each feature, ask: *what does it measure,
   and what does it miss?* (A step count misses a swim, a wheelchair push, a dance, a
   quiet stretch.) *How accurate is it?* (Step counts and calorie burns are estimates.)
   *Who set this target, and for whom?* (10,000 steps is a marketing number, not a
   medical truth.)
5. **Draw your line (10 min).** In your journal, write: *here is where this tool helps
   me, and here is where I will not let it drive.* Name one way you would use it
   wisely — or one reason you would leave it off.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the tool is a mirror with a shape. You get to decide
   what your body is *for* — and no streak, ring, or number outranks that.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a short feature list to sort into "helps" and "strains," and a
  sentence starter ("This tool helps me when ___, but I will not let it ___.").
- **Extension:** Analyze *why* a designer built a streak or comparison feature (engagement?
  profit? care?) and argue what a *caring* design would do differently.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one way a tracker supports and one way it
  distorts a healthy relationship with movement, and state their own line?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The help-or-distortion evaluation,
  added to the unit portfolio as the "tech in my pocket" analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *have you ever used a step counter or fitness app — did it help
you or stress you?* Compare your two columns.
