# Lesson 10 — Surveying and Navigation with Right Triangles

## Summary

Learners apply the Pythagorean theorem and the trigonometric ratios to real
**surveying and navigation**: finding the height of a tall object by angle of
elevation, the distance across an uncrossable gap, and a bearing to hold. They build
and use a simple clinometer and check each result by a second method.

## Objectives

- Use trigonometric ratios and the Pythagorean theorem to solve right-triangle
  problems in real contexts such as surveying or navigation. _(D05.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

You want to know how tall a tree is, but you cannot climb it. You want to know how far
it is across a river, but you cannot walk it. You want to hold a straight course over
open water with no roads to follow. People have faced all three for thousands of
years — and solved them with the same small kit: a **right triangle**, two known
quantities, and the relationships between its sides and angles. Surveyors still work
this way today, and so do navigators, builders, and mapmakers. The tree, the river,
and the open sea all surrender to a triangle drawn in the mind.

## Materials

- Surveying problem set
- Protractor, ruler, and a weighted string
- Math journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the surveying problem set.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, the three trig ratios; from Grade 8, the Pythagorean
  theorem. Today we apply both to measuring the world.
- Prepare the clinometer (protractor + weighted string) and a tall object to sight.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **height, distance
across a gap, and bearing are all right-triangle problems — sight the angle of
elevation or depression, measure one side, and the trig ratios plus the Pythagorean
theorem give the rest.** Teach with worked examples (S-011), then let learners measure
something real and check by a second method (feedback).

The **global** lens: measuring by right triangle is a shared human technique. The
right-triangle relationship was known in Mesopotamia long before the name
"Pythagorean theorem" attached to it — the tablet Plimpton 322 (c. 1800–1650 BCE)
carries Pythagorean triples (S-354); the Indian Sulba Sutras state the relation for
altar construction (S-292, S-434); and the Chinese *Nine Chapters* (c. 200 BCE) uses
the gou-gu rule (S-289). The name is a convenience, not a claim about who first knew
it. The **intellectual** lens: every surveying problem reduces to "name the two
knowns, choose the right relation, solve." The **critical-thinking** lens: always
check a result by a *second* method (shadow vs. elevation; sine vs. Pythagorean
theorem) — a measurement you cannot check is a guess. The **egalitarian** lens: these
were and are tools for ordinary work — laying fields, building homes, fishing and
sailing — belonging to everyone, not to experts. The **environment** lens: measuring
land honestly matters for fair division of fields and for knowing the places we depend
on.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9, write the three trig ratios. What two knowns
   does each need to find a missing side?
2. **Height by elevation (15 min).** Worked example: stand a measured distance d from
   a tree and sight the **angle of elevation** θ to its top with a clinometer. Then
   tan θ = (height − eye height) / d, so height = d · tan θ + eye height. Work one full
   example with numbers, then build your clinometer (protractor + weighted string).
3. **Distance across a gap (15 min).** You cannot cross a river, but you can walk a
   baseline b along the bank, then sight the far bank at an angle θ from the baseline's
   end. Then tan θ = (distance across) / b, so the width = b · tan θ. Work it, then use
   the Pythagorean theorem as a second check when you know two sides.
4. **Bearing (5 min).** A **bearing** is a direction measured as an angle. If you sail
   a course and drift, a right triangle of "intended" and "actual" path tells you how
   far off you are — and the angle tells you how to correct. State the idea in one
   sentence.
5. **Measure something real (10 min).** In pairs, sight and measure one real object —
   a tree, a building, a pole — and find its height. Check by a second method (shadow
   ratio). Compare answers across pairs.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say the two knowns you need to find (a) a height and (b) a
   distance across a gap.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a half-solved problem with the triangle already drawn and labeled;
  focus on choosing the ratio.
- **Extension:** Solve a two-step problem — the angle of elevation to a cliff top and
  the angle of depression to its base from the same point — to find the cliff's
  height.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self + performance):** Can the learner sight an angle, measure a
  baseline, and compute a real height or width, checked by a second method?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed surveying problem set with the real
  measurement and its second-method check, added to the geometry toolkit.

## Home connection

Pick a tall thing near home — a tree, a lamp post, a building. Measure your distance
from it and sight its top (a straw and a protractor work). Compute its height, then
check with its shadow. Show someone both answers.
