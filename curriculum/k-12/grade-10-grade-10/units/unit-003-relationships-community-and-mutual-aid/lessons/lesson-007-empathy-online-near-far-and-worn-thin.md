# Lesson 7 — Empathy Online: Near, Far, and Worn Thin

## Summary

Learners **evaluate how digital platforms shape empathy**. They learn the difference
between *empathy* (feeling with), *empathic concern* (caring that motivates helping), and
*personal distress* (feeling overwhelmed), and see how a feed both *extends* care to
distant voices and *flattens* it: empathy runs strongest toward one identifiable person
and weakens under an endless stream of crises — a wearing-down called **compassion
fatigue**. They close by choosing, deliberately, where their care will go.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how digital platforms shape empathy, compassion fatigue, and echo chambers —
  beginning with empathy and compassion fatigue. _(D02.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

One child's face, one name, one story — and your chest tightens; you want to help. Now
scroll: ten disasters, fifty headlines, a thousand faces, and something in you goes quiet.
It is not that you stopped caring; it is that the same capacity that leaps toward one
person buckles under a flood. This is one of the quietest effects of living with a feed in
your pocket: it can carry a stranger's joy and pain straight to you from across the world —
a real gift — and it can also leave you numb, because no heart can hold a thousand crises
at once. Whether your feed is yours or a shared screen at a shop, learning to *read* this
effect — and to choose where your care goes — is a skill for staying human in a connected
world.

## Materials

- Compassion curve diagram
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the compassion curve.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall the difference between empathy (feeling *with*) and
  sympathy (feeling *for*) (D02.S2.06.01). Today we add two finer distinctions and ask
  what a screen does to all of them.
- Prepare a worked example: one named story and one stream of headlines.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **digital platforms both
extend and flatten empathy — they carry distant voices to us, yet empathy runs strongest
toward one identifiable person and weakens under a constant stream of suffering, a
wearing-down called compassion fatigue — so sustaining care is a deliberate choice, not an
automatic result of seeing more.** The distinctions are well grounded: researchers
separate *empathic concern* (other-oriented care) from *personal distress* (self-oriented
overwhelm), with different motivational consequences (S-230), and document that empathy is
*narrow and biased* — strongest toward those who resemble us and toward single
identifiable victims, weaker toward large numbers — a descriptive claim about how empathy
actually works, not a verdict that empathy is bad (S-413). The "identifiable victim" and
"psychic numbing" effects are standard findings in this literature. The WHO frames
adolescence as a formative time whose digital habits can shape wellbeing, and notes media
can help or weigh down (S-311). Present all of this as *description*, and hold the value
(choose where your care goes) as a value.

Model the curve once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **technology** lens is the spine: read the feed as a
*designed* object — endless scroll and autoplay are choices that keep the stream coming.
The **social-emotional** lens: this topic touches real exhaustion and grief; invite, never
force, and let learners share only what they choose. The **ethics** lens: compassion
fatigue is not a moral failure — it is a predictable limit, and the ethical question is
how to care *sustainably* rather than feel guilty for going numb. The **egalitarian**
lens: whose suffering reaches us (and whose does not) is shaped by algorithms, geography,
and power — some people's pain is endlessly shown, others' is invisible. The
**critical-thinking** lens: ask *how many crises can I actually hold, and what does acting
on one look like?* The **global** lens: the same mechanism operates for everyone online,
but its weight falls unevenly. Preview: Lesson 8 turns to the echo chamber — the other
way platforms shape empathy.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 6: what is the difference between empathy and sympathy?
   (Feeling *with* someone vs. feeling *for* them.) Today we ask what a screen does to
   that feeling.
2. **Meet the distinctions (10 min).** Learn three words. **Empathy** — feeling with
   another. **Empathic concern** — care that moves you to *help*. **Personal distress** —
   feeling so overwhelmed by another's pain that you turn away to protect yourself (S-230).
   All three are human; only one reliably leads to helping.
3. **Meet the compassion curve (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Watch a worked example: one
   named face and story — the curve rises, and you want to act. Then a stream of ten, fifty,
   a thousand headlines — the curve falls. This is **compassion fatigue**: care worn thin by
   a flood, and the "identifiable victim" effect: one person moves us, a statistic does not
   (S-413).
4. **Read a real feed (12 min).** Look at a news or social feed (or recall one). Count how
   many distant crises pass in one minute. Notice your own response: which one stopped you,
   and why? Which ones slid past?
5. **Choose where your care goes (12 min).** In your journal: *one stream I can put down is
   ___, and one place I choose to put my care — concretely — is ___.* Name one real action,
   however small.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: going numb is not a moral failure — it is a signal that you
   have been asked to hold too much. You get to choose, deliberately, where your care goes —
   and to sustain it by acting, not just scrolling.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-word version ("one face moves me; a flood numbs me") and a
  sentence starter ("I will put my care into ___ by ___.").
- **Extension:** Argue how a platform *could* be designed to sustain care (fewer, deeper
  stories; a path to act; rest) instead of draining it — and name the trade-off.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner distinguish empathy, empathic concern, and personal
  distress, explain the compassion curve, and name one deliberate place to put their care?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "where my care goes" journal entry,
  added to the unit portfolio as the empathy-and-compassion-fatigue reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *do you ever feel numb after too much bad news — and what helps you
care without burning out?* Compare your answers.
