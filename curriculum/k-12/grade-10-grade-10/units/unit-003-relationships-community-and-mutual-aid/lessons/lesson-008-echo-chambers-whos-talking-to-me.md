# Lesson 8 — Echo Chambers: Who's Talking to Me?

## Summary

Learners **evaluate echo chambers and filter bubbles**: how **recommendation systems** and
our own choices (**homophily** — liking to be with people who think like us) narrow what
we see, and how that narrowing shapes empathy — it is easy to feel with people who already
agree with us, and hard across difference. They close by auditing their own feed and
naming one step to widen it.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how digital platforms shape empathy, compassion fatigue, and echo chambers —
  the echo-chamber half of the standard. _(D02.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

Scroll your feed for a minute and notice who is talking to you. Often it is a chorus of
voices saying versions of what you already think — liking what you like, angry at what
angers you, agreeing with you back. It feels comfortable, like a room full of friends. But
a room where everyone agrees is also a room where you never have to stretch: it is easy to
feel with people who already sound like you, and far harder to feel with someone whose
life and views are different. Some of that room is your own doing — we all drift toward
the familiar — and some of it is built by the feed itself, which learns what keeps you
scrolling and shows you more of it. Whether your feed is in your pocket or on a shared
screen, learning to notice *who is missing* from the room — and to open the door — is a
skill for keeping your mind, and your empathy, wide.

## Materials

- Echo chamber diagram
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the echo chamber diagram.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall that empathy runs narrow and can be worn thin. Today we
  ask the other question: what *narrows the room itself* — and how does that shrink our
  empathy?
- Prepare a worked example: a topic where the learner's feed repeats one view.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an echo chamber is what
happens when recommendation systems and our own homophily narrow the voices we hear — and
narrowing the room narrows empathy, because it is easy to feel with the similar and hard
across difference; the counter-move is to notice who is missing and deliberately widen the
circle.** The mechanism is real: recommendation and ranking algorithms prioritize content
that engages, and personalization can surround a person with confirming information — the
"filter bubble" (S-482) and the echo-chamber dynamic that Britannica documents (S-481).
Be honest about the *debate*: scholars disagree about how strong the filter-bubble effect
is — some find it weaker than popular accounts claim — so present it as a *tendency to
examine*, not a law (S-482). The counter-moves are practical: follow across difference,
seek first-person voices, and check claims against independent sources — "lateral reading"
(S-063, S-217).

Model the mechanism once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **technology** lens is the spine: read the feed as a
*designed* object whose ranking is a choice made by someone, for engagement or profit. The
**critical-thinking** lens: ask *who is missing, and whose account am I not hearing?* — and
check each claim against its source. The **ethics** lens: living only among the like-minded
is a quiet ethical risk, because it lets our judgments go untested; widening the circle is
an act of intellectual honesty. The **egalitarian** lens: whose voices get amplified and
whose get crowded out is a question of power — the same feed that comforts the majority can
silence the minority. The **social-emotional** lens: this topic can feel exposing (our
feeds reveal our habits); invite, never force, and let learners share only what they
choose. The **global** lens: the same mechanism works in every language and region, but its
effects differ with who holds platform power. Preview: with empathy examined, Lessons 9–10
turn to belonging and community.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: empathy runs narrow and can be worn thin. Today we
   ask what *narrows the room itself* — and what that does to our empathy.
2. **Meet the echo chamber (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Inside the circle, every voice
   repeats one note; you feel agreed with, and comfortable. Learn the two forces that built
   the room: **homophily** — we drift toward people who think like us — and the
   **recommendation feed** — which learns what keeps us scrolling and shows more of it
   (S-481, S-482).
3. **Notice the empathy cost (10 min).** It is easy to feel *with* someone who already
   sounds like you. It is hard to feel with someone whose life is different — and the
   narrower your room, the less practice you get. Write one sentence: *the more my feed
   agrees with me, the ___ my empathy becomes across difference.*
4. **Audit your feed (12 min).** Take one topic you care about. List the voices you always
   hear on it. Then list the voices you *never* hear — the people most affected, the
   dissenters, the first-person voices from inside another community. Be honest: why are
   they missing?
5. **Open the door (12 min).** Learn the counter-moves: **follow across difference** (one
   voice unlike yours), **seek first-person voices** (from Lessons 5–6), and **check claims
   against independent sources** — lateral reading (S-063, S-217). Write one concrete step
   you will take this week to widen your circle.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a room full of agreement is comfortable, but comfort is not
   the same as understanding. You get to choose how wide your door stands.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-column "voices I hear / voices I never hear" sheet and a
  sentence starter ("To widen my circle, I will ___.").
- **Extension:** Weigh the filter-bubble debate — why some researchers find the effect
  weaker than popular accounts claim — and argue what evidence would settle it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name the two forces that build an echo chamber,
  explain its effect on empathy, and state one concrete step to widen their circle?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The feed-audit and widen-the-circle
  entry, added to the unit portfolio as the echo-chamber reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *whose voices do you hear most — and whose are you missing?* Then
each of you names one voice you could seek out this week.
