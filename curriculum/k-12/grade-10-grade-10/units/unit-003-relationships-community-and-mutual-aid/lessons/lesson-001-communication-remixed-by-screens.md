# Lesson 1 — Communication, Remixed by Screens

## Summary

Learners **evaluate how technology and social media change communication**: which cues
they keep, which they hide, and what they add. Using the reduced-cues picture of online
communication (S-343), they distinguish what is *gained* — reach across distance, speed,
a permanent record — from what is *lost or reshaped* — tone, face, body, and the instant
chance to repair a misread. They close by naming one gain and one thing to watch for in
their own messages.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology and social media shape communication — beginning with *how*
  the medium changes the message itself. _(D02.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Say the sentence "I'll be there soon" three ways: warm, joking, and angry. In person, the
words carry the whole meaning — your face, your voice, your hands say as much as the
sentence. Now type the same sentence into a message. The screen keeps only the words and
strips the rest: the warmth, the joke, the edge all vanish, and the reader has to guess.
Whether you live in a crowded city or a quiet village, and whether your screen is a phone
in your pocket or a shared one at a shop, the same quiet trade happens every time you send
a message: the screen **remixes** your communication, keeping some signals and dropping
others. Learning to read that trade is a skill you will use for the rest of your life.

## Materials

- Face-to-face vs. screen two-column sheet
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column sheet (with a third band for "what screens add").
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall that communication carries meaning through tone, body
  language, and context (D02.S1.09.01). Today we ask what happens when a screen strips
  some of those carriers away — and adds a few of its own.
- Prepare one worked example of a message that got misread because the tone was missing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **every medium shapes
what a message can carry — screens keep words and speed but drop tone, face, and body,
and add reach, permanence, and an invisible audience — so misreading is ordinary, not a
personal failure, and checking is a skill.** The reduced-cues account is well documented:
online communication removes or delays the social cues that carry meaning (tone, facial
expression, gesture, immediate reaction) and can loosen self-restraint — the "online
disinhibition effect" (S-343). The gains are real too: messages cross distance instantly,
persist for later, and reach audiences we choose or never see (S-481 documents what social
media platforms are and how their feeds work). Present both sides as *description*, not as
a verdict that "screens are bad" — the same affordances that connect also mislead.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **technology** lens is the spine: read the medium as a
*designed thing* — persistence, public-by-default audiences, and disappearing stories are
all choices someone built, and each shapes what we dare to say. The **critical-thinking**
lens: ask *which cues survive and which are dropped, and how does that change the meaning?*
The **social-emotional** lens: misreads and hurtful replies are real and common; invite,
never force, and let learners share only what they choose. The **ethics** lens: a screen
that hides the other person's face can make cruelty feel costless — noticing that is the
first guard against it. The **egalitarian** lens: access to these tools is not equal —
cost, connectivity, disability, and language shape who can reach whom (S-281) — and the
"norms" of one platform are not universal truths. The **global** lens: people everywhere
weave screens into daily talk differently; no single way of being online is the right one.
Preview: Lesson 2 turns from *what screens change* to *how to use them respectfully*.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back to Grade 9: how do you know what a friend *really*
   means? Not just their words — their tone, their face, their body, how fast they reply.
   Today we ask: what happens to all of that when the message travels through a screen?
2. **Meet the two sides (10 min).** Look at the two-column sheet. Watch a worked example:
   a text that says "fine." In person, "fine" plus a sigh plus crossed arms means one
   thing; the same word alone on a screen means something the reader must guess. List, in
   the first column, the cues you get *in person* (tone, face, gesture, instant reaction);
   in the second, the cues the *screen hides*; and in the third band, what the screen
   *adds* (reach across distance, speed, a lasting record, an audience you cannot see).
3. **Feel it in a real message (10 min).** Recall a message you sent or received that got
   misread. Which cue went missing? (The joke without the laugh? The concern without the
   warm voice?) Write one sentence about it.
4. **Name the disinhibition (10 min).** Learn the term: because the other person's face is
   not in front of us, people sometimes type things they would never say aloud — the
   "online disinhibition effect" (S-343). Think of one example you have seen, and one
   reason it happens (no face, no immediate reaction, sometimes no name).
5. **Draft your trade (12 min).** In your journal, write: *here is one thing screens let me
   do that I value (reach, speed, record), and here is one thing they cost me (lost tone,
   misread words, a cruel word made easy).*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a screen is a remix, not a mirror. It keeps some of your
   meaning and drops the rest — so the person reading it deserves your care *and* your
   patience. Preview: next time, we learn the respectful moves.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a short list of cues to sort into "kept" and "dropped," and a sentence
  starter ("A screen keeps ___ but drops ___, so I should ___.").
- **Extension:** Analyze a specific platform's design (disappearing messages, permanent
  feed, public comments) and argue how that one design choice changes what people say.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one cue a screen hides and one thing it adds,
  and give a real example of a misread?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "remix trade" journal entry, added to
  the unit portfolio as the "how screens shape communication" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *have you ever had a message misread because the tone was missing —
what happened?* Compare your two stories, then decide together on one way to send clearer
messages.
