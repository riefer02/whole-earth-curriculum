# Lesson 2 — Respect in the Feed: Using Digital Tools Well

## Summary

Learners turn from *what screens change* to *how to use them respectfully*. They
practice a **pause** before posting, run a message through three gates — *is it true?
is it kind? would I want it said or shown about me?* — and honor **consent, privacy,
and boundaries**. They close by writing one personal rule for respectful digital use.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology and social media shape communication and use digital tools
  respectfully — the "use it well" half of the standard. _(D02.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Imagine a photo from a friend's party appears online — tagged with your name, or with
your friend mid-laugh looking silly. No one asked first. Now flip it: you are the one
about to post. The pause you take in that moment — *should I ask? would they want this
shown? would I want it shown about me?* — is the whole skill in one breath. Whether your
screen is a phone, a shared family device, or a public kiosk, and whether you are in a
big city or a small town, respect online is the same as respect anywhere: it is about
treating the person on the other side as a person, even when you cannot see their face.
Learning to pause, check, and ask is a habit you will carry everywhere.

## Materials

- Pause-before-you-post decision card
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-gate decision card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall that screens hide tone and face, so misreading is
  common — which is *why* the pause matters. Today we practice the respectful moves.
- Prepare one worked example of a draft that fails a gate and gets revised.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **respectful digital
use is a practiced skill — pause, check the truth and the kindness of a message, ask
before sharing others' words or images, and set boundaries for your own privacy.** The
anchors are well established: online-safety guidance centers keeping personal information
private and telling a trusted adult when something feels wrong (S-127, S-128); media
literacy frames asking where information comes from and separating fact from persuasion
as a core skill (S-063, S-217); and the reduced-cues picture from Lesson 1 (S-343)
explains *why* a pause is needed. Present these as *skills to practice*, never as a list
of forbidden things — the goal is a learner who acts with care, not fear.

Model the flow once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **ethics** lens: respect online is the same ethical work as
respect offline — truthfulness, kindness, consent — with a new tool in hand. The
**egalitarian** lens: "respectful use" must not become a lecture aimed only at some
learners; everyone posts, everyone is posted about, and everyone's boundaries count
equally. The **technology** lens: read the *defaults* — posts are often public-by-default,
records persist, and audiences are invisible — and notice that respectful use often means
changing a default. The **social-emotional** lens: this topic touches real moments of hurt,
shame, and being left out; invite, never force, and let learners share only what they
choose. The **critical-thinking** lens: "is it true?" means checking before sharing —
rumors and edited images spread fast, and a respectful user does not pass them on
unchecked (S-063). The **global** lens: norms of what is respectful differ across
cultures and platforms; the three gates are a starting place, not a universal law.
Preview: Lesson 3 moves from screens to conflict — how to cool a fire down.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: screens drop tone and face, so the reader guesses.
   That is *why* we pause. Today we learn the pause itself.
2. **Meet the three gates (10 min).** Look at the decision card. Watch a worked example: a
   draft that says "you always ruin everything." Run it through the gates — *is it true?*
   (no — "always" is not true), *is it kind?* (no), *would I want it said about me?* (no).
   Revise it: "I felt hurt when that happened. Can we talk?" Notice what changed.
3. **Learn the consent and privacy check (10 min).** Before sharing anything about another
   person — a photo, a story, a tag — ask yourself: *did I ask? would they want this
   shown?* And for yourself: *who can see this?* Learn that posts are often
   **public-by-default** and that what is posted tends to **persist**, so choosing who can
   see your words and pictures is part of respect (S-127, S-128).
4. **Practice the pause (15 min).** Write a real draft — a message you have wanted to send,
   or one you have seen sent. Run it through the three gates one at a time. Revise it so it
   can pass (or decide it should not be sent at all — sometimes the respectful move is not
   to post).
5. **Draft your rule (10 min).** In your journal, write one personal rule: *before I post,
   I will ___; and I will not share about others without ___.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the person on the other side is a person. A pause, a check,
   and a question are all the respect you need to start with.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-gate version ("true? kind?") and a sentence starter ("Before I
  post I will ___ so that ___.").
- **Extension:** Analyze a platform's default privacy settings and redesign them so the
  *default* is respectful — then argue for your change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner run a draft through the three gates, name the
  consent check, and state one personal rule for respectful use?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The personal "respectful use" rule,
  added to the unit portfolio as the "digital tools used well" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *what is one rule you follow before you share something online —
and why?* Compare your rule with theirs.
