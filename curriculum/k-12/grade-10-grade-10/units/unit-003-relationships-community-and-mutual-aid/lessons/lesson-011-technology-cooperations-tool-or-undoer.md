# Lesson 11 — Technology: Cooperation's Tool or Undoer?

## Summary

Learners **analyze how technology can enable or undermine cooperation and mutual aid
globally**. It can coordinate help across distance, move money to people without banks,
and map crises in real time — yet the same systems can surveil, misinform, and extract.
They close by evaluating one tool and arguing that **design is a choice**, not destiny.

## Objectives

- Analyze how technology can enable or undermine cooperation and mutual aid globally.
  _(D02.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

When a flood hits a town, the same pocket device can do two opposite things. It can be
the thing that saves people: a neighbor posts where help is needed, strangers send money
in minutes, a shared map shows which road is open — people who have never met coordinate
like one body. Or it can be the thing that fails them: rumors spread faster than water,
a company profits from the chaos, and a government watches who is organizing. A tool is
never just a tool — it is a choice someone made about who it serves. Whether your
community faces a flood, a drought, a crisis, or just a hard winter, the question is the
same: *is this technology helping us pull together, or is it pulling us apart — and who
decided?* That question is the whole lesson, and you will ask it of every new tool for
the rest of your life.

## Materials

- Enables vs. undermines two-column sheet
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the difference between charity and mutual aid and
  solidarity (D02.S4.08.02). Today we ask what *technology* does to cooperation and mutual
  aid — for better and for worse.
- Prepare a worked example: one tool that enables and one that undermines.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **technology is
neither cooperation's savior nor its ruin — it can enable mutual aid (coordination,
mobile money, open maps, crowdfunding) and undermine it (surveillance, misinformation,
extractive platforms, algorithmic division), and which way it tips is a design and power
choice, not an accident.** The enabling cases are well documented: mobile money grew where
bank branches were scarce, letting people send, save, and pay by phone — M-Pesa, launched
2007 in Kenya, is the canonical example (S-210, S-211); open maps and crisis coordination
let strangers organize aid in real time. The undermining cases are equally documented:
surveillance chills organizing, misinformation spreads faster than correction, and
platform power can extract from the very people it claims to serve (S-481, S-281). Present
both sides as *facts to weigh*, not a balanced "both sides" that dodges the question —
some tools, in some hands, do more of one than the other.

Model one enable and one undermine, then let learners work (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **technology** lens is the spine: read the tool
as a *designed* object — every feature was chosen by someone, for some purpose. The
**ethics** lens: ask *who does this serve, and who does it watch or extract from?* The
**egalitarian** lens is essential: cooperation tools are not equally available — the
digital divide means the people who most need mutual aid may be the least connected (S-281),
and the same platform that helps a diaspora send money home can charge the poorest the
most. The **global** lens: the same app means different things in a rich city and a rural
village, and in a democracy and under surveillance. The **critical-thinking** lens: for
each claim about a tool's good, ask *for whom, and at what cost?* Preview: Lesson 12
grounds this in concrete examples of cooperative economies and mutual-aid networks across
the world.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between charity and mutual
   aid? (Charity keeps power with the giver; mutual aid meets needs *together*, as equals.)
   Today we ask what technology does to mutual aid.
2. **Meet the two columns (10 min).** Look at the sheet. Watch a worked example. **Enables:**
   a phone coordinates neighbors after a flood; mobile money lets a worker without a bank
   send help home in minutes (S-210, S-211); an open map shows where the need is.
   **Undermines:** surveillance watches who organizes; misinformation spreads faster than
   the truth; a platform profits from people's desperation. Notice: the *same tool* can do
   both.
3. **Evaluate one tool (15 min).** Choose a tool you know — a messaging app, a money app, a
   map, a crowdfunding site, a social network. Fill the two columns: how does it *enable*
   cooperation and mutual aid, and how does it *undermine* them? Be specific about who
   gains and who loses.
4. **Ask the sharp questions (10 min).** For each side, ask: *who designed this, and for
   whom? who is left out (the unconnected, the watched, the poor)? what would a caring
   design change?* Write one sentence answering each.
5. **Draft your verdict (10 min).** In your journal: *on balance, this tool ___ cooperation
   and mutual aid because ___. But design is a choice — here is one change that would tip it
   toward cooperation: ___.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: no tool is neutral. Whether it pulls us together or pulls us
   apart is decided by people — including, one day, you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a short feature list to sort into "enables" and "undermines" and a
  sentence starter ("This tool helps people help each other by ___, but it also ___.").
- **Extension:** Compare mobile money's promise for people without banks against its fees
  and surveillance risks, and argue how to keep the good while checking the harm.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one way technology enables and one way it
  undermines cooperation and mutual aid, evaluate one tool, and argue that design is a
  choice?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The enable-vs-undermine evaluation,
  added to the unit portfolio as the "technology and mutual aid" analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *has a phone or app ever helped people in our community help each
other — and has one ever gotten in the way?* Compare your two columns.
