# Lesson 5 — Seeing a Community from the Inside

## Summary

Learners distinguish **looking at a community from the outside** — through distance,
labels, and assumptions — from **understanding it from within** — through its own
history, values, and first-person voices. They practice the inside posture: ask, listen,
and let the community name itself, rather than assume. They close by choosing a community
to study respectfully in Lesson 6.

## Objectives

- Practice cross-cultural empathy by studying a community's history and values from
  within — beginning with the *posture* of "from within." _(D02.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Think of a group *you* belong to — your family, your neighborhood, your faith or team or
town — and imagine how an outsider who has only seen it in one news clip might describe
it: a single label, a single story, flattened. Now think of how *you* would describe it,
in your own words: the history, the jokes, the values, the things that matter that no
outsider sees. The difference between those two descriptions is the whole lesson: empathy
across cultures is not looking at a community through a telescope — it is sitting inside
the circle, asking questions, and letting the community tell its own story. Whether the
community is across the world or across your town, this is how you come to understand a
people *as they understand themselves* — and it is a skill for life.

## Materials

- Outside view vs. inside view diagram
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-lens diagram.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall empathy across difference — seeking to understand views
  unlike your own (D02.S2.09.01). Today we sharpen it: *whose* account counts, and how do
  we get it?
- Prepare a worked example: one outside account and one inside account of the same
  community, side by side.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **"from within" is a
posture — seek the community's own history, values, and voices, and treat outside labels
as guesses to check, not facts.** The anchor is the distinction between studying *at* a
distance and studying *with and from* a community's own accounts (its members' words,
art, institutions, and histories). This is a value and a method, not a scientific claim:
the **egalitarian** commitment is that a community is the authority on itself, and that
respectful study centers first-person voices rather than repeating stereotypes (which
research shows can narrow how we see whole groups of people — S-226). Present this as a
*skill and a commitment*, and be honest that no outsider ever fully "gets inside" another
person's world — the goal is humility, not mastery.

Model the contrast once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: this is how we meet other
peoples and times without flattening them. The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *who made
this account, and for whom?* — an outside report and an inside account answer different
questions. The **ethics** lens: respectful study asks permission, gives credit, and does
not extract a story to use it; studying *from within* means the community's dignity stays
intact. The **egalitarian** lens: whose version of a community gets repeated is a question
of power — centering inside voices is a small act of fairness. The **social-emotional**
lens: empathy across difference can stir discomfort and humility; invite, never force,
and let learners share only what they choose. Preview: Lesson 6 turns the posture into a
plan — studying one community's history and values in its own words.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what does it mean to seek understanding across
   difference? (To learn how someone unlike me sees the world.) Today we sharpen the
   question: *whose* account do we trust, and how do we get it?
2. **Meet the two lenses (10 min).** Look at the diagram. The **outside lens** sees from a
   distance and reaches for labels — a single story, a single word, "those people are
   like ___." The **inside lens** sits in the circle and asks — *how do you tell your own
   history? what do you value? what would you want me to understand?* Watch a worked
   example: an outside line about a community versus the community's own words about
   itself. Notice what the inside account carries that the outside one missed.
3. **Feel the difference on your own community (10 min).** Take a community *you* belong
   to. Write one outside-style label an outsider might use, then write two sentences in
   your own words about what that community actually is. Compare. Which one is true?
4. **Learn the inside posture (10 min).** The inside posture has three moves: **seek
   first-person voices** (the community's own words, art, and stories); **ask, don't
   assume** (check your guesses against what members actually say); and **let the
   community name itself** (use its own names and meanings, not yours). Learn each move
   with a small example.
5. **Choose a community to study (12 min).** Pick a community you can study respectfully —
   one whose own voices you can reach (through people, books, recordings, or media the
   community makes). It may be near or far. Write: *I will study ___ from within by
   seeking these first-person sources: ___.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: you will never fully stand inside another person's world —
   and that is the point. The goal is not mastery but humility: to ask, to listen, and to
   let people name themselves.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a fill-in prompt ("One outside label is ___, but the community's own
  words say ___.") and a short list of possible communities to study.
- **Extension:** Analyze who controls a community's public story (who owns the media, who
  is quoted) and argue why first-person voices change the account.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state the difference between the outside and
  inside lenses, name the three moves of the inside posture, and choose a community to
  study?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The chosen-community and
  first-person-sources plan, added to the unit portfolio as the start of the empathy study.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *what is one thing outsiders usually get wrong about a community
we belong to — and what is the real story?* Write their answer as a first-person source.
