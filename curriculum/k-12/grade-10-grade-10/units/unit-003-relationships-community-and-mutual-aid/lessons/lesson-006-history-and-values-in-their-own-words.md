# Lesson 6 — History and Values, in Their Own Words

## Summary

Learners carry out a **cross-cultural empathy study from within**: they gather a
community's own **history, values, art and stories, and institutions** from
**first-person sources**, ask permission and give credit, and **check their own
assumptions**. They close with a short account of the community's history and values in
the community's own words.

## Objectives

- Practice cross-cultural empathy by studying a community's history and values from
  within — carrying out the study itself. _(D02.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

When you want to truly understand a people, the best sources are the ones they made
themselves: an elder's story, a song, a founding memory, the reason a holiday matters, a
rule the community lives by. These are not decorations around a "real" history written
by someone else — they *are* the history, told by the people who live it. Gathering them
takes care: you ask before you record, you listen more than you talk, you give credit,
and you let the words stand as they were said. Whether you study a community across the
world or the one next door, this is how empathy becomes real — not by imagining what
others feel, but by listening to what they actually say.

## Materials

- Study-from-within organizer
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the center-and-spokes organizer.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, recall the inside posture — seek first-person voices, ask
  don't assume, let the community name itself. Today we fill the organizer with what those
  voices say.
- Prepare a worked example of one spoke, filled from a first-person source.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the study itself is
simple and repeatable — gather the community's own history, values, art and stories, and
institutions from first-person sources, with consent and credit, and check your own
assumptions as you go.** This is a method and a value, not a scientific claim: the
**egalitarian** commitment is that a community is the authority on itself, and respectful
study centers its own voices rather than repeating stereotypes (S-226 documents how
repeated narrow images can flatten how whole groups are seen). The **ethics** of
representation are real and teachable: ask permission before recording or quoting, give
credit, and do not turn someone's story into a trophy — study *with*, not *on*. Be honest
that "from within" is always approximate: even the most careful outsider stays partly
outside, so the posture is humility, not ownership.

Model one spoke once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: this is the discipline of
meeting other peoples and times without flattening them. The **critical-thinking** lens:
ask of every source *who made it and for whom*, and check where your own assumption has
been filling a gap the community never said. The **egalitarian** lens: whose version of a
community gets repeated is a question of power — citing inside voices is a small act of
fairness. The **social-emotional** lens: sitting with another community's history can
stir discomfort, admiration, and humility; invite, never force, and let learners share
only what they choose. Note for the facilitator: if a learner's chosen community is
Indigenous or otherwise marginalized, be especially careful to steer them to the
community's own sources and to treat consent and attribution as non-negotiable. Preview:
with empathy in hand, Lessons 7–8 ask what digital platforms do to empathy itself.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: what are the three moves of the inside posture?
   (Seek first-person voices; ask, don't assume; let the community name itself.) Today we
   put them to work on one community.
2. **Meet the organizer (10 min).** Look at the diagram. In the center: **the community's
   own words.** Around it, four spokes to fill from first-person sources: **its history**
   (how members tell their own past), **its values** (what the community says matters),
   **its art and stories** (songs, tales, images it makes), and **its institutions** (the
   groups and practices it keeps). Watch a worked example: one spoke filled from a real
   first-person source.
3. **Learn the care rules (8 min).** Before gathering: **ask permission** before recording
   or quoting; **listen more than you talk**; **give credit** (name the person or source);
   and **check your assumptions** — if you think "they must ___," find out whether the
   community actually says so, and if not, let the gap stand.
4. **Gather (20 min).** Work on your chosen community. Fill as many spokes as you can from
   first-person sources you can reach now — a person you can ask (respectfully), or a book,
   recording, song, or media the community made. Write each source's own words down
   faithfully, with credit.
5. **Draft your account (8 min).** Write a short "history and values, in their own words"
   paragraph: what this community says about its own past, and what it says it values —
   in its words, not yours.
6. **Close (4 min).** Remember: you have not "figured out" a people — you have listened.
   That listening, done with care, is the whole skill.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer two spokes to fill and a sentence starter ("In their own words, this
  community's history is ___, and they value ___.").
- **Extension:** Compare two first-person accounts of the same community and explain what
  each emphasizes, and what your own assumption would have gotten wrong.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner fill at least two spokes from first-person sources,
  with consent and credit, and state one assumption they checked?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "history and values, in their own
  words" account, added to the unit portfolio as the cross-cultural empathy study.

## Home connection

Ask a willing elder or neighbor in your own community: *how would you tell our community's
history, and what do we value?* Write their words down with their permission and credit.
