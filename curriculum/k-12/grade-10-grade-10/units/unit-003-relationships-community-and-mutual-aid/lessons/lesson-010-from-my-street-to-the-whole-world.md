# Lesson 10 — From My Street to the Whole World

## Summary

Learners **explain how local communities connect to regional, national, and global
ones**. They trace one everyday thing — a good, a river, a song, a person — across all
four nested scales and name who benefits and who is left out at each. They close by
mapping their own place in the nested whole.

## Objectives

- Explain how local communities connect to regional, national, and global ones.
  _(D02.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

Look at one ordinary thing near you — the fruit on a market stall, the shirt you are
wearing, the water in a tap, the song on the radio. Each one has a journey: the fruit was
grown somewhere, carried along a road, sold in a town, priced in a currency a nation
prints, and traded in a market that reaches across oceans. The river that waters a field
crosses from one district into the next and into the sea; the song travels from a
neighbor's voice to a recording heard on another continent. Your street is not a small
island — it is a knot in a net that reaches from your door to the whole world, and the
same net carries goods, people, ideas, and water in every direction. Whether you live in
a village or a megacity, by a coast or in the mountains, learning to trace those threads
is how you see your own place in the wide world — and how you see who the net serves, and
who it leaves behind.

## Materials

- Nested circles map
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four nested rings.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall comparing forms of community across cultures (D02.S3.09.01).
  Today we zoom *out* — not comparing one community to another, but tracing how any one
  community threads into the wider world.
- Prepare a worked example: one item's journey across all four scales.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **communities are nested —
local, regional, national, and global — and goods, people, ideas, and water flow across all
four scales, connecting every street to the wider world while also deciding who benefits and
who is left out.** The concept is well grounded: a nation is an "imagined community" whose
members will never meet most of their co-members, yet hold an image of their communion —
and shared media and institutions bind them (S-415). The concrete threads (goods, water,
people, ideas) make the abstraction holdable: trade routes, watersheds, migration, and
media all cross the very borders people draw (S-281 on how digital and economic flows both
connect and divide). Present "nested communities" as a *useful map*, not a natural law —
where the lines fall is a human choice, drawn differently in different places.

Model one journey once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: trace one thread across all
four scales and see the whole. The **community** lens: ask what holds each ring together —
shared place, shared language and institutions, shared planet. The **egalitarian** lens is
essential: the net does not serve everyone equally — the same supply chain that delivers a
cheap shirt can underpay the worker who made it, and the same border that unites a nation
can split a family — so tracing a thread is also tracing power. The **ethics** lens: once
you see the net, the question becomes *what do I owe the people at the other end of the
thread?* The **environment** lens: water, soil, and climate do not respect national
borders — a river and the air are shared across every ring. The **critical-thinking**
lens: check where the map simplifies — real life crosses scales in ways no neat set of
circles captures. Preview: with the nested map in hand, Lessons 11–12 turn to cooperation
and mutual aid across that whole.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what forms of community have you compared? Today we
   zoom out — not side by side, but *nested*: your street inside a region, inside a nation,
   inside a world.
2. **Meet the nested rings (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Four rings: **local** (your
   neighborhood, school, town), **regional** (the wider area, watershed, market region),
   **national** (the country with its laws, language, and shared institutions — a community
   whose members will never all meet, yet share an image of their communion; S-415), and
   **global** (the planet, its trade, climate, and migration).
3. **Trace one thread (15 min).** Watch a worked example, then choose your own. Pick one
   everyday thing — a fruit, a shirt, a phone, a song, a river, a family member who
   migrated. Trace it: where did it start, how did it cross each ring, where did it end up?
   Write each step with an arrow.
4. **Ask who the net serves (10 min).** For your thread, ask at each ring: *who benefits,
   and who is left out or underpaid?* (The cheap shirt and the worker who made it; the
   river and the people downstream.) Write one sentence about the fairness you can see.
5. **Map your own place (10 min).** In your journal: *I live in ___ (local), which sits in
   ___ (regional), inside ___ (national), connected to the world by ___.* Name one thread
   that ties you to each wider ring.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: your street is a knot in a wide net. Seeing the net is the
   first step to caring about everyone in it — near and far.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a pre-drawn set of four rings and a two-step trace (local → global) with
  a sentence starter ("The ___ I use started in ___ and reached me through ___.").
- **Extension:** Trace a single issue — a river's pollution, a food's price, a festival's
  spread — through all four scales and analyze who holds power at each ring.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner trace one everyday thing across all four nested
  scales, name who benefits and who is left out, and map their own place in the whole?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The nested-circles trace, added to the
  unit portfolio as the "local to global" analysis.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *where does one thing in our home come from, and whose hands
brought it here?* Trace it together from local to global.
