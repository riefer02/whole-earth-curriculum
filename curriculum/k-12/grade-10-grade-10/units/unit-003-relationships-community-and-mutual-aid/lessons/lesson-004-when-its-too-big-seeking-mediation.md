# Lesson 4 — When It's Too Big: Seeking Mediation

## Summary

Learners learn **when a conflict is too big to solve alone** — when safety is at risk,
when power is unequal, when harm repeats, or when the fight is stuck — and **how
mediation works**: a neutral third party who helps both sides be heard and reach a fair
solution both can accept. They practice a mediation in triads and name who they would
ask when a conflict outgrows them.

## Objectives

- Handle conflict through de-escalation and seek mediation when a conflict is too big to
  solve alone — the "seek mediation" half of the standard. _(D02.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Not every fire can be put out by the two people standing in it. Sometimes one person
holds far more power, or the hurt keeps repeating, or everyone is so angry that every
word adds fuel — and asking for help is not weakness, it is judgment. A **mediator** is a
person who stands outside the heat and helps both sides be heard, without taking a side,
until a fair way forward appears. Whether that mediator is a trusted teacher, a family
elder, a community worker, or a trained peer, and whether your conflict is in a home, a
school, a workplace, or a neighborhood, knowing *when to ask* — and *who to ask* — is a
skill that keeps small harms from becoming large ones.

## Materials

- Too-big checklist and mediation triangle diagram
- Role-play scenario cards (mediation)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the checklist and the mediation triangle.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall the de-escalation ramp. Today we ask: what if the ramp
  is not enough — how do I know, and what then?
- Prepare the scenario cards and a worked example of a mediation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **de-escalation is the
first move, but some conflicts are too big to solve alone — and seeking mediation is a
mature, skilled choice, not a failure.** The "too big" signs to teach are concrete:
**safety** (someone could be hurt), **power imbalance** (one side cannot speak freely),
**repeated harm** (it keeps happening), and **stuck pattern** (the same fight loops with
no resolution). Mediation is a structured process: a **neutral** third party lets each
side speak and be restated, then helps them find a fair solution both accept (S-022,
S-148). The restorative frame adds: the goal is to repair harm and restore relationship,
not only to punish (S-022, S-161).

Model the process once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **social-emotional** lens is the spine: naming "this is too
big for me" requires honest self-awareness, and the topic can touch real hurt — invite,
never force, and let learners share only what they choose. The **ethics** lens: seeking
mediation is an ethical act — it protects the more vulnerable side and refuses to let harm
go unaddressed. The **egalitarian** lens is essential: when power is unequal, "just talk it
out" can itself be unfair, because the less powerful person cannot speak freely — that is
precisely when a neutral third party matters most. The **critical-thinking** lens: learn
to read the *signs* — not every disagreement is too big, and not every "too big" is
obvious at first. The **global** lens: every culture has its own forms of third-party
peacemaking — elders, councils, restorative circles — and mediation is one widely shared
form among many. Preview: with conflict skills in hand, the unit turns to empathy — first
across cultures (Lessons 5–6).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, what is the ramp? (Breathe, lower voice, listen,
   name the feeling, find common ground.) Now ask honestly: are there conflicts where the
   ramp is *not enough*? What would make that true?
2. **Meet the too-big checklist (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Learn the four signs:
   **safety** (someone could be hurt), **power imbalance** (one side cannot speak freely),
   **repeated harm** (it keeps happening), and **stuck pattern** (the same fight loops). For
   each, name a real or imagined example.
3. **Meet the mediation triangle (10 min).** Watch a worked example: two people who keep
   fighting over the same thing, and a neutral mediator. The mediator does not decide who
   is right. They let each side speak, **restate** each side fairly, and ask: *what would
   make this right — for both of you?* The goal is a fair solution both accept, and repair
   of the harm, not just punishment (S-022, S-161).
4. **Practice a mediation (15 min).** In a triad, take a scenario card — unequal power,
   repeated harm, or a stuck fight. Rotate the three roles: the two parties and the
   mediator. The mediator listens, restates each side, stays neutral, and helps them reach
   a fair next step.
5. **Name who you would ask (10 min).** In your journal: *one conflict that is (or was)
   too big for me to solve alone is ___, and a person I could ask is ___, because ___.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: asking for a mediator is not losing — it is how adults,
   communities, and whole nations handle their hardest conflicts. Knowing when to ask is
   strength.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-sign checklist ("someone could be hurt" and "it keeps
  happening") and a sentence starter ("This is too big because ___, so I will ask ___.").
- **Extension:** Analyze the difference between mediation, where the parties decide, and a
  ruling, where someone decides *for* them — and argue when each fits.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + performance):** Can the learner name the four "too big" signs,
  demonstrate the mediator's moves (listen, restate, find a fair solution), and name who
  they would ask?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "too big / who to ask" journal
  entry, added to the unit portfolio as the mediation reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *has there ever been a conflict too big for you to solve alone —
and who helped?* Compare their story with your "who to ask."
