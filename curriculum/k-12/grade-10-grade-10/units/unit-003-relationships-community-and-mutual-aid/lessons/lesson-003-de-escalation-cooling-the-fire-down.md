# Lesson 3 — De-escalation: Cooling the Fire Down

## Summary

Learners learn **de-escalation** as a practiced skill: notice when a conflict is heating
up, **regulate their own body first**, then use a calm voice, real listening, naming the
feeling without blame, and finding one point of agreement. They practice on a low-stakes
scenario and reflect on their own pattern — the fuel they add and the water they could
pour instead.

## Objectives

- Handle conflict through de-escalation — the "cool it down" half of the standard.
  _(D02.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Think of a disagreement that suddenly got loud — over a shared chore, a borrowed thing,
a misread message. Somebody's voice rose, somebody's shoulders went up, and in a minute
two people who like each other were saying things they did not mean. That climb — from a
small spark to a full fire — is **escalation**, and it has a shape we can learn to read
early. The same moment, handled another way, can go the other direction: a slow breath, a
lowered voice, a real listen, and the fire cools instead. Whether your conflicts happen
in a kitchen, a classroom, a market, or a group chat, this is a skill you will use in
every relationship for the rest of your life — and the person you can always start with
is yourself.

## Materials

- Escalation ladder / de-escalation ramp diagram
- Role-play scenario cards
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the ladder-and-ramp diagram.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall assertive (not passive or aggressive) communication and
  the repair steps of apologize, listen, agree (D02.S1.08.02). Today we go one step
  earlier — *before* the hurt needs repair, how do we keep a spark from becoming a fire?
- Prepare the role-play scenario cards and a worked example of the ramp.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **escalation is a
climb we can read — and de-escalation is a set of teachable moves: regulate yourself
first, lower your voice, slow the pace, listen, name the feeling without blame, and find
one point of agreement.** The skill is well grounded: self-regulation and the "I-statement"
habit (saying how *I* feel rather than blaming) are core conflict-resolution practices
(S-148, S-001), and restorative practice frames conflict as something to listen through
and repair rather than win (S-022). The physiology note is honest and hedged: a heated
conflict raises arousal — heart and breath quicken — and calming the body first (a slow
exhale) makes the calm voice possible; this is descriptive science, not a verdict.

Model the ramp once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **social-emotional** lens is the spine: de-escalation
begins with noticing *your own* body and feelings, and this topic can touch real hurts —
invite, never force, and let learners share only what they choose. The **ethics** lens:
de-escalation is not "letting someone walk over you" — it is refusing to add fuel while
still being honest about your own needs. The **egalitarian** lens: the moves work the same
for everyone, but the stakes are not equal — people who are routinely dismissed or
punished more harshly may feel *less* safe to cool a conflict down, and that is a justice
fact, not a personal failing. The **critical-thinking** lens: name your own pattern — what
is your fuel, and what is your water? The **global** lens: every culture has its own ways
of keeping peace and saving face; these moves are one toolkit among many, held with
respect. Preview: Lesson 4 asks when a conflict is *too big* to cool alone — and who to
ask.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of a conflict that got loud fast. What climbed — the voice,
   the words, the heart? That climb is escalation, and it has a shape. Today we learn to
   read it early and walk it back down.
2. **Meet the ladder and the ramp (10 min).** Look at the diagram. The **ladder** goes up:
   irritation → frustration → anger → blow-up, each rung hotter and harder to step back
   from. The **ramp** comes down: breathe → lower your voice → listen → name the feeling
   → find one point of agreement. Learn each step aloud.
3. **Regulate yourself first (8 min).** Watch a worked example, then try it. Sit quietly and
   notice your body: if a conflict were starting now, where would the heat be — chest,
   jaw, shoulders, stomach? Take one slow breath in, and a longer, slower breath out. This
   is step one of the ramp: you cannot pour water from an empty, boiling pot.
4. **Practice the ramp (15 min).** With a partner, take a scenario card — a group project
   where one person did not do their part, a borrowed item returned broken, a text read as
   rude. One of you is upset; the other practices the ramp: lower your voice, slow down,
   listen and restate, name the feeling without blame ("I felt ___ when ___"), and find one
   thing you both agree on. Then swap roles.
5. **Name your own pattern (12 min).** In your journal: *my fuel is ___ (what makes me
   climb fast), and my water is ___ (the move that cools me).* Write one sentence about a
   real moment you could have used the ramp.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: de-escalation is not weakness — it is the strong, skilled
   choice. You get to decide how a spark goes.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-move version ("breathe, then listen") and a sentence starter
  ("When I feel ___ I will ___ before I speak.").
- **Extension:** Explain, in your own words, why calming the body first makes the calm
  voice possible — and what could still make a conflict too big to cool alone.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + performance):** Can the learner name the ladder's rungs, demonstrate
  two ramp moves in role-play, and state their own fuel and water?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "fuel and water" journal entry,
  added to the unit portfolio as the de-escalation reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *when you feel a conflict heating up, what do you do to cool
yourself down first?* Compare your fuel-and-water with theirs.
