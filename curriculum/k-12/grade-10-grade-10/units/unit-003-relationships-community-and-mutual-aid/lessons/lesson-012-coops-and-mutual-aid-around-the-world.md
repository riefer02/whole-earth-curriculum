# Lesson 12 — Co-ops and Mutual Aid Around the World

## Summary

Learners **examine cooperative economies and mutual-aid networks across the world**. They
distinguish **charity** (a one-way gift, power kept by the giver) from **mutual aid**
(equals deciding together) and **cooperative ownership** (shared ownership, shared
benefit), and meet real examples — communal-work traditions, credit unions, and worker
cooperatives. They close by naming one cooperative or mutual-aid effort they would join or
build.

## Objectives

- Examine examples of cooperative economies and mutual-aid networks across the world.
  _(D02.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

A community can meet a need in three very different ways. It can *give*: a wealthy person
hands food down, the giver keeping the power and the receiver staying passive — that is
**charity**. It can *share*: neighbors pool what they have and decide together who needs
what, no one above anyone — that is **mutual aid**. Or it can *own together*: workers run
their own workshop, families share a savings fund, a village runs its own water system —
that is a **cooperative economy**, where the people who use or make a thing also own and
decide it. The difference is not in how much is given, but in *who holds the power and who
decides*. Whether your community is a village, a city block, or a network across
continents, these three ways of caring are everywhere — and learning to tell them apart,
and to see them working, is how you come to see that the economy is not a force of nature
but something people build, together.

## Materials

- Charity vs. mutual aid vs. cooperative diagram
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-panel spectrum.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the charity-vs-mutual-aid distinction (D02.S4.08.02); from
  Grade 5, recall examples of mutual aid from different cultures and times (D02.S4.05.01).
  Today we go deeper: naming *cooperative ownership* as a third way, with world examples.
- Prepare a worked example of each panel.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **cooperation and mutual
aid are not marginal — they are widespread, documented ways people meet needs together,
and the key distinction is power: charity keeps it with the giver, mutual aid shares it
among equals, and cooperative ownership ties it to shared ownership and benefit.** The
anchors are sound. Mutual aid — cooperation and reciprocity born of belonging, not pay — is
a widespread human practice, named and documented in Kropotkin's classic (S-163). Communal
self-help traditions are real and named: *harambee* ("all pull together") in Kenya,
*gotong royong* in Indonesia, *mink'a/minga* in the Andes, and barn raising in North
America (S-164). The charity-vs-solidarity distinction — charity as a one-way gift in
which the giver keeps power, mutual aid as people meeting needs together as equals — is
the standard framing (S-345). Worker cooperatives (such as the Mondragón federation in the
Basque Country) and credit unions are well-known cooperative forms; describe them as
*widely documented examples* and invite learners to check first-party detail (the
International Cooperative Alliance and Mondragón are the primary references — see
Resources). Hold "sharing power is better" as a *value*, distinct from the descriptive
claim that these forms exist and work.

Model each panel once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **egalitarian** lens is the spine: the whole point of
mutual aid and cooperation is that no one is above and no one is left out — and asking
*who decides* is how you tell the forms apart. The **ethics** lens: charity is not
"bad" — it can save lives — but it keeps the receiver dependent, while mutual aid and
cooperation build dignity and power. The **global** lens: these forms appear across
cultures and times, each shaped by its own place — hold them side by side, never ranked.
The **community** lens: ask what each form builds — dependency, solidarity, or ownership.
The **critical-thinking** lens: not every group that calls itself a "co-op" shares power
fairly — check who actually decides and who actually benefits. Note: this is the unit's
closing lesson — invite learners to gather the thread back to Lesson 1, where respectful
communication began the whole arc.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is the difference between charity and mutual
   aid? (Charity keeps power with the giver; mutual aid meets needs together, as equals.)
   Today we add a third way and meet real examples.
2. **Meet the three ways (12 min).** Look at the diagram. **Charity:** a one-way gift —
   the giver keeps the power, the receiver stays passive. **Mutual aid:** equals decide
   together who needs what — no one above anyone (S-345). **Cooperative economy:** the
   people who use or make a thing own it and decide it together — shared ownership, shared
   benefit. Watch a worked example of each.
3. **Meet world examples (12 min).** Learn real, named forms. **Communal work:** *harambee*
   in Kenya, *gotong royong* in Indonesia, *mink'a/minga* in the Andes, barn raising in
   North America (S-164). **Cooperative ownership:** worker cooperatives such as the
   Mondragón federation in the Basque Country, and **credit unions** — savings and lending
   owned by their members. These are not rare — cooperation is a widespread human practice
   (S-163).
4. **Match and check (10 min).** Take three needs — food, money, work — and for each, name
   how *charity*, *mutual aid*, and a *cooperative* would meet it, and who would decide.
   Notice where the power sits in each.
5. **Choose yours (10 min).** In your journal: *one cooperative or mutual-aid effort I
   would join — or help build — is ___, because it would meet ___ while keeping power with
   ___.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the economy is not weather — it is something people build.
   Every cooperative and every mutual-aid network began when ordinary people decided to
   share the deciding. That option is yours too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a three-row table (need → charity / mutual aid / cooperative) with a
  sentence starter ("A ___ would meet ___ while keeping power with ___.").
- **Extension:** Compare a worker cooperative with a conventional firm and argue how
  ownership changes who decides and who gains — then name one real cooperative and check
  your account against its own description.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner distinguish charity, mutual aid, and cooperative
  ownership, name at least two real world examples, and state one effort they would join or
  build?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The "one I would join or build" entry,
  added to the unit portfolio as the cooperative-economies-and-mutual-aid analysis — and
  the closing artifact of the unit's portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *have you ever been part of something where people shared the work
and the deciding — a fund, a farm, a building project, a neighborhood group?* Write down
their story.
