# Lesson 7 — Poverty and Responsibility: Competing Answers

## Summary

Learners analyze a global justice question — **who bears responsibility for poverty** — through three
traditions of justice: **capabilities (Sen), fairness (Rawls), and ubuntu**. They compare what each
tradition counts as "responsibility," note where they agree and differ, and write their own reasoned
position.

## Objectives

- Analyze a global justice question about poverty, and weigh competing answers from more than one
  tradition of justice. _(D09.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Poverty is not one thing and has no single cause. Where someone is born, what work and pay are
available, inherited wealth, health, and conflict all shape who has enough and who does not — and
these are **structural** conditions, not measures of a person's worth (S-234, S-283). Because the
causes are structural, the question of responsibility is too: does the responsibility to end poverty
rest on individuals, on governments, on the global order, or on all of us together? Different
traditions of justice answer differently — and each answer shows something the others miss. Today we
hold three of them side by side.

## Materials

- Competing answers sheet
- Poverty case study

## Preparation

- Copy the competing answers sheet and the case study.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall the four candidate answers about climate responsibility. Today we
  meet a *different* family of answers — whole traditions of justice, each with its own idea of
  responsibility.
- Prepare the three traditions and one worked example of comparing two of them.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **different traditions of justice —
not only Western ones — give different, defensible answers to "who is responsible for poverty," and
weighing them is the skill.** Present each tradition fairly and briefly: **capabilities** (Sen) asks
whether people are actually *able* to do and be the things that make a life worth living — so
responsibility falls on whoever can expand those freedoms (S-460); **fairness** (Rawls) asks what
rules we would choose behind a "veil of ignorance" of our own position, so responsibility falls on
arrangements that would be chosen by someone who did not know if they would be rich or poor (S-459);
**ubuntu** (a Southern African ethic, often glossed "I am because we are") holds that a person's
humanity is bound up with others', so poverty is everyone's concern and no one is fully themselves
while another is left out. The **global** lens is the spine: this is the lesson where the curriculum
most deliberately de-centers any single tradition — ubuntu, Confucian role ethics, Buddhist
consequentialism, and dharma-based duty all carry ideas of justice as old and as serious as the
Western canon (S-465, S-468, S-467, S-469). The **egalitarian** lens: every tradition here is being
used to ask who is left out. The **community** lens: ubuntu names belonging itself as a good. Keep
all three as *questions and frameworks*, not as the one correct answer (philosophy §11); distinguish
the documented structural drivers of poverty (S-234) from the values each tradition adds (philosophy
§5). Preview: next we turn to technology, data, and the right to privacy.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: name one candidate answer about climate responsibility. Today
   the question is poverty — what is different about it?
2. **Meet the case (10 min).** Read the poverty case study. Underline the structural drivers — where
   the person was born, work and pay, health, conflict. Note: none of these is a measure of worth.
3. **Meet the three traditions (15 min).** Read the competing answers sheet:
   - **Capabilities (Sen)** — responsibility falls on whoever can expand what people are actually able
     to do and be.
   - **Fairness (Rawls)** — responsibility falls on rules that would be chosen by someone who did not
     know if they would be rich or poor.
   - **Ubuntu** — "I am because we are"; no one is fully themselves while another is left out, so
     poverty is everyone's concern.
   For each, write one sentence: how would this tradition answer "who is responsible?"
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, compare two traditions on the case: where do they agree,
   where do they differ, and which answer do you find more convincing — and why?
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your reasoned position: which tradition
   (or blend) you lean toward, one strength, one limit, and one thing any fair answer must not
   ignore.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share in a small group. Notice: the traditions are not a quiz with a right
   answer — they are *languages* for asking the same question.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a one-line plain-English gloss of each tradition and sentence starters for the
  comparison.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth tradition from your own family or culture and compare it with the
  three.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write or draw each tradition's answer, sort
  slips under the three traditions, or have a partner read their written position — no step requires
  the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state two traditions' answers, give a strength and a
  limit of each, and defend a position?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The four-sentence position, added to the "global justice" section.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: whose responsibility is it when a family has too little — the family, the
community, the government, or all of us? Compare with the three traditions.
