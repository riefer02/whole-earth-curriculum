# Lesson 2 — Weighing a Technology: The Phone in My Hand

## Summary

Learners **weigh the benefits and harms of a technology they use daily — the phone** — across its
full lifecycle: minerals, manufacture, use, and disposal. A **worked example** models the weighing
skill, then learners apply it stage by stage and write their own reasoned judgment.

## Objectives

- Apply ethical reasoning to a technology — weighing benefits and harms for people and the planet —
  and defend a reasoned judgment. _(D09.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

The phone in your pocket can call a friend across the sea, find a health clinic, move money, and
read a schoolbook — in a single hour it can do work that once took a journey of days. But that same
phone began as rock dug from the ground, was assembled in a factory far away, and will one day be
thrown away. Some of the people who dug that rock or sorted that waste worked in conditions that
hurt them. A phone is not simply "good" or "bad": it is a **trade-off**, and weighing it honestly —
benefit against harm, near against far — is exactly what ethical reasoning about technology means.

## Materials

- Phone lifecycle sheet
- Worked-example card

## Preparation

- Copy the lifecycle sheet and worked-example card.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall that every tool has effects — who benefits, who is harmed (the
  phone's life cycle; e-waste and cobalt). Today we weigh those effects with more care.
- Prepare one worked example of weighing a single benefit against a single harm.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a technology is never all
benefit or all harm; weighing it means naming benefits and harms for the people near it and the
people far away, across its whole life — then judging which trade-off you can defend.** Teach the
weighing skill with a worked example first, then guided practice, then independent work — novices
learn a procedure this way (S-011). Keep the facts accurate and hedged: cobalt and other minerals in
phones are *sometimes* mined in conditions that harm and exploit workers, including children
(S-398); discarded phones become e-waste, which when improperly dumped or recycled can release
hazardous substances, with children and pregnant women especially vulnerable (S-397). At the same
time, mobile connectivity is real and widely valued — it is how hundreds of millions of people
bank, learn, and stay connected (S-281, S-211). Hold both true at once; do not collapse into a
verdict that a phone is simply evil or simply wonderful. The **egalitarian** lens: always ask who
bears each harm and who keeps each benefit — the distance between them is the point. The **global**
lens: the people harmed by a phone's birth and death live on other continents; the question of
fairness crosses borders. Distinguish evidence (the documented harms) from values (how much each
harm should weigh). Preview: next lesson we weigh an environment, not a device.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what is one benefit and one harm of a mobile phone you already
   know? Say each in a sentence.
2. **Meet the lifecycle (10 min).** Look at the lifecycle sheet — four stages: **minerals**,
   **factory**, **use**, **dispose**. For each stage, read who is touched there.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one weighing done step by step: (a) name the benefit —
   connectivity lets a family stay in touch and a shop take payments; (b) name the harm — some of
   the minerals inside are sometimes mined in conditions that exploit workers, including children;
   (c) say who gains and who loses; (d) judge: is the trade-off worth it, and what would make it
   fairer? This is the pattern you will use.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, weigh one more stage (e.g., **dispose** — e-waste that
   harms soil and the people who sort it, against the phone's long useful life). Use the four steps
   aloud, then agree on a judgment.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, weigh the whole phone in four sentences: one
   benefit, one harm, who bears each, and your reasoned judgment. Mark yourself "not yet" if you
   skipped a step, and try again.
6. **Close (5 min).** Read your judgment to a partner. Notice: two people can weigh the same facts
   and reach different judgments — that is *reasoning*, not failure.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("The benefit is…; the harm is…; who gains is…; who loses
  is…; I judge…").
- **Extension:** Add a second, less visible harm (energy used by data centers; the minerals in the
  battery) and fold it into the weighing.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write or draw each weighing step, arrange
  benefit/harm slips on the lifecycle, or have a partner read their written judgment — no step
  requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name a benefit and a harm, say who bears each, and
  give a reasoned judgment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed weighing (four sentences), as the first page of the
  unit's "ethical-reasoning" portfolio.

## Home connection

With a family member, choose one household object and trace one step of where it came from and where
it will go. Name one benefit and one harm together.
