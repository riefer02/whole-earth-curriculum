# Lesson 12 — Civic Technology and Digital Citizenship

## Summary

Learners analyze the ethics of **civic technology and digital citizenship** through three forces —
**misinformation, surveillance, and inclusion/exclusion**. They apply media-literacy questions to
real cases, weigh how digital tools both help and harm public life, and name what a responsible
digital citizen does.

## Objectives

- Analyze the ethics of civic technology and digital citizenship, including misinformation,
  surveillance, and who is included or excluded. _(D09.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

A citizen today can organize a protest from a phone, check a fact in seconds, and vote in a poll
without leaving home. The same tools can also flood a public square with false stories, watch people
without their knowledge, and leave whole communities out because they cannot afford a connection.
Being a digital citizen is not just *using* these tools; it is asking, about every message and every
platform: **who made this, why, for whom, with what evidence, and who is left out?** Today you learn
to ask those questions about the tools themselves.

## Materials

- Civic tech case sheet
- Digital citizenship lens card

## Preparation

- Copy the case sheet and lens card.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8 and 9, recall that digital tools change civic participation, with risks and
  benefits (organizing online, misinformation, surveillance). Today we deepen that into an ethics
  analysis.
- Prepare one worked example of applying the five questions to a message.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **civic technology is not neutral —
it can spread misinformation, enable surveillance, and include or exclude people, and a responsible
digital citizen asks the five media-literacy questions of every message and platform.** Keep the
facts hedged and balanced: digital tools genuinely helped organize protests during the 2010–11 Arab
Spring uprisings, while the same tools also spread misinformation and enabled surveillance (S-391);
synthetic media can now make it hard to tell real from false (S-393); and access to the internet is
uneven by cost, geography, and disability (S-281). Present these as documented, weighed risks and
benefits, not a simple verdict that technology is good or bad. The **technology** lens is the spine.
The **egalitarian** lens: ask who is excluded — the digital divide means the loudest voices online
are not the whole public. The **ethics** lens: the same act (sharing a post) can help or harm, so
intent and care matter. The **global** lens: citizenship and its digital tools look different in
every country and tradition — none is the default. Use the five questions from media literacy: who
made this, why, for whom, with what evidence, what is left out (S-063, S-151). Keep questions open
(philosophy §11); distinguish what a platform *does* (evidence) from what a citizen *should* do
(values) (philosophy §5). Preview: next we turn to repairing a specific harm — designing a
restorative process.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8/9: name one benefit and one risk of organizing online. Say each
   in a sentence.
2. **Meet the three forces (10 min).** Read the case sheet: **misinformation**, **surveillance**,
   **inclusion/exclusion** — each with a short example. Underline who is affected in each.
3. **Meet the lens card (10 min).** Read the five questions: **who made this, why, for whom, with
   what evidence, what is left out?** Watch one worked example of applying them to a viral claim.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, choose one force and analyze its example with the five
   questions. Then weigh: how does this force help public life, and how does it harm it? Swap and
   compare.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write one paragraph on each force — one
   sentence on how it works, one on who is affected, one on what a responsible digital citizen does
   about it. Mark "not yet" if you skipped a force.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one thing a responsible digital citizen should *always* do. Notice how
   the same five questions recur across all three forces.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the five questions as sentence starters and a half-analyzed example to finish.
- **Extension:** Trace how the three forces interact on a single platform you use, and argue for one
  concrete change that would make it fairer.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write answers to the five questions, sort
  example slips by force, or have a partner read their written analysis — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner apply the five media-literacy questions to a message,
  and name how misinformation, surveillance, and inclusion/exclusion each affect public life?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-paragraph analysis, as the "civic technology" page of the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

With a family member, take one message from a group chat or feed and ask the five questions together.
What did you notice that you had not before?
