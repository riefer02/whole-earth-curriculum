# Lesson 3 — Weighing an Environment: Energy and Trade-Offs

## Summary

Learners apply the **weighing skill** to an environment question — energy and land use — where every
option carries a cost. They weigh a dam, a wind farm, and a standing forest, naming benefits and
harms for people and the planet, then write a reasoned judgment about one choice.

## Objectives

- Apply ethical reasoning to an environment question — weighing benefits and harms for people and
  the planet — and defend a reasoned judgment. _(D09.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Every source of energy is also a use of land and water, and every use of land changes who and what
can live there. A dam can bring electricity to a whole region but floods a valley where people farmed
and fish spawned; a wind farm turns the wind into power but can cut through a bird's migration path;
a forest left standing stores carbon and holds soil but produces no electricity at all. There is no
option with zero cost — only trade-offs to be seen clearly and weighed honestly. That is what it
means to reason about the environment: not to find a perfect answer, but to see the real costs and
decide which ones you can defend.

## Materials

- Energy trade-off sheet
- Three short case cards (dam, wind farm, forest)

## Preparation

- Copy the trade-off sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the four weighing steps — name the benefit, name the harm, say who
  gains and who loses, judge. Today we use the same steps on an environment choice.
- Prepare one worked example of weighing a single environment trade-off.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an environment or energy choice is
never cost-free; weighing it means naming benefits and harms for people *and* for other living
systems, then defending a judgment.** Reuse the four steps from Lesson 2 (name the benefit; name the
harm; say who gains and who loses; judge), so the skill is spaced and reinforced, not re-taught from
scratch (S-039). Keep the science honest and hedged: burning fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases
that warm the climate (S-005); dams provide power and water but can displace communities and change
rivers; wind and solar avoid most operating emissions but use land and materials. Present these as
documented effects, not verdicts — and note that people disagree about how to weigh them, which is a
question of values, not of fact (philosophy §5). The **egalitarian** lens: the people who bear a
project's harms (a flooded valley, a felled forest, a polluting plant) are often not the same people
who receive its benefits — name that gap. The **global** lens: energy choices are made in one place
but their effects travel (a coal plant warms a climate everywhere). The **environment** lens is the
spine: the "stakeholders" include rivers, forests, birds, and the climate itself. Preview: next we
turn from weighing to critiquing — reading an argument for what it leaves unsaid.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what are the four steps of weighing? Say them in order.
2. **Meet the cases (10 min).** Read the three case cards — a dam, a wind farm, a forest kept
   standing. For each, underline one benefit and one harm for people, and one benefit or harm for
   the planet.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one weighing done step by step for the dam: (a) benefit —
   electricity for many homes and water for crops; (b) harm — a valley is flooded, and people who
   lived there are displaced; (c) who gains and who loses — distant electricity users gain, the
   valley's people and its river lose; (d) judge — is the trade-off justifiable, and what would make
   it fairer?
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, weigh the wind farm using the same four steps aloud.
   Agree on a judgment, then switch and weigh the forest.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, weigh one choice in four sentences: a
   benefit, a harm, who bears each, and your reasoned judgment. Mark yourself "not yet" if you
   skipped a step.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share judgments in a small group. Notice: the same facts can support more than
   one defensible judgment — the skill is *seeing the costs clearly*, not agreeing on one answer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the four-step sentence starters from Lesson 2 and a pre-filled example to
  annotate.
- **Extension:** Weigh the *same* choice for a different stakeholder (a bird, a farmer, a child in
  another country) and say how the judgment shifts.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write the four steps, sort printed benefit/harm
  slips onto the sheet, or have a partner read their written judgment — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name a benefit and a harm for people and for the
  planet, say who bears each, and defend a judgment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The four-sentence environment weighing, added to the ethical-
  reasoning portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask a family member: what in our home uses energy, and where do they think it comes from? Name one
benefit and one harm of that source together.
