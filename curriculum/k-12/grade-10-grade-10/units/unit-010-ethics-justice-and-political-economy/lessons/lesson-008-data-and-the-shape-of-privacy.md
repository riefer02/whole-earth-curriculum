# Lesson 8 — Data and the Shape of Privacy

## Summary

Learners evaluate how technology and data **expand or limit the right to privacy**. Using a spectrum,
they place technologies from "privacy limited" to "privacy expanded," analyze a case where the same
data both helps and exposes people, and argue for where protections are needed.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology and data can expand or limit rights such as privacy, and identify who is
  affected differently. _(D09.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

The same technology can be a door or a window. A phone lets you reach a doctor and send money to a
relative far away — it *expands* what you can do. But the same phone records where you are, what you
search, and whom you speak to — and when that data is collected without your say, or used to watch
you, your privacy *shrinks*. Privacy is not one switch; it is a spectrum, and every tool sits
somewhere on it, often in two places at once. Seeing where — and for whom — is the first step to
arguing about what should be protected.

## Materials

- Privacy spectrum sheet
- Privacy case study

## Preparation

- Copy the spectrum sheet and the case study.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall that human rights are honored or denied unequally depending on
  power and access. Today we apply that to privacy and data.
- Prepare one worked example of placing a technology on the spectrum and explaining why.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **technology and data do not have a
single effect on privacy — they expand it for some and limit it for others, often at once; the skill
is seeing which and for whom.** Frame privacy as a real, recognized right: the Universal Declaration
of Human Rights affirms protection from arbitrary interference with privacy, family, home, and
correspondence (S-149). Keep the facts hedged and non-panicky: data tools genuinely help (mobile
money where banks are scarce, S-211; remote care; connection), and genuinely risk exposure
(surveillance, data that outlives consent). The **technology** lens is the spine. The **egalitarian**
lens: the people whose privacy shrinks are often the least powerful — and the digital divide means
some are excluded from the benefits while others are over-exposed (S-281). The **global** lens:
privacy is understood differently across cultures, and protections differ by law and custom — none is
the default. Distinguish evidence (what data is collected) from values (what should be protected)
(philosophy §5), and hold questions open (philosophy §11). Preview: next lesson we build an argument
for protections.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what does it mean that rights are honored or denied unequally?
   Give one example.
2. **Meet the spectrum (10 min).** Look at the spectrum sheet — a line from **privacy limited** on
   the left to **privacy expanded** on the right. Read the technologies listed and where each might
   sit.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one placement done step by step for a health app: (a) how it
   *expands* privacy — it can give care at home without anyone else in the room; (b) how it *limits*
   privacy — it records your body's data, which may be stored or shared without your say; (c) for
   whom — a rural patient gains care, a monitored worker may lose control of their data. Place it
   mid-spectrum and say why.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, read the case study — a data use that both helps and
   exposes people. Mark where it expands and where it limits privacy, and for whom. Place two more
   technologies on the spectrum and agree.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, choose one technology and write: how it
   expands privacy, how it limits it, for whom, and where on the spectrum it sits. Mark "not yet" if
   you only named one direction.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one placement. Notice how often the same tool sits in two places at once —
   that doubling is the point.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled spectrum (one technology already placed) and sentence starters.
- **Extension:** Find a technology that expands *and* limits privacy for the *same* person, and
  explain the tension.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can place printed technology cards along the
  spectrum, write their reasoning, or have a partner read their written explanation — no step
  requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name how a technology expands and limits privacy, say
  for whom, and place it on the spectrum with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The spectrum placement and explanation, as the first page of the
  "technology and rights" section.

## Home connection

With a family member, list one app or service you use. What does it know about you, and who else can
see it? Ask whether that feels like a door or a window.
