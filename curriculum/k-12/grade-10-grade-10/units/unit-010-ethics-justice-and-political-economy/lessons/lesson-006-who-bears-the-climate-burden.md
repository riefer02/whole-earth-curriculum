# Lesson 6 — Who Bears the Climate Burden?

## Summary

Learners analyze a global justice question — **who bears responsibility for climate harm** — and
weigh four competing answers: **polluter pays, historical responsibility, capability, and common but
differentiated responsibility**. They hold each answer's strengths and limits, then write their own
reasoned position.

## Objectives

- Analyze a global justice question about climate harm, and weigh competing answers about who bears
  responsibility. _(D09.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Climate change does not fall evenly. The places that emitted the most greenhouse gases over the past
two centuries — powering industry, homes, and transport — are often not the places that feel the
worst floods, droughts, and storms now; many of the most exposed places are ones that emitted little
but depend on rain-fed farms and low-lying coasts (S-005, S-006). This is a **justice** question, not
just a science question: when a harm is caused by many people over many generations and felt most by
people who did not cause it, who is responsible, and what do they owe? There is more than one honest
answer, and today we weigh them.

## Materials

- Responsibility framework sheet
- Climate harm case study

## Preparation

- Copy the framework sheet and the case study.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall that justice has more than one theory (equal treatment, need,
  restitution) and that systemic injustice combines actions, rules, and structures. Today we apply
  that to climate harm.
- Prepare the four candidate answers and one worked example of weighing two of them.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **responsibility for climate harm is
a genuine, contested question, and weighing the answers — polluter pays, historical responsibility,
capability, and common but differentiated responsibility — is the skill.** Hold the science as
evidence and the answers as *values*: it is documented that emissions are unequally distributed and
that harms fall hardest on the most exposed (S-005, S-006); *how much* each actor owes is a values
question on which reasonable people disagree. The **egalitarian** lens: always ask who caused it,
who is harmed, and whether the same people are both. The **global** lens is the spine: this is a
question that crosses borders and generations, and the answers are debated in every region, from the
"polluter pays" principle to "common but differentiated responsibilities" in international climate
agreements. The **environment** lens: the "victims" include rivers, reefs, glaciers, and future
generations, not only people alive today. Do not settle the question as dogma (philosophy §11);
distinguish evidence from values (philosophy §5). Preview: next lesson we weigh the same question
for poverty.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: name two different ideas of justice you met (e.g., equal
   treatment, need, restitution). Say each in a sentence.
2. **Meet the case (10 min).** Read the climate harm case study. Underline who contributed, who is
   harmed, and whether they are the same people.
3. **Meet the four answers (15 min).** Read the framework sheet. Four candidate answers to "who is
   responsible?":
   - **Polluter pays** — those who emitted the most should pay the most.
   - **Historical responsibility** — those who emitted most *in the past* owe for the harm their past
     still causes.
   - **Capability** — those most *able* to pay and act should bear the most, whatever they emitted.
   - **Common but differentiated** — everyone shares the duty, but not equally: those who caused more
     and can do more, do more.
   For each, note one strength and one limit.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, weigh two answers against each other for the case: which
   fits best, and what does each leave out? Agree on a tentative answer, then say what would change
   your mind.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your reasoned position in four
   sentences: which answer you lean toward, one strength, one limit, and one thing a fair response
   must not ignore.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share positions in a small group. Notice: different defensible answers can
   coexist — the question is whether you can *defend* yours, not whether everyone agrees.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames for each answer ("The polluter-pays answer says…; its limit
  is…").
- **Extension:** Combine two answers into a hybrid (e.g., historical responsibility + capability) and
  argue for it against the others.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write their position, place a marker beside a
  framework, or have a partner read their written position — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state two competing answers, give a strength and a
  limit of each, and defend a position?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The four-sentence position, as the first page of the "global
  justice" section of the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: if a harm was caused by many people long ago but felt mostly by others today,
who should pay to fix it? Compare answers.
