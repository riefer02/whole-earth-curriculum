# Lesson 4 — Hidden Assumptions: Reading the Unsaid

## Summary

Learners learn to **critique a moral argument** by finding its hidden assumptions and bias — the part
that is said without being said. Using an iceberg image, they separate a stated claim from its
unstated premises, test those premises, and name where bias slips in.

## Objectives

- Critique a moral argument for hidden assumptions or bias, by separating its stated claim from its
  unstated premises. _(D09.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Every argument is like an iceberg: only a little of it is above the water. Above the water is what
the speaker says out loud — "this phone is good for everyone." Below the water is what the speaker
*assumes* without saying it — that everyone can afford a phone, that everyone has a network, that
"good" is measured the same way for everyone. An argument can look complete and still rest on parts
no one ever said out loud. Finding those parts — and asking whether they are true and whether they
are fair — is the heart of reading an argument critically. It is a skill you can use on an
advertisement, a speech, a news report, or your own reasoning.

## Materials

- Argument iceberg sheet
- Two short moral arguments

## Preparation

- Copy the iceberg sheet and the two arguments.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall the difference between a fact, a belief, and a value; and that a
  moral argument has premises and a conclusion. Today we look for the *unstated* premises.
- Prepare one worked example of finding a hidden assumption.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an argument's visible part is
small; its hidden assumptions and bias are the part below the water — find them, test them, and the
argument is no longer invisible.** Teach the skill with a worked example, then guided practice
(S-011). Frame critique as *care*, not attack: we read an argument closely to understand it better,
and to notice who is left out. The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: ask "what must be true
for this to be true, and is it?" The **egalitarian** lens: bias often hides in the question of who
is assumed to matter — "everyone" can silently mean "people like the speaker." The **global** lens:
arguments are shaped by where they are made; an assumption that is obvious in one place is strange in
another, and that difference is information, not a mistake. Keep questions open (philosophy §11) and
distinguish evidence from values (philosophy §5): finding an assumption is a claim about *what the
argument says*; judging whether that assumption is fair is a question of values. Preview: next lesson
we rebuild the argument with the assumptions brought into the light.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what is the difference between a premise and a conclusion? Give
   a one-sentence example.
2. **Meet the iceberg (10 min).** Look at the iceberg sheet. Above the water: the **stated claim**.
   Below the water: the **hidden assumptions** — the things that must be true for the claim to hold.
   Read one short argument together and mark where the waterline is.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one critique done step by step for "Everyone should switch to
   smartphones; they make life better for all of us." (a) What is said? (b) What is assumed? — that
   everyone can afford one; that everyone has a network and the skills to use it; that "better" is
   the same for everyone. (c) Test each assumption: is it true for everyone? (d) Where does bias
   slip in? — the argument speaks for "all of us" while assuming a life like the speaker's.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, take the second argument and repeat the four moves.
   Write down at least two assumptions, then test each one. Swap and compare what you found.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, choose one argument and write: the stated
   claim, two hidden assumptions, and where bias slipped in. Mark "not yet" and retry if you only
   found one.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one hidden assumption you found. Notice how the same sentence looked
   different once the waterline was drawn.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled iceberg (claim written, one assumption already placed) to
  complete.
- **Extension:** Find a *counterexample* — one real person or place where the assumption fails — and
  say how it changes the argument.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write the claim and assumptions on the iceberg,
  point to the hidden parts, or have a partner read their written critique — no step requires the
  voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner separate a stated claim from its hidden assumptions,
  test one assumption, and name where bias slips in?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed iceberg, kept as the first page of the "critique"
  section of the portfolio.

## Home connection

Find a claim in an advertisement or a headline at home. Ask: what must be true for this to be true?
Name one assumption together.
