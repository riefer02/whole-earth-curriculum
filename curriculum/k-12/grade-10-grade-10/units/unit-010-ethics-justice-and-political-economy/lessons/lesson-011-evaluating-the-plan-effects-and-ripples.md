# Lesson 11 — Evaluating the Plan: Effects and Ripples

## Summary

Learners **evaluate the likely effects** of the collective-action plan they designed in Lesson 10 —
the intended outcomes and the ripples they did not intend. Using a ripple diagram and a checklist,
they name who benefits, who is harmed, what could go wrong, and how they would know it worked, then
revise one step.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the likely effects of a collective-action plan, including intended and unintended
  consequences, and revise it. _(D09.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Every action drops a stone in water, and the ripples travel further than the splash. A plan to
build a well gives water to a village — the intended effect — but it may also draw people from
neighboring areas, change who controls the water, and lower the water table for farms downstream —
ripples no one planned. A good planner does not just hope for the best; they *look ahead* and ask:
who benefits, who is harmed, what could go wrong, and how will we know it worked? Then they revise.
Today you evaluate your own plan that way.

## Materials

- Ripple diagram sheet
- Evaluation checklist

## Preparation

- Copy the ripple diagram and checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, recall your plan — its goal, allies, and steps. Today we look ahead at
  what it would actually do.
- Prepare one worked example of tracing a plan's ripples.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a plan is not finished when it is
written — it must be evaluated for intended effects, second-order effects, and unintended ripples,
then revised.** Teach the ripple-tracing with a worked example, then guided practice (S-011). The
**critical-thinking** lens is the spine: ask "and then what?" repeatedly, and ask how we would *know*
the plan worked (what would we measure?). The **egalitarian** lens: a plan's ripples are not felt
equally — trace who bears each one, especially the unintended harms. The **ethics** lens: an
unintended harm is still a harm, and a fair planner responds to it rather than ignoring it. The
**global** lens: effects cross scales — a local plan can ripple into a regional or global system, and
vice versa. Keep the evaluation honest but not paralyzing: the point is not to find the perfect plan
(philosophy §11) but to see its real shape and improve it. Distinguish what we can *predict* from
what we can only *watch for* (philosophy §5). Preview: next we turn to the ethics of civic technology
and digital citizenship.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10: state your plan's goal in one sentence. Today we look ahead.
2. **Meet the ripple diagram (10 min).** Look at the ripple sheet — a stone in water, rings spreading
   outward: **intended effect**, **second-order effect**, **unintended effect**. Read the worked
   example and see how one plan's ripples were traced.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one evaluation done step by step for the well: (a) intended —
   clean water within reach; (b) second-order — more people come, the well is busier; (c)
   unintended — the water table drops and downstream farms feel it. (d) How would we know it worked?
   — measure how many families get water and whether the water stays clean. (e) Revise — add a step
   to monitor the water table and include downstream farmers as allies.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, take your Lesson-10 plan and fill the ripple diagram:
   one intended effect, one second-order effect, one unintended ripple. Then run the checklist: who
   benefits, who is harmed, what could go wrong, how would you know it worked?
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your evaluation and then **revise one
   step** of your plan in response to an unintended ripple. Mark "not yet" if you found no unintended
   effect — look again.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share your one revision with a partner. What did you see that you had missed
   yesterday?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled ripple diagram (intended effect already written) and a menu of
  possible ripples.
- **Extension:** Predict a **feedback loop** — an effect that changes the plan itself — and add a step
  to monitor or respond to it.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can fill the ripple diagram in writing, draw the
  ripples, or have a partner read their written revision — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name an intended and an unintended effect, say who is
  affected, describe how they would know it worked, and revise one step?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed ripple diagram and one revision, added to the
  "collective action" section.

## Home connection

Ask a family member about a change your community made (a road, a rule, a shop). What was intended,
and what ripples surprised people?
