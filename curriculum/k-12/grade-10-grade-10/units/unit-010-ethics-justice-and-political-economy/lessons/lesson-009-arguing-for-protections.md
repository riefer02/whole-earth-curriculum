# Lesson 9 — Arguing for Protections

## Summary

Learners build a **reasoned argument that a right such as privacy should be protected**, and to what
degree. Using a four-part scaffold — **claim, reason, evidence, response to a counterargument** — they
argue for a specific protection, then test it against a partner's challenge.

## Objectives

- Argue for protections of rights such as privacy, with a claim, reasons, evidence, and a response to
  a counterargument. _(D09.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

Saying "privacy matters" is easy; saying *what should be protected, how much, and why* is the real
work. If a school, an app, or a government wants to collect data about you, a good protection
argument names what should be off-limits, why, and what limit is fair — because no one wants privacy
without any benefit, and no one wants data with no limits. A strong argument for a protection is
specific, honest about trade-offs, and able to answer the person who disagrees. Today you build one.

## Materials

- Protection argument scaffold
- Worked-example card

## Preparation

- Copy the scaffold and worked-example card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 8, recall how a technology can expand and limit privacy at once. Today we
  argue about the *limit* side — what should be protected.
- Prepare one worked example of a four-part protection argument.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **arguing for a protection means
being specific — a claim, a reason, evidence, and an answer to a counterargument — and honest about
degree.** Teach the scaffold with a worked example, then guided practice (S-011). Ground the right in
the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (privacy from arbitrary interference, S-149), while noting
that *how much* protection and *which* trade-off is a values question, not a settled fact (philosophy
§5). The **ethics** lens: a good argument is one a person who disagrees could still respect. The
**egalitarian** lens: ask whose privacy is cheapest to violate — protections should not be a luxury of
the powerful. The **technology** lens: protections must actually fit the technology — consent,
proportionality (collect only what is needed), transparency, and accountability are four live ideas
to draw on. The **global** lens: different places protect privacy differently, and none is the
default; learners may argue from their own context. Keep questions open (philosophy §11). Preview:
we move from arguing about rights to *organizing with others* — designing collective action.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: name one way data can limit privacy. Today you argue about
   what to protect.
2. **Meet the scaffold (10 min).** Look at the four-part scaffold: **claim** (what should be
   protected, and how much), **reason** (why it matters), **evidence** (a fact or example that
   supports it), **response** (your answer to someone who disagrees). Read the worked-example card.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one argument built step by step: *claim* — an app should ask
   before it shares health data, and share only what is needed; *reason* — health data is deeply
   personal and can be used against a person; *evidence* — data can outlive a person's consent and be
   shared in ways they never saw; *response* — "but sharing helps research" → so ask consent, and
   de-identify the data, rather than taking it silently.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, build your own protection argument using the scaffold.
   Then swap and play the person who disagrees — give a counterargument, and have your partner
   answer it.
5. **Independent practice (10 min).** In your journal, write your full four-part argument and add one
   sentence on *degree* — what is too little protection, and what is too much. Mark "not yet" if a
   part is missing.
6. **Close (5 min).** Read your claim and response aloud to a partner. Which part was hardest, and
   why?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-built argument (claim and reason filled) to complete with evidence and a
  response.
- **Extension:** Strengthen the argument by answering a *second*, stronger counterargument, and
  sharpening the claim's degree.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write the four parts, underline each, or have a
  partner read their written argument — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner produce a claim, a reason, evidence, and a response to
  a counterargument, and specify a degree?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The four-part argument, added to the "technology and rights" section.

## Home connection

Argue for one protection in your home or school (e.g., "my room's door should be knocked before
opening"). Give a claim, a reason, and an answer to a counterargument.
