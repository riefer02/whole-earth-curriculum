# Lesson 13 — Designing a Restorative Process

## Summary

Learners **design a restorative process for a specific harm**, deciding who should be heard, how
responsibility is taken, and how repair is made. They use the three restorative questions, study a
real restorative practice, and write their own design, then check it for fairness.

## Objectives

- Design a restorative process for a specific harm, including who should be heard, how responsibility
  is taken, and how repair is made. _(D09.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

When someone is hurt, the first question is usually "what punishment do they deserve?" Restorative
practice asks a different set of questions first: **what happened, who was harmed, and how do we make
it right?** (S-022). It begins by hearing the person who was hurt, giving the person who caused the
harm a real chance to understand and take responsibility, and then agreeing on repair — an apology
that means something, work that restores, a change so it does not happen again. Repair, not only
punishment. Today you design such a process for a real harm.

## Materials

- Restorative design sheet
- Harm case study

## Preparation

- Copy the design sheet and the case study.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall reconciliation and restorative justice — their possibilities and
  limits — and the three restorative questions. Today we turn them into a *design*.
- Prepare one worked example of designing a restorative process.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a restorative process is designed,
not improvised — it decides who should be heard, how responsibility is taken, and how repair is made,
around the three questions: what happened, who was harmed, how do we make it right (S-022).** Teach
the design with a worked example, then guided practice (S-011). Honor the roots: restorative justice
draws on long-standing Indigenous practices — peacemaking circles, the Diné (Navajo) idea of hózhó
(balance and harmony), and similar traditions — not only on modern frameworks (S-161). The
**community** lens is the spine: harm is repaired *in* relationship, not outside it. The
**egalitarian** lens: the person harmed must have a real voice and real power in the process — a
process designed by the powerful alone is not restorative. The **social-emotional** lens: this lesson
touches real hurt; keep the case study age-appropriate and non-graphic, let learners choose the
intensity they engage with, and always connect difficulty to agency (the process itself is the
agency). The **ethics** lens: responsibility is taken, not forced — an apology that is coerced is not
a real apology. Distinguish what a restorative process *does* (evidence from practice) from whether it
is the right response (a values question) (philosophy §5). Preview: next and last, we analyze
peacebuilding — preventing and transforming conflict nonviolently.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9: what are the three restorative questions? Say them in order.
2. **Meet the case (10 min).** Read the harm case study. Underline who was harmed and who caused the
   harm. Note: the point is not to judge, but to design a process.
3. **Meet the design sheet (10 min).** Look at the three questions and the design boxes: **who should
   be heard**, **how responsibility is taken**, **how repair is made**. Read the worked example and
   see how one process was designed.
4. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch one design built step by step: (a) who should be heard — the
   person harmed, the person responsible, people close to both, a fair facilitator; (b) how
   responsibility is taken — the person responsible names what they did, hears the harm, and agrees to
   repair; (c) how repair is made — a real apology, work that restores what was broken, and a plan so
   it does not repeat. Notice consent: no one is made to speak before they are ready.
5. **Guided practice (15 min).** In a pair, design a process for the case. Fill the three boxes, then
   check it against the fairness list: (a) the harmed person's voice leads; (b) responsibility is
   taken, not forced; (c) repair is concrete and checkable.
6. **Independent practice (5 min).** In your journal, write your design in three sentences — one per
   box — and add one limit: what could this process *not* fix? Mark "not yet" if a box is empty.
7. **Close (5 min).** Share your design with another pair. What was the hardest box to fill, and why?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a half-filled design (who should be heard already listed) and sentence
  starters for each box.
- **Extension:** Weigh your restorative design against a punishment-only response for the same harm,
  and defend which you would choose and when.
- **Expression:** Learners who do not speak aloud can write the three boxes, point to who should be
  heard in the circle, or have a partner read their written design — no step requires the voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name who should be heard, how responsibility is taken,
  and how repair is made, and note one limit?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-sentence design, as the "restorative practice" page of the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask a family member: after a hurt in our family or neighborhood, what would make it right — a
punishment, an apology, or something else? Compare with your design.
