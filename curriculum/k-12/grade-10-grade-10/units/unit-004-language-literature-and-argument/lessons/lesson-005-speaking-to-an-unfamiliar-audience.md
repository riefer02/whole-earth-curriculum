# Lesson 5 — Speaking to an Unfamiliar Audience

## Summary

Learners **plan and deliver a short address to a public or unfamiliar audience**,
combining the register work of Lesson 4 with the craft of delivery — breath, pace,
posture, and projection. They use a two-minute planner and a delivery checklist, then
self- and peer-assess, learning that confidence is built, not born.

## Objectives

- Speak to a public or unfamiliar audience with confidence, adapting register and
  formality to the setting. _(D04.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

The knot in your stomach before you speak to people you do not know is not a sign that
something is wrong with you — it is your body preparing for something that matters. The
speakers you admire were not born calm; they planned their words, practiced their
breath, and learned to let the nervousness ride along. Whether the audience is a
classroom, a community meeting, a ceremony, or a camera that reaches strangers, the
same truth holds: **confidence is a skill you practice, one breath and one prepared
sentence at a time.**

## Materials

- Two-minute address planner
- Delivery checklist
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the planner and checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall register — today you choose a register for a real
  audience, then deliver it. From Lesson 3, recall the parts of an argument (claim +
  evidence) to keep your address reasoned, not just felt.
- Prepare one worked example: a short, strong opening and closing you can model aloud.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **public speaking is a
practiced skill with visible parts — preparation (purpose, audience, one point, one
piece of evidence), and delivery (breath, pace, volume, posture, eye line) — and
nervousness is normal and manageable, not a verdict.** The register work of Lesson 4
(S-237) now becomes a choice made *for a real listener*: the same message is said
differently to peers than to a formal body, and the skilled speaker reads the audience
and adapts. Confidence, in the developmental picture for ages 14–18, is partly a matter
of practice and feedback — the more a learner speaks and hears themselves improve, the
more the fear recedes.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). Keep stakes low: everyone speaks to a partner before anyone
speaks to the whole room, and a recording can replace a live audience where that is
safer. Delivery may be oral, signed, or through a communication device (AAC) — the craft
being assessed is purpose, audience, one point, one piece of evidence, and a register
matched to the audience, not the voice or the standing posture. The **social-emotional**
lens is the spine: name the physical signs of
nervousness (racing heart, shallow breath, shaking hands) as the body's preparation,
not a failure; teach a slow breath as a real tool. The **egalitarian** lens: the
"right" accent or register is a gate — every voice, every dialect, every first language
is welcome on this stage, and confidence should not require a learner to erase their
own way of speaking. The **global** lens: every culture has its own arts of public
address — formal oratory, call-and-response, story — and a learner may draw on their own
tradition's strengths. The **ethics** lens: with an audience comes responsibility — say
what is true, own your sources, and do not use the platform to harm. The
**critical-thinking** lens: keep the address *reasoned* — one claim, one piece of
evidence — so confidence is built on substance, not just polish. Preview: Lesson 6
turns from speaking to *reading* — evaluating media for bias and omission.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what is register? Today you pick a register for a
   real audience — and you deliver it out loud.
2. **Plan the address (15 min).** Fill the two-minute planner: your **purpose** (to
   inform, to persuade, to honor?), your **audience** (who, and how formal?), **one main
   point**, **one piece of evidence**, an **opening** that gets attention, and a
   **closing** that lands. Watch a worked example of a strong opening and closing, then
   write your own.
3. **Learn the delivery moves (10 min).** Read the delivery checklist: slow your pace
   (nerves speed you up), take one breath before you begin, and project your voice so the
   back of the room hears you (speak from your chest, or sign clearly, or let your device
   carry the words). Sit or stand open and grounded. If it is comfortable for you and
   your culture, let your eyes move across the audience; eye contact is *optional*, not
   required — some cultures and some learners find it easier to look at a spot just above
   the listeners' heads, or not at all. Practice the breath together: in for four, out
   for six.
4. **Deliver to a partner (15 min).** Stand and deliver your two-minute address to one
   or two listeners. They listen for *one* strength and *one* thing to try next, using
   the checklist.
5. **Self-assess and revise (10 min).** Hear the feedback. What worked? What will you
   change? Mark one revision on your planner and say the changed line aloud once more.
6. **Close (5 min).** In your journal: *before I spoke I felt ___; after I spoke I felt
   ___; the next time I speak to strangers I will remember ___.* Remember: the nervous
   feeling is energy you can use.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Reduce the address to one minute and one sentence each for opening,
  point, and closing; allow speaking seated if standing is a barrier.
- **Alternative delivery:** A learner who is nonverbal, has a speech or voice
  impairment, or whose first language is signed may deliver the address in sign, through
  a communication device or AAC, or as a written script shared with the listener. Only
  the channel differs — the assessed craft (purpose, audience, one point, one piece of
  evidence, and a register matched to the audience) is identical, and the planner and
  checklist still apply. A learner who cannot manage a live audience may instead record,
  sign, or write the address.
- **Extension:** Deliver to a larger or mixed-age audience, field one question, and add
  a fair counter-point with a one-sentence rebuttal.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance + peer):** Can the learner deliver a two-minute address with
  a clear point, one piece of evidence, and a register matched to the audience, and
  name one thing to improve?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The filled planner (and, where possible, a recording),
  added to the portfolio as the "public address" the unit's assessment plan calls for.

## Home connection

Give your two-minute address to an adult you trust — or a voice note to a relative far
away — and ask them to tell you one thing they remember. That single remembered thing
is your message working.
