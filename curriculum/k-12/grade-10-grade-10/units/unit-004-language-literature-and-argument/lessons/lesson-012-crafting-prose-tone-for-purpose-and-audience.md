# Lesson 12 — Crafting Prose: Tone for Purpose and Audience

## Summary

Learners **choose and hold a tone** to serve a purpose and reach an audience. They plan
purpose, audience, and tone words, study one idea written three ways, then draft a short
piece whose word choice and rhythm carry a single, deliberate tone — and revise that
piece for a different audience without changing the facts.

## Objectives

- Craft prose with deliberate style, varying tone to suit purpose and audience.
  _(D04.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

The same news — "the well is running low" — can be said gently to a worried grandparent,
urgently to a neighbor, and flatly in a formal report. The facts do not change; the
**tone** does, and the tone is what tells the reader how to feel and how to respond. A
writer who has not chosen a tone has not really chosen what to say — the words just
happen to them. Picking a tone on purpose, and holding it, is what turns a note into a
message: a letter that comforts, a call that moves, an argument that persuades.

## Materials

- Tone-for-purpose sheet
- "One idea, three tones" worked set
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy the tone-for-purpose sheet and the worked set.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, recall sentence rhythm and word choice. Today those tools
  are aimed at a *purpose* and an *audience*. From Lesson 4, recall register — tone is
  register's emotional color.
- Prepare one worked example: one idea written warm, urgent, and measured.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **tone is the
emotional register of a piece, chosen to serve a purpose and an audience; it is carried
by word choice and rhythm, and it must hold steady or it loses the reader.** Tone and
register (Lesson 4, S-237) are cousins: register is formality, tone is feeling. A
deliberate writer plans both. The writing-craft evidence is explicit: adolescents write
better when taught to plan for audience and purpose and to revise with models, not when
left to intuition (S-102).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **intellectual** lens is the spine: the tone-for-purpose
plan is a small act of design — name the goal, then choose the words that meet it. The
**ethics** lens: tone can be used to calm or to inflame, to clarify or to mislead — the
same facts, two tones, two effects; choosing tone is a moral choice as much as a craft
one. The **egalitarian** lens: a reader's home language and dialect shape which tones
feel warm or cold; there is no universal "professional" tone, and a learner's own way of
putting things is not a defect to edit away. The **global** lens: cultures differ in
what tone fits a given moment — what is polite in one may be cold in another — so tone is
read *in context*, never as a fixed rule. The **social-emotional** lens: writing in a
tone that does not come naturally (firm when you are shy, warm when you are formal) is a
real stretch; normalize it and let learners name the feeling. Preview: Lesson 13 widens
the canvas — composing not just words, but image, sound, and design.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11, what two tools did you practice? (Sentence rhythm
   and word choice.) Today you aim them at a *purpose* and an *audience* — that aim is
   called tone.
2. **Meet tone (15 min).** Look at the "one idea, three tones" set: the same message
   written warm, urgent, and measured. Read each aloud. For each, name the **tone**, the
   **purpose** (to comfort? to move? to report?), and the likely **audience**. Notice
   *which words* carry the tone.
3. **Plan your piece (10 min).** Fill the tone-for-purpose sheet: what is your
   **purpose**, who is your **audience**, and which three **tone words** (e.g., warm,
   steady, firm) will you hold? Choose a real message you want to send — a thank-you, a
   request, a warning, an argument.
4. **Draft in one tone (15 min).** Write a short piece (a paragraph) that holds your
   chosen tone from first word to last. Use Lesson 11's tools: a short sentence where you
   want emphasis, precise words that fit the tone.
5. **Read it back and revise (7 min).** Read your piece aloud. Where does the tone slip?
   Fix one word or one sentence so the tone holds. Then — challenge — rewrite your
   *opening line only* for a different audience, keeping the facts identical.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: the facts are the message; the tone is how it lands.
   You now choose both. Next time, you compose with more than words.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-chosen purpose and audience and a list of tone words to
  select from; draft two sentences rather than a paragraph.
- **Extension:** Revise the full piece for a second audience of a very different kind,
  and write a short note explaining each tonal change and why it serves the new reader.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name their purpose, audience, and tone, draft a
  short piece that holds that tone, and explain one revision that restores it?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The tone-for-purpose plan and the drafted piece, added
  to the portfolio as the second styled-prose entry.

## Home connection

Read your piece to someone at home without telling them the tone you intended, and ask
them to name it. If they name something different, that is useful information — revise
one line together.
