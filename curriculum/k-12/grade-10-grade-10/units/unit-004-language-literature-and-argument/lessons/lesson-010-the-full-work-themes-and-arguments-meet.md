# Lesson 10 — The Full Work: Themes and Arguments Meet

## Summary

Learners **bring the whole extended work together**: its central theme and central
argument, how the two connect, and one supported, honest judgment. They complete a
four-quadrant synthesis organizer and write a short synthesis that draws evidence from
the logs kept across Lessons 8–9, closing the sustained-reading thread.

## Objectives

- Read extended, complex literary and informational texts, tracking themes and
  arguments across a full work — by *synthesizing* both threads into one supported
  judgment. _(D04.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

You do not really know a long book until you can hold it whole in one hand: *this is
what it was about, this is what it was trying to show, and this is what I think of
that.* Everything you noticed along the way — every bead on the theme thread, every
link in the argument chain — was gathering toward this moment. A supported judgment is
different from a snap opinion: it is built out of the evidence you actually logged, and
it is honest about what the work does well and what it leaves open. That is the
difference between having *finished* a book and having *read* it.

## Materials

- Full-work synthesis organizer (four quadrants)
- Reading journal (theme log + argument log)
- The chosen extended work (completed or near-completed)

## Preparation

- Copy the four-quadrant organizer.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 8–9, bring the theme thread and argument chain you built.
  Today you weave them into one picture.
- Prepare one worked example: a short synthesis sentence that connects a theme to an
  argument and backs each with a text detail.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **synthesis is not a
summary — it is a *judgment* built from tracked evidence: name the central theme, name
the central argument, show how they connect, and state a supported overall judgment
that is honest about nuance.** A synthesis draws on the active, question-driven reading
the learner has done all along (S-040): the two logs are the raw material, and the
synthesis is the finished piece. The theme and the argument are not separate — in a
strong work, the theme is *argued* (shown, proven, tested) and the argument is *about*
the theme; naming that connection is the heart of the task.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **intellectual** lens is the spine: this is a small work
of scholarship — claim, evidence, and honest limit. The **critical-thinking** lens: the
judgment must weigh strengths *and* gaps; a synthesis that only praises or only faults
is not a synthesis. The **ethics** lens: be fair to the author — represent the work's
strongest version before judging it. The **egalitarian** lens: every learner's chosen
work, from any tradition and language, earns the same serious treatment; there is no
"high" and "low" text here. The **global** lens: invite learners to note what their
work assumes about the world, and how a reader from another culture might read it
differently. Preview: Lesson 11 turns from reading to *writing* — crafting prose with
deliberate style.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Open your two logs — the theme thread and the argument chain.
   Today you weave them into one judgment of the whole work.
2. **Meet the organizer (10 min).** Look at the four quadrants: **central theme**,
   **central argument**, **how they connect**, and **my supported judgment**. Watch a
   worked example: one synthesis sentence that names a theme and an argument, shows
   their connection, and backs each with a detail from the text.
3. **Fill the first two quadrants (15 min).** From your logs, write the work's central
   theme (what idea did it keep returning to?) and its central argument (what was it
   trying to show?). Use the evidence you logged — cite the moments, not just the
   feeling.
4. **Show the connection (10 min).** Write how the theme and argument meet: how does
   the work *argue* its theme? Which bead on the thread is also a link in the chain?
5. **Write the supported judgment (12 min).** In your journal, write a short synthesis
   (a paragraph): *this work returns to the idea of ___, and argues that ___; its
   strength is ___, and it leaves open ___; on the whole, I judge it ___ because ___.*
   Make sure every judgment is backed by a detail you logged.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: you did not just finish a book — you read it whole.
   That same skill reads a person, a debate, and a lifetime of news. Preview: next,
   you become the writer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames for each quadrant and allow the judgment to be
  spoken rather than written, then transcribed.
- **Extension:** Add a "nuance" box: name one place the work is genuinely ambiguous, and
  argue why that ambiguity is a strength rather than a flaw.

## Assessment

- **Summative (self + portfolio):** The completed synthesis organizer and the synthesis
  paragraph, checked against the logs for evidence — added to the portfolio as the
  "extended-text reading journal" capstone.

## Home connection

Read your synthesis paragraph aloud to someone at home. Ask them: *does my judgment
sound fair to the book?* Revise one sentence based on what they say.

- On the claim/evidence/rebuttal structure behind the argument thread: Toulmin, *The
