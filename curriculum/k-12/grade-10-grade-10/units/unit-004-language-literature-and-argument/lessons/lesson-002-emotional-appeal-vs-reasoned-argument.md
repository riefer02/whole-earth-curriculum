# Lesson 2 — Emotional Appeal vs. Reasoned Argument

## Summary

Learners learn to **separate emotional appeal from reasoned argument**. Using a short,
authentic passage, they sort words that stir feeling from claims that carry evidence,
name what the speaker is quietly asking them to do, and decide whether the feeling is
supporting the argument or standing in for it.

## Objectives

- Critique rhetoric, distinguishing emotional appeal from reasoned argument, by
  sorting a real message into feeling-moves and reason-moves. _(D04.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

When a friend says "everyone is going — you'd be left out," that is a feeling aimed at
your fear of being alone. When the same friend says "we leave at six, the bus is cheap,
and you'll be back before dark," that is a set of reasons you can check. Both are
normal; both can be true at once. The skill is not to reject feeling — it is to *see*
when a speaker is stirring feeling *instead of* giving you a reason, so you can decide
with your eyes open. This happens everywhere: a campaign poster, a market cry, a
funeral tribute, a sermon, a political rally on your street or on your screen.

## Materials

- Two-column sheet (feeling vs. reason, with a "what are they asking me to do" band)
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the three appeals — ethos, pathos, logos. Today we
  focus on the line between pathos (feeling) and logos (reason), and how they mix.
- Prepare one worked example: a short, authentic passage with clear feeling-words and
  at least one checkable claim.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a message can move
feelings and offer reasons at the same time; the critique is to find where the feeling
is doing the work that a reason should do — and to notice what the speaker wants you to
do.** Emotional appeal is not wrong: courage, care, and grief are real, and speakers
rightly reach them. The distinction to teach is between a feeling that *supports* a
reasoned case and a feeling that *substitutes* for one. Reasoned argument, in the
tradition that shapes this unit, means a claim backed by evidence and open to being
checked (S-236). A strong feeling with no checkable claim is still a strong feeling —
but it is not yet an argument.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). Use a passage from your own context if you prefer; the worked
example should be short, real, and age-appropriate, never a trap for a learner's own
beliefs. The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: mark *feeling-words* and
*checkable claims* in the same text, then ask which is carrying the weight. The
**ethics** lens: stirring fear or anger to sell a lie is manipulation; stirring hope to
move people toward fairness is not the same thing — judge by what is being asked and
for whom. The **egalitarian** lens: people with less power are often spoken *at* with
feeling and denied the reasons; demanding the reasons is a demand to be treated as an
equal. The **technology** lens: digital feeds optimize for feeling — outrage and awe
travel farther than careful reasoning (S-481) — so the skill is most needed exactly
where you read most. The **social-emotional** lens: naming your own feeling as it
rises ("I am being made afraid") is the first guard against being carried. Preview:
Lesson 3 turns this sort into a full critique of one public speech.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what are the three appeals? Today we zoom into
   the line between *pathos* (feeling) and *logos* (reason) — the line this whole
   unit turns on.
2. **Meet the two columns (15 min).** Look at the two-column sheet. In the left column
   go *words that stir feeling* — fear, pride, pity, anger, love, shame. In the right
   column go *claims with evidence* — statements you could check. Watch a worked
   example: read a short passage together and sort it sentence by sentence. Mark which
   words aim at the heart and which at the head.
3. **Ask what they want (10 min).** Every persuasive message is asking you to *do*
   something — vote, buy, join, believe, stay quiet, act now. In the bottom band,
   write what this speaker is asking of you. Then ask: is the feeling *supporting* a
   reason, or *standing in for* one?
4. **Sort your own (15 min).** Choose one real message you heard or read this week —
   an ad, a post, a speech, a family plea. Sort it into the two columns. Mark one
   claim that is checkable and one feeling that is doing the work of a reason.
5. **Write your finding (7 min).** In your journal: *this message moves me by ___,
   and it reasons with me by ___; the feeling is (supporting / replacing) the reason,
   because ___.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: feeling is not the enemy of truth — but a feeling
   dressed up as a reason deserves a second look. Preview: next time, we critique a
   whole speech, start to finish.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a shorter passage and pre-highlight the feeling-words; ask only
  "is there a reason I could check?"
- **Extension:** Take a message whose feeling is doing the work, and write the
  missing reasoned argument it would need — a claim, one piece of evidence, and what
  it cannot prove.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner mark one feeling-move and one reason-move in a
  passage, and state whether the feeling supports or replaces the reason?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-column sort of a real message, added to the
  unit portfolio as the "feeling vs. reason" entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust about an ad or speech they remember that made them feel
something strongly. Together, find the sentence that stirred the feeling — and ask
whether there was a reason behind it they could check.
