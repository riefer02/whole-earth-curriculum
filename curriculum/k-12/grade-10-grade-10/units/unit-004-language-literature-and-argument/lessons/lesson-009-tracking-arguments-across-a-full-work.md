# Lesson 9 — Tracking Arguments Across a Full Work

## Summary

Learners track a second thread through their extended work: the **argument**. Using an
argument-chain tracker, they log each claim, its evidence, and any counter-argument the
text itself raises, watching a thesis build across a whole book. They connect the
rhetoric critique of Lessons 1–3 to a *sustained* text rather than a single speech.

## Objectives

- Read extended, complex literary and informational texts, tracking arguments across a
  full work — by following the claim → evidence → counter-argument chain. _(D04.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

A long book that wants to convince you does not make one point once; it *builds* a
case — a claim here, a piece of evidence there, a worry answered a few chapters later.
Reading only the opening or only the ending would miss the work the middle is doing.
Following an argument across a whole work is like following a river to its mouth: you
learn not just what the author believes, but *how* they got there, what they considered,
and what they answered along the way. Whether the work is a memoir making a case about
a life, or a long essay making a case about the world, the skill is the same: keep
track of the chain.

## Materials

- Argument-chain tracker
- Reading journal (argument log)
- The chosen extended work

## Preparation

- Copy the argument-chain tracker and argument log.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall claim, evidence, appeal, and fair counter-point. From
  Lesson 8, recall the theme thread. Today you track the *argument* thread through the
  same work.
- Prepare one worked example: a short passage where the author makes a claim and offers
  evidence, and note how you would log it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a sustained argument
is a chain — claim, evidence, and counter-argument — that builds across a work; the
reader's job is to log the links and ask whether each holds and whether the author
answers the strongest objection.** This reuses the Toulmin frame of claim, data
(evidence), warrant, and rebuttal (S-236), now stretched across a book rather than a
speech. Literary works argue too — a novel or memoir makes claims about how people are,
what matters, what is just — and the same chain can be read there, more subtly.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). Reading comprehension is built by actively generating
questions and tracking structure, not by passive rereading (S-040). The
**critical-thinking** lens is the spine: for each link, ask *does this evidence support
this claim, and does the author answer the counter-argument fairly?* The **intellectual**
lens: this is the unit's sustained scholarship — a reader who can reconstruct an
author's case has earned the right to judge it. The **ethics** lens: a fair reader
tracks the author's *strongest* argument, not a straw version; steelman before you
critique. The **egalitarian** lens: authors from any tradition — a memoirist, an
essayist, a novelist, an oral epic retold — deserve the same careful reading; argument
is not the property of one culture. The **global** lens: the same chain appears in
argumentative traditions worldwide, from Indian nyāya debate to the persuasive essay,
and learners may name their own tradition's forms. Preview: Lesson 10 brings theme and
argument together into a full synthesis of the whole work.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8, what theme are you following? Today you follow a
   second thread — the *argument* — through the same book.
2. **Meet the argument chain (15 min).** Look at the argument-chain tracker: each link
   is a **claim**; hanging below it is its **evidence**, and beside it any
   **counter-argument** the text raises and how it answers. Watch a worked example:
   read a short passage where the author claims something and gives a reason, and log
   the link.
3. **Distinguish the central thesis (10 min).** As you read, ask: what is the *one*
   thing this whole work is trying to show? Write the central claim in your own words —
   that is the chain's first link. Then note how later chapters add links to it.
4. **Log claims, evidence, and counter-arguments (15 min).** Continue reading your
   work. For each new section, log one claim, the evidence offered for it, and any
   counter-argument the author names or answers. If the author ignores a strong
   objection, note that too — it is a real observation.
5. **Test one link (7 min).** Choose one link from your chain. Ask: does the evidence
   actually support the claim, or only sit beside it? Write one sentence of judgment.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: an argument is a chain you can inspect link by link.
   Next time, you'll weave the theme and the argument together into one picture of the
   whole work.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-labeled chain with the central claim already written; ask
  the learner to find and add one piece of evidence and one counter-argument.
- **Extension:** Track the author's *warrant* — the unstated rule connecting evidence to
  claim — for two links, and judge whether it holds (S-236).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state the work's central claim, log one piece of
  evidence and one counter-argument, and say whether the evidence supports the claim?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The argument-chain tracker, added to the portfolio as
  the second strand of the extended-text reading journal.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home the central argument of the work you are reading, in one sentence.
Then ask them to poke one hole in it — and check whether the book has already answered
their objection.
