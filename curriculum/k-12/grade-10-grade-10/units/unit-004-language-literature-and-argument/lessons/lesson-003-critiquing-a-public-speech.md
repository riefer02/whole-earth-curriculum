# Lesson 3 — Critiquing a Public Speech

## Summary

Learners put Lessons 1 and 2 together into a **full critique of one public speech**.
Using a five-part scaffold — claim, evidence, appeal, what is asked of them, and a fair
counter-point — they practice on a worked example and then critique a speech of their
own choosing, writing a fair assessment that names both strengths and gaps.

## Objectives

- Critique rhetoric in public speech, distinguishing emotional appeal from reasoned
  argument, by working through a complete speech with a claim–evidence–appeal scaffold.
  _(D04.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Every time a leader, a campaign, a preacher, or a protester stands up to persuade you,
they are building a case in front of you — and you are allowed to ask questions of it.
Does this claim hold up? Where is the evidence? What am I being asked to do, and for
whom? Being able to answer those questions is the difference between being *spoken to*
and being *reasoned with*. It is a skill you will use at a town meeting, at a ballot
box, at a market, and in front of any screen.

## Materials

- Speech-critique scaffold (five rows)
- A short printed or projected public speech (worked example)
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the scaffold and the worked speech (Wangari Maathai's 2004 Nobel lecture
  excerpts, or a community leader's address — keep it short and real; S-059).
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, recall the three appeals and the feeling-vs-reason
  sort. Today we run the whole critique on one speech.
- Read the worked speech once yourself and mark its claims and evidence so you can
  model the first rows.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a critique is a
*fair reading* that names what a speech claims, what evidence it offers, which appeal
it leans on, and what it asks — then weighs strengths and gaps without mocking the
speaker.** A reasoned argument is a claim backed by evidence, open to checking (S-236);
a critique asks whether the evidence actually supports the claim and whether a
counter-point is fairly handled. Wangari Maathai's 2004 Nobel Peace Prize lecture is a
strong worked example: it makes real claims (the Green Belt Movement planted over
thirty million trees; started in 1977; spread to other African countries) that can be
checked (S-059), and it also reaches the audience's feeling about land, dignity, and
future. It lets learners see a speaker use *both* appeals well — which is the honest,
non-cynical conclusion this lesson should reach.

Model the first two rows, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: hold the speech
to the question "does the evidence support the claim?" The **ethics** lens: critique
the *argument*, never the *person*; the goal is truth and fairness, not humiliation.
The **egalitarian** lens: critique is a power — it treats every speaker, however
powerful, as accountable to reason; and it belongs to everyone, not just experts. The
**global** lens: choose speeches from many traditions, and let learners critique a
speaker from their own community or language — the skill transfers across every art of
speaking. The **technology** lens: the same scaffold reads a viral video, a campaign
ad, or a politician's clip — media are speeches too. Preview: Lesson 4 shifts from
*reading* a speaker to *being* one, beginning with register.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What did you sort in Lesson 2? (Feeling-moves vs. reason-moves.)
   Today you run the whole critique on one speech — fairly, from start to finish.
2. **Meet the scaffold (10 min).** Look at the five rows: **claim** (what the speaker
   says is true or should be done), **evidence** (what backs it up), **appeal** (which
   of ethos/pathos/logos carries it), **asked of me** (what the speaker wants me to do),
   and **fair counter-point** (a strong objection the speaker must answer).
3. **Worked example, rows 1–2 (15 min).** Read the worked speech aloud twice — once to
   hear the feeling, once to catch the claims. Together, write the main claim and the
   evidence offered for it. Check: does the evidence actually support the claim, or
   only sit beside it?
4. **Worked example, rows 3–5 (10 min).** Which appeal carries the speech? What is the
   audience asked to do? Then write one *fair* counter-point — the strongest honest
   objection — and whether the speech answers it.
5. **Critique your own (15 min).** Choose a short speech — from your region, your
   language tradition, or a speaker you admire — and fill all five rows yourself.
6. **Write a fair critique (5 min).** In your journal, write two or three sentences:
   *this speech argues that ___, supports it with ___, and asks me to ___; its strength
   is ___, and its open question is ___.*
7. **Close.** Remember: to critique is to take a speaker seriously enough to ask for
   their reasons. That is respect, not rudeness.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the claim and evidence pre-written for the worked speech; ask
  only for the appeal and the "asked of me" rows.
- **Extension:** Add Toulmin's warrant and rebuttal: name the unstated rule connecting
  evidence to claim, and write the rebuttal the speaker should give to the
  counter-point (S-236).

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Learners exchange scaffolds and check one another's "fair
  counter-point" for fairness — does it steelman the speaker or mock them?
- **Summative artifact (unit):** The completed five-row critique of a chosen speech,
  added to the portfolio as the "rhetoric critique" the unit's assessment plan calls
  for.

## Home connection

Watch or listen to a short speech with an adult you trust, then fill in one row each:
you find the claim, they find the evidence — and decide together whether the two match.
