# Lesson 7 — Evidence and Belief: Telling Them Apart

## Summary

Learners **distinguish evidence from belief**. A claim about the world can be tested
and corroborated; a belief or value is *held* rather than *proven*. They sort a mixed
set of statements, fact-check one claim, and reflect on what each is allowed to do in
their thinking — learning to hold both with honesty, and to let evidence discipline
their factual claims without demanding that every belief submit to a test it was never
meant to pass.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate media for the difference between evidence and belief, by sorting
  claims that can be tested from values and convictions that are held. _(D04.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

"I saw the shop open at seven" is a claim about the world — you could check it. "The
shopkeeper is a good person" is a judgment you hold — no single fact settles it. Life
is full of both, tangled together, and a great deal of confusion (and more than a
little manipulation) comes from treating one as if it were the other: dressing a value
up as a fact, or dismissing a fact as "just your opinion." The skill is not to abandon
your beliefs — it is to know which of your thoughts are *claims* (and therefore owe the
world evidence) and which are *values* (and therefore owe the world honesty, but not
proof).

## Materials

- Evidence vs. belief sheet
- A short set of mixed statements (checkable claims + held beliefs/values)
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and the statement set (keep statements age-appropriate, uncontroversial
  enough to discuss safely, and drawn from daily life — e.g., "this river floods in the
  rainy season" vs. "we should look after this river").
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall the five questions, especially "what is the
  evidence?" Today we split evidence from belief on its own.
- Prepare one worked example: sort two statements and say *why* each belongs where it
  does.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **evidence and belief
are different kinds of thought, and each is legitimate in its own place — a claim about
the world owes evidence and can be checked, while a value or conviction is held rather
than proven, and we mislead ourselves when we swap them.** This distinction is a core of
media and information literacy: UNESCO frames the ability to distinguish information
from persuasion, and evidence from belief, as central to evaluating media (S-217); the
media-literacy question-sets of NAMLE and the Center for Media Literacy build the same
habit (S-151; S-442). The lesson's deeper commitment (philosophy §5) is that the
curriculum itself distinguishes evidence from values and models the difference.

Hold the line gently and without ranking anyone's beliefs: the point is *clarity about
the kind of claim*, not a verdict that beliefs are lesser. A belief can be deeply true
for a person, a family, or a tradition without being a testable claim — and that is
fine. Model the sort once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: sort every
statement by "could I test this?" The **ethics** lens: it is dishonest to pass a value
off as a fact, and it is also dishonest to sneer at a belief simply because it cannot be
tested — respect the kind of claim being made. The **egalitarian** lens: the powerful
often dress their values as facts ("it has always been this way") to close off challenge;
asking "is that a claim or a belief?" reopens the door. The **global** lens: different
cultures draw the evidence/belief line in different places, and both hold wisdom —
describe, don't rank. The **social-emotional** lens: discovering a cherished belief is
"not a claim" can feel like a loss; name that feeling and let it sit. Preview: Lesson 8
begins the extended reading — tracking themes across a full work.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6, the five questions end with "what is the
   evidence?" Today that question gets its own day.
2. **Meet the two columns (15 min).** Look at the evidence-vs-belief sheet. Left column:
   **evidence** — a claim about the world that can be tested and corroborated by a
   second source. Right column: **belief or value** — a conviction I hold (fairness,
   family, faith, taste) that is not settled by a test. Watch a worked example: sort
   two statements and say *why* each goes where it does.
3. **Sort the set (15 min).** In pairs, sort the mixed statements. For each "evidence"
   claim, say how you would test it; for each belief, say what makes it a value rather
   than a claim. Note the tricky ones — where the line is genuinely blurry.
4. **Check one claim (10 min).** Take one "evidence" claim and actually check it:
   trace it to a source, and ask whether a second, independent source agrees. If the
   set has no claim to check, invent a safe one ("the nearest river rises in the rainy
   season") and check it together.
5. **Write the honest mix (7 min).** In your journal: *one thing I believe that is not
   a claim is ___, and it matters to me because ___; one thing I treat as fact that I
   should actually check is ___.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: beliefs deserve honesty, claims deserve evidence, and
   confusing the two is how we get fooled — and how we fool ourselves.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a shorter statement set with clear-cut examples, and a starter
  ("This is a claim because I could check it by ___.").
- **Extension:** Take a real public statement and separate its factual claims from its
  value claims, then fact-check one factual claim against two independent sources.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner sort a new statement as evidence or belief and
  explain the test that decides, or why no test applies?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The "honest mix" journal entry, added to the portfolio
  as the evidence-vs-belief reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust about one thing your family believes strongly. Together, decide:
is it a claim about the world (something to check), or a value (something to hold)? Talk
about how your family treats each kind.
