# Lesson 13 — Multimodal Composition, Accessible by Design

## Summary

Learners **compose a multimodal text** — combining words, image, sound, and design —
and make it **accessible by design**: alt text and captions, readable contrast, clear
structure, and a text version of any sound. They plan, build, and test one piece with
an accessibility checklist, learning that accessibility is not an afterthought but part
of what a text *is*.

## Objectives

- Compose multimodal texts that combine writing, image, sound, and design, with
  attention to accessibility. _(D04.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

Think of the last thing you actually paid attention to — a poster, a video, a post, a
flyer for a community event. It worked because it *combined* more than words: an image
caught your eye, a layout guided you, maybe a sound or a color carried a feeling. Now
imagine you could not see the image, or could not hear the sound, or the text was too
faint to read. If the maker built it well, you would still get the whole message —
because a good multimodal text *carries its meaning in more than one channel at once*.
Accessibility is not a special favor for a few people; it is the difference between a
message that reaches *some* people and one that reaches *everyone*.

## Materials

- Multimodal planner (four quadrants + accessibility checklist)
- Accessibility checklist
- Art supplies or a device

## Preparation

- Copy the planner and checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 11–12, recall sentence craft and tone. Today those become one
  *mode* among four, and accessibility joins the craft.
- Prepare one worked example: a simple poster or slide with a missing alt text and weak
  contrast, to fix together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a multimodal text
combines writing, image, sound, and design into one message, and accessibility — alt
text, captions, readable contrast, clear structure, and a text version of sound — is a
design requirement, not a favor.** The international standard is WCAG, organized around
four principles: content must be **perceivable**, **operable**, **understandable**, and
**robust** (S-280). At this level, learners meet a practical subset: every image has a
caption and alt text; color is never the only signal; contrast is readable; headings
give structure; and any sound has a text version. These are the same skills the
curriculum's own graphics follow (see `docs/art-style.md`).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **technology** lens is the spine: read the *medium* — how
an image, a sound, and a layout each carry meaning, and how a design choice includes or
excludes. The **egalitarian** lens is the heart: accessibility is justice — a text that
a screen reader, a hard-of-hearing learner, or a low-vision reader cannot use is a text
that has silently chosen its audience; the digital divide (S-281) also decides who can
even reach the tool, so keep a paper path open. The **ethics** lens: building for
everyone, not just the "typical" user, is a moral commitment, and it is also simply
better craft. The **global** lens: accessibility needs and norms differ across
languages, scripts, and cultures — right-to-left scripts, tone languages, sign
languages — so "accessible" is context-dependent, and multilingual learners can extend a
piece into two languages. The **critical-thinking** lens: test your own piece with the
checklist, and let a partner *experience* it without one sense to find what you missed.
Preview: Lesson 14 turns from how we *compose* to who holds *power* in language.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 11–12, you learned to craft words. Today you add
   image, sound, and design — and one more demand: that the piece work for everyone.
2. **Meet the four modes (10 min).** Look at the multimodal planner's four quadrants:
   **words** (the writing), **image** (a picture, symbol, or chart), **sound** (spoken
   word, music, or a voice note), and **design** (layout, color, order). A strong piece
   uses more than one so the message survives even if one channel fails.
3. **Meet the accessibility checklist (15 min).** Read the checklist: every image has a
   **caption** and **alt text** (words that describe it for someone who cannot see it);
   **color is never the only signal**; **contrast** is strong enough to read; **headings**
   give structure; and any **sound has a text version**. Watch a worked example: a slide
   with no alt text and faint text — fix it together.
4. **Plan and build your piece (20 min).** Choose a real message to share — an
   invitation, a how-to, a small argument, or an announcement. Plan all four modes, then
   build your piece (on paper or a device). Write the alt text and caption for your
   image, and a text version of any sound.
5. **Test it (7 min).** Swap with a partner. They close their eyes (or imagine they
   cannot see the image) and tell you the message from your alt text and words alone;
   then they check contrast, headings, and the sound's text version. Fix one thing they
   found.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: a message that reaches everyone is a better message,
   full stop. Next time, we ask whose language has power — and whose does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build a two-mode piece (image + text) with a provided alt-text frame, and
  complete a three-item accessibility checklist.
- **Extension:** Build all four modes and apply the four WCAG principles — perceivable,
  operable, understandable, robust — to your own piece and a peer's, with evidence for
  each (S-280).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Does the piece combine at least two modes, and does it
  pass the accessibility checklist — alt text/caption, readable contrast, structure, and
  a text version of any sound?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The multimodal piece, added to the portfolio as the
  "accessible multimodal composition" the unit's assessment plan calls for.

## Home connection

Show your piece to someone at home and ask them to experience it once with their eyes
closed, then once with sound off. Whatever still works is the accessibility doing its
job — and whatever does not is your next fix.
