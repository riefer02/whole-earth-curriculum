# Lesson 4 — Register: Matching Your Voice to the Room

## Summary

Learners meet **register** — adapting language's formality, forms of address, and tone
to the setting — and practice it as a skill of respect and clarity. They build a
spectrum from close to formal, place real settings along it, and re-write one message
five ways, seeing that every language and culture draws this line in its own way.

## Objectives

- Speak to a public or unfamiliar audience with confidence, adapting register and
  formality to the setting — beginning by *mapping* the registers we already move
  between. _(D04.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

You already do this every day without thinking: the way you greet a close friend is
not the way you greet a grandparent, and neither is the way you would speak to a
doctor, a shopkeeper, or a stranger asking directions. The words, the titles, the tone
— even how loud or soft you speak — shift with the room. This is not putting on a fake
self; it is *fitting your voice to the listener*, which is what being understood and
showing respect both require. In a city apartment, a village gathering, or a formal
interview, the skill is the same: read the room, then match it.

## Materials

- Register spectrum strip
- "One message, five ways" sheet
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the spectrum strip and the "one message, five ways" sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall how a speaker adapts a presentation to audience and
  purpose (D04.S1.07.02). Today we name the *axis* they adapt along: register.
- Prepare one worked example: one request (e.g., "please help me") said five ways.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **register is the
dimension of language — formality, address, tone — that we vary to fit a setting; it is
a skill of respect and clarity, and every culture marks it differently.** The Common
European Framework and its companion volume treat adapting register (formal/informal)
and forms of address as a core of communicative competence, alongside knowing more than
one language (S-237). Many languages mark respect *inside the grammar itself* —
honorifics, polite verb endings, titles — so "register" is not just word choice but
sometimes grammar. Hold this as description and appreciation: no register is "better";
the skilled speaker matches the room. Frame code-switching (moving between languages or
registers) as a resource, not a deficit — a speaker who shifts between home language and
formal language is doing something *harder*, not something lesser.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: compare how different
cultures mark respect in language, and let learners teach the class a form of address
from their own language. The **egalitarian** lens: the "formal" register of a society is
often the register of its powerful — and demanding respect for *every* way of speaking
is a small act of justice. The **social-emotional** lens: anxiety about "getting the
register right" is real, especially for learners speaking a second language or an
unfamiliar dialect; normalize the wobble. The **ethics** lens: matching register is a
way of honoring the other person; misjudging it is usually awkwardness, not insult, and
can be repaired. The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *why* a setting calls for this
register — who set the rule, and whom does it include or exclude? Preview: Lesson 5
turns register into delivery — speaking with confidence to an unfamiliar audience.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of the last time you spoke to three very different people
   in one day. How did your voice change for each? That change has a name today:
   register.
2. **Meet the spectrum (15 min).** Look at the register strip — a line from "close and
   intimate" on one end to "formal and public" on the other. Place these settings on
   the line: home with family, the market, the classroom, a wedding or ceremony, a
   doctor's office, a court or a job interview. Watch one worked example: the request
   "please help me" said five ways — a whisper to a sibling, a call to a friend, a
   polite ask to a neighbor, a careful request to an official, a formal plea at a
   hearing. Notice what changes: words, titles, tone, volume, even the grammar.
3. **Find the moves (10 min).** On the "one message, five ways" sheet, circle the
   words that shift between registers. Underline any form of address — a name, a title,
   a polite word. Ask: what does each choice tell the listener about how I see them?
4. **Write five ways (15 min).** Choose your own message — asking for help, saying
   thank you, or disagreeing politely — and write it for five different settings. Try
   at least one in your home language and one in a second language you know.
5. **Reflect (7 min).** In your journal: *the register I find hardest is ___, because
   ___; one form of address my culture or family uses is ___, and it shows respect by
   ___.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: matching your voice to the room is respect made
   audible. Next time, you'll *deliver* that voice to a real, unfamiliar audience.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide three pre-written versions of one message and ask the learner
  only to place them on the spectrum and explain one difference.
- **Extension:** Research an honorific system (e.g., titles, polite verb forms, or
  kinship address) in a language you know or are learning, and show how grammar itself
  marks respect.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner place five settings on the register spectrum
  and re-write one message for two of them, naming what changed?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The "one message, five ways" sheet, added to the
  portfolio as the register entry for the public-speaking thread.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *how do you change the way you speak between home, work, and a
formal occasion?* Listen for one word or title they switch — and ask who taught them.
