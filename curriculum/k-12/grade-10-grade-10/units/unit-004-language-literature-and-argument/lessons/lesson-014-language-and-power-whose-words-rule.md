# Lesson 14 — Language and Power: Whose Words Rule?

## Summary

Learners **analyze how language and power interact**, examining law, medicine, and
school to see whose language is used, whose is left out, and what that decides about
who is heard, trusted, and included. They connect this to home language, multilingualism
as wealth, and the idea of language justice — holding the analysis as description plus
values, not a single verdict.

## Objectives

- Analyze how language and power interact, such as whose language is used in law,
  medicine, and school. _(D04.S4.10.01)_

## Connection

Stand where you cannot understand the words that decide things about you — a clinic
form, a court notice, a school rule — and you feel it: language is not only for
talking, it is for *gatekeeping*. Whoever sets the language sets who is at home in the
room and who is a stranger in it. In a hospital, a courtroom, or a classroom, the
language that is "official" is often one group's language, and everyone else must
translate themselves into it just to be heard. Naming that — seeing *whose* words rule —
is the first step toward a world where every language gets to be the language of power
and care.

## Materials

- Language-and-power map (law / medicine / school)
- Short example texts (optional)
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the map and any example texts (keep them short and real — a legal notice, a
  medical instruction, a school notice in formal or technical language).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall register and formality. Today we ask who *set* the
  register, and whom it excludes.
- Prepare one worked example: read one formal notice and translate it into plain home
  language, noticing the gap.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **language carries
power — in law, medicine, and school, the language that is official, technical, or
"standard" is one group's language, and everyone else must translate themselves into it;
so language is a gate that includes some and excludes others, and noticing it is the
first step toward language justice.** This is analysis *plus* values: the factual claim
is that official and technical language privileges certain speakers; the value is that
every language deserves to be a language of power and care. The curriculum holds
multilingualism as wealth and home language first (S-019): globally, about 40 per cent
of people do not have access to education in a language they speak or understand
(S-019). Writers from colonized contexts have named this directly — Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o
argues that being made to write in the colonizer's language distanced him from his home
world (S-424), and Gloria Anzaldúa describes living between languages and the demand to
"tame" one's tongue (S-425) — present these as *lived arguments* to reflect on, not
verdicts to adopt. Languages shape habitual thought in moderate, well-supported ways
(the Sapir–Whorf tradition, held in its moderate form, S-168), which is *why* whose
language is used matters.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **egalitarian** lens is the spine: ask of every setting
*who is at home here and who is made a stranger?* The **ethics** lens: using jargon to
keep people out is a quiet harm; making communication accessible is a quiet good. The
**global** lens: official-language policies differ worldwide, and multilingual states,
Indigenous language revitalization, and everyday code-switching all show there is no
single "right" arrangement — describe many, rank none. The **technology** lens: machine
translation and voice tools can *lower* the gate — but they are uneven, often favoring
dominant languages, and can reproduce exclusion (S-281). The **critical-thinking** lens:
distinguish the factual claim (this language is privileged here) from the value (that is
unjust, and here is what would be fairer). Preview: Lesson 15 closes the unit with
politeness and address — the everyday face of the same question.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4, who sets the "formal" register in a room? Today
   you ask that question of the three biggest rooms: law, medicine, and school.
2. **Meet the map (15 min).** Look at the language-and-power map: three columns — law,
   medicine, school — and two questions for each: **whose language is used here?** and
   **who is left out?** Watch a worked example: read a formal notice together, then say
   it in plain home language. Notice the gap — the formal version is not "clearer," it
   is *someone's* language wearing the clothes of authority.
3. **Fill the columns (15 min).** For each setting, write whose language is used
   (official, technical, "standard"), who is at home in it, and who must translate
   themselves. Draw on real examples you have seen or heard.
4. **Hear the lived arguments (10 min).** Learn how writers have named this: Ngũgĩ wa
   Thiong'o, made to write in a colonizer's language, felt distanced from his home world
   (S-424); Gloria Anzaldúa wrote of living between languages and the demand to tame her
   tongue (S-425). In your journal, write one sentence on your own experience — including
   being monolingual, if that is you — of feeling at home or a stranger in a language.
5. **Name the value (7 min).** Separate fact from value: it is a *fact* that some
   languages are privileged in these rooms; it is a *value* that every language deserves
   to be a language of power and care. Write one sentence of each.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: language is a gate, and you now know how to see it. Next
   time, we practice the everyday courtesy that can open a small door in any of these
   rooms.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one setting (school) and use a provided plain-language
  translation to compare; name one person who is left out.
- **Extension:** Analyze a real language policy (an official-language law or a
  medium-of-instruction rule) for its effects on equity and inclusion, and argue for one
  change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name whose language is used in one of the three
  settings, who is left out, and distinguish the factual claim from the value judgment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The language-and-power map plus the fact/value
  sentences, added to the portfolio as the "language-and-power analysis."

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *have you ever been in a clinic, a court, or a school where the
language used was not yours — and how did it feel?* Listen for one moment where being
heard mattered, and how the language helped or blocked it.
