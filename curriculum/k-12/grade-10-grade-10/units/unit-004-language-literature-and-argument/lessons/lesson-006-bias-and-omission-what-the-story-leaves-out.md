# Lesson 6 — Bias and Omission: What the Story Leaves Out

## Summary

Learners **evaluate media for bias and omission** using five core questions — who made
this, what is emphasized, what is left out, who benefits, and what is the evidence.
They apply the questions to two accounts of one event, then to one real item of their
own choosing, learning that bias often lives in what is *not* said as much as in what
is.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate digital and print media for bias, omission, and the difference
  between evidence and belief — beginning with *framing and omission*. _(D04.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Two people can tell you about the same market-day argument and leave you with two
different truths — not because either lied, but because each chose what to emphasize
and what to skip. The same happens with the news and the feed: every headline, photo,
and clip is a *selection*, made by someone, for someone. A story that is "true" in its
facts can still mislead by what it leads with, who it quotes, and what it never
mentions. Learning to ask "what is left out?" is a superpower you carry from the
market stall to the newsroom to your own phone.

## Materials

- Media-evaluation sheet (five questions)
- Two short news items on the same event
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and select two short, age-appropriate accounts of one event (same
  facts, different framing — e.g., a local dispute, a sporting decision, a policy
  change).
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–3, recall reading for *appeal* and *claim vs. evidence*.
  Today we read for *selection* — what the maker chose to show and hide.
- Prepare one worked example: mark the first account's emphasis and one omission.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **every media message
is a selection made by someone for an audience; bias shows up as emphasis, framing, and
especially omission — so the reader's job is to ask the five questions, not to hunt for
a perfectly neutral outlet.** The five questions follow established media-literacy
frameworks: the Center for Media Literacy's five core concepts (all media are
constructed; media use creative language; different people experience the same message
differently; media embed values and points of view; most media seek profit and/or
power) (S-442); NAMLE's key questions (who created this, why, what techniques are used,
how might others read it, what is omitted, who benefits) (S-151); and UNESCO's media
and information literacy framing of access, evaluate, and engage (S-217). Omission is
the subtle one: a report can be factually accurate and still mislead by what it never
says. Note that even bias can be automatic rather than deliberate — people (and
outlets) absorb the angles of their surroundings (S-344) — so the lesson asks learners
to *catch* bias, not to assume bad faith.

Model the five questions once, then let learners work (worked example → guided practice
→ independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: ask the five
questions of every item, habitually. The **technology** lens: the feed is a *designed
selection* — algorithms choose what to show, and concentrated ownership shapes what is
reported at all (S-481; S-281) — so "what is left out" is partly engineered. The
**egalitarian** lens: whose voices get quoted, and whose are omitted, is a question of
power — omission is often how the less powerful are kept out of the story. The
**ethics** lens: fair reporting means checking facts, naming sources, and representing
sides fairly — an ideal to hold, not a claim that any outlet is perfect. The **global**
lens: media differ across countries and languages, and no single outlet speaks for the
world; read across borders when you can. Preview: Lesson 7 sharpens the last question —
evidence versus belief.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what makes a critique *fair*? Today you turn that
   fairness on the news itself — asking what a story shows, and what it hides.
2. **Meet the five questions (10 min).** Look at the media-evaluation sheet. The five
   questions: **who made this?** **what is emphasized?** **what is left out?** **who
   benefits?** **what is the evidence?** Watch a worked example: mark the first news
   item's headline, its first sentence, who it quotes, and one thing it never mentions.
3. **Compare two accounts (15 min).** Read the second account of the same event. Fill
   the sheet for both. Ask: where do they differ? What did each leave out? Which
   details lead, and why might that be?
4. **Find the omission (10 min).** The hardest question is "what is left out." For each
   account, write one thing a person would *not* know if they read only that one.
5. **Evaluate your own (10 min).** Choose one real news item or post you saw this week.
   Run the five questions on it. Write one sentence naming its angle — and one sentence
   naming its omission.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: no story is complete; the reader's job is to notice the
   seams. Next time, we separate the last question — evidence from belief — on its own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two accounts with the leading detail already underlined;
  ask only "what did each leave out?"
- **Extension:** Look up who owns or funds the outlet behind one item, and argue how
  that ownership might shape what it covers or omits (S-281).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name what one account emphasizes and one thing
  it omits, and state who might benefit from that framing?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The media-evaluation sheet for one real item, added to
  the portfolio as the "bias and omission" entry.

## Home connection

Watch or read one news item with an adult you trust, then each of you write down one
thing the story left out. Compare — did you notice the same gap?
