# Lesson 11 — Crafting Prose: Sentences and Word Choice

## Summary

Learners **craft prose with deliberate style**, beginning with the sentence. They map a
paragraph's rhythm — short, medium, and long sentences — vary it on purpose, and
sharpen word choice for precision and connotation. They revise a draft twice and
explain each change, treating style as a set of *choices* rather than a fixed talent.

## Objectives

- Craft prose with deliberate style, varying sentence structure and word choice to suit
  purpose and audience — beginning with the sentence itself. _(D04.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Read these two lines aloud: "The fire was out. The house was cold. We waited." Now:
"The fire had long since gone out, and the house had settled into the kind of cold that
seeps into your bones, so we waited." Same facts, two rhythms — the first short and
hard, the second long and sinking. Writers do this on purpose: sentence length is a
tool, like a drummer's beat, and word choice is a tool, like a cook's spice. Whether you
are writing a letter, a post, a story, or an argument you want someone to actually read,
the same question stands: *what should this sentence do, and how can I make it do it?*

## Materials

- Sentence-rhythm strip
- Word-choice ladder
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy the rhythm strip and word-choice ladder.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall writing with precise language and well-chosen details
  (D04.S3.07.01). Today we make the *sentence* the unit of craft.
- Prepare one worked example: a flat paragraph to revise aloud, once for rhythm and once
  for word choice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **style is choice, not
talent — sentence length creates rhythm and emphasis (short sentences land hard; long
ones carry and build), and word choice controls precision and connotation (what a word
means, and what it makes a reader feel).** This is the unit's writing-craft thread
(Lessons 11–12). Effective adolescent writing is taught with explicit strategies —
models, sentence-level practice, and revision — not left to intuition (S-102; the
writing process and explicit strategy instruction are the evidence-backed core). A
worked example of revision is the key move: show a flat sentence become a crafted one,
and name *why* each change works.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **intellectual** lens is the spine: revision is where
thinking happens — each change is a decision with a reason. The **critical-thinking**
lens: ask of every sentence "what is it doing, and is that what I want?" The **ethics**
lens: precise, honest words serve truth; inflated or evasive words can be used to
deceive — craft carries responsibility. The **egalitarian** lens: every learner's
home-language way of putting things is a resource, not an error — style may vary across
languages and dialects, and none is "broken." The **global** lens: sentence craft and
word-choice art exist in every written and oral tradition — from a Bashō haiku's
compression (S-351) to a griot's performance — and learners may draw on any of them.
Preview: Lesson 12 turns from the sentence to *tone* — matching word and sentence to
purpose and audience.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9, what did you learn about choosing precise words?
   Today you make the sentence itself the thing you craft.
2. **Hear the rhythm (15 min).** Look at the sentence-rhythm strip. A paragraph's
   rhythm comes from sentence length: **short** sentences land hard and set emphasis;
   **medium** ones carry the middle; **long** ones build and flow. Watch a worked
   example: read a flat paragraph, then a revised one that mixes lengths on purpose.
   Clap the beats — one clap per word — to *feel* the difference.
3. **Map and vary your own (15 min).** Take a paragraph you have already written. Map
   its sentence lengths on the strip. Then revise it: add one short punch sentence where
   you want emphasis, and one long flowing sentence where you want the reader to feel
   carried. Read both aloud.
4. **Climb the word ladder (12 min).** Look at the word-choice ladder: a plain word, a
   more precise one, a vivid one — and note each word's **connotation** (what it makes a
   reader feel). Replace three words in your paragraph with sharper choices, and write
   *why* each new word earns its place.
5. **Explain your craft (5 min).** In your journal: *I changed ___ to ___ because ___;
   the short sentence I added lands on the idea of ___.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: a sentence is a tool, and you are the one holding it.
   Next time, you match that tool to a purpose and an audience.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-written paragraph and ask for one rhythm change and one
  word swap, with sentence starters for the "why."
- **Extension:** Revise a paragraph three ways for three different purposes (to alarm,
  to reassure, to persuade), changing both rhythm and word choice each time.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner map a paragraph's rhythm, revise it for length
  and word choice, and explain two specific changes?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The "before and after" paragraph with explanations,
  added to the portfolio as the first styled-prose entry.

## Home connection

Read your revised paragraph aloud to someone at home, first the "before," then the
"after." Ask them which sentence stuck with them — that is your rhythm working.
