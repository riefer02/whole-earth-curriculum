# Lesson 1 — The Three Appeals — and Many More

## Summary

Learners meet **rhetoric** as the art of persuasion and sort persuasive moves into
three appeals: **ethos** (the speaker's character), **pathos** (the audience's
feeling), and **logos** (the reasoning) (S-346). They learn these three as *one*
tradition among many arts of speaking worldwide, and close by finding one example of
each appeal in a voice they already know.

## Objectives

- Critique rhetoric in public speech and media, distinguishing emotional appeal from
  reasoned argument — beginning by *naming* the three appeals a speaker can make.
  _(D04.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last time someone really moved you to say yes — a friend who talked you
into a plan, a grandparent who settled a family argument, a speaker at a ceremony, an
ad that made you want something. Each of them persuaded you somehow. One leaned on
who they are to you (you trust them), one reached your feelings, and one gave you
reasons. Whether the voice is a market seller in a busy city or an elder under a
village tree, the same three levers are at work: **character, feeling, and reason**.
Learning to see those levers — in your own language first, then in any language — is
the first step to being persuaded *on purpose* instead of by accident.

## Materials

- Three-appeals chart (ethos / pathos / logos)
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-column chart.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9, recall how a speaker's word choice, tone, and delivery
  shape meaning and persuade (D04.S1.08.01). Today we name the *kinds* of appeal
  those choices make.
- Prepare one worked example — a short, familiar request said three ways.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **persuasion works
through three appeals — ethos (character), pathos (feeling), and logos (reasoning) —
named in the ancient Greek tradition (S-346), and these are one tradition among many;
every culture has its own arts of speaking, and none is the "right" one.** Aristotle's
*Rhetoric* (c. 350 BCE) organized persuasion into these three appeals — the speaker's
character (êthos), the audience's emotional state (pathos), and the argument itself
(logos) (S-346). Teach this as *description*, not as a universal law of all speaking:
West African griots (jeli/jali) carry genealogy and history through oral performance
(S-446); the Māori whaikōrero is a formal, structured oratory; the Indian nyāya
tradition built careful rules of debate; the Islamic khuṭba addresses a congregation
in its own register. Name these as *different arts with different strengths*, held
side by side — never ranked.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: read *which*
lever a speaker pulls and ask whether it carries the load or hides a missing reason.
The **global** lens: treat the three appeals as one (ancient Greek) frame among many
rhetorical traditions, and invite learners to name the speaking arts of their own
community. The **ethics** lens: persuasion can serve care or harm — the same three
levers build a fair argument or a con. The **egalitarian** lens: whose voices get to
persuade publicly, and whose are kept out, is a question of power, not skill alone
(S-281). The **social-emotional** lens: noticing when a feeling is being stirred *in
you* is a kind of self-awareness; invite, never force, sharing. Preview: Lesson 2
turns to the heart of the unit — telling emotional appeal apart from reasoned
argument.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back to Grade 9: how did you know a speaker was trying to
   persuade you? (Their word choice, their tone, how they delivered it.) Today we name
   the *kinds* of persuasion behind those choices.
2. **Meet the three appeals (15 min).** Look at the three-appeals chart. A speaker
   persuades in three ways: **ethos** — you trust *who is speaking*; **pathos** — you
   are moved by *what you feel*; **logos** — you are convinced by *the reasoning
   itself*. Watch one worked example: a single request ("come help carry the water")
   said three ways — as a trusted friend (ethos), with warmth and urgency (pathos),
   and with a reasoned case ("if we share it, it's lighter for everyone") (logos).
   Label each.
3. **Sort real examples (15 min).** In your journal, write one sentence you remember
   hearing that persuaded you — from a speech, an ad, a family talk, or a song. Decide:
   which lever did it pull most? Could it be more than one? Put it in a column, and
   note why.
4. **See it is one tradition among many (10 min).** Learn that "ethos, pathos, logos"
   comes from one ancient culture's study of speaking (S-346). Many other peoples have
   their own arts: griots who carry history in performance (S-446), formal Māori
   oratory, Indian debate rules, the khuṭba of a mosque. Write down the name of one
   speaking art from your own community or family — the way an elder opens a
   gathering, the way people argue well where you are from.
5. **Draft your map (7 min).** For each appeal, write one real example from your own
   life in your home language, then try one sentence in a second language you know.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: persuasion is a craft, and you can read it. Next time,
   we separate the feeling from the reasoning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a short list of sentences to sort into the three columns, and a
  starter ("This speaker leans on ___ because ___.").
- **Extension:** Find a recorded speech and label one moment of ethos, one of pathos,
  and one of logos, then say which is strongest and whether that is a strength or a
  warning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner sort a persuasive sentence into ethos, pathos,
  or logos and explain why?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-appeals chart with one example of each
  appeal, added to the unit portfolio as the "rhetoric toolkit" opener.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *who is the best speaker you have ever heard, and what made
their words work?* Sort their answer together into character, feeling, or reasoning —
and notice which lever your family values most.
