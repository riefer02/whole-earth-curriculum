# Lesson 15 — Politeness and Address Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners **communicate respectfully in a multicultural setting**. They map how
different cultures greet, address, and show respect, learn a "respectful check" of
questions to ask rather than assume, and practice adapting their own politeness. They
close the unit by reflecting that no culture's way is the only "right" one — respect is
learned, not assumed.

## Objectives

- Communicate respectfully in a multicultural setting, adapting to different cultural
  norms of politeness and address. _(D04.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

The same small act — how you greet someone — carries completely different meanings in
different places. In one culture you bow and use a title; in another you shake hands
and use a first name; in a third, the *order* of greeting, who speaks first, is what
shows respect. Get it "wrong" and you have not just stumbled over words — you may have
signaled disrespect you did not mean. Get it right, and you have said, in the other
person's own idiom, *I see you.* No single culture owns politeness; what is shared is
the *intention* — and the skill of asking, watching, and adapting, so that respect lands
the way you meant it to.

## Materials

- Politeness-and-address grid
- "Respectful check" card
- Reading journal

## Preparation

- Copy the grid and the respectful-check card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall register and address; from Lesson 14, recall that
  language carries power. Today you practice the everyday courtesy that opens doors
  across that difference.
- Prepare one worked example: greet and address a partner two ways, naming what each
  signals.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **politeness and
address are cultural norms — how we greet, title, and show respect differs across
cultures and languages, often inside the grammar (honorifics, polite forms); the skill
is to watch, ask, and adapt, never to rank one culture's way above another.** Register
and formality (S-237) are the shared machinery here: every language and culture draws
the close-to-formal line, but *where* and *how* differ. Honorific systems — titles,
polite verb endings, kinship forms of address — mark respect inside grammar in many
languages; a learner who knows one such system has something to teach the room. Hold the
value plainly: no culture's politeness is the "correct" one, and respect is an intention
made visible in the other person's idiom.

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: the room itself is a
multicultural setting — let learners teach their own culture's forms and learn one
another's. The **ethics** lens: adapting politeness is honoring the other person; the
"respectful check" (ask, don't assume) is the honest guard against stereotyping a whole
culture from one example. The **egalitarian** lens: no accent, dialect, or politeness
norm is "less polite" — difference is not deficit, and a learner who does not yet know a
norm is not failing, they are learning. The **social-emotional** lens: getting a norm
wrong can feel mortifying — normalize the wobble, and teach repair ("I did not know —
thank you for showing me"). The **critical-thinking** lens: ask *what* a form of address
is doing (respect? distance? warmth? hierarchy?) before imitating it, so adaptation is
thoughtful, not mimicry. This is the unit's closing lesson: the rhetorical critique,
the public voice, the careful reading, the crafted prose, the accessible design, and
the analysis of power all land here, in how one person speaks to another.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Across this unit you critiqued rhetoric, spoke to strangers,
   read whole books, crafted prose, designed accessibly, and traced language's power.
   Today it all lands in one place: how you speak to a person.
2. **Meet the grid (15 min).** Look at the politeness-and-address grid. For one
   relationship or setting — greeting an elder, asking a favor, disagreeing politely —
   write how *your* culture greets, addresses, and shows respect. Watch a worked
   example: greet and address a partner two ways, naming what each signals.
3. **Add a second culture (15 min).** In the next row, write how *another* culture you
   know — or one a classmate teaches you — does the same three things. Notice what
   differs: the words, the title or name, the bow or handshake, the order of speaking,
   even the grammar (some languages build respect into the verb itself).
4. **Learn the respectful check (10 min).** Read the "respectful check" card: before
   speaking across a culture, ask — *how is respect shown here? how should I address
   you? what should I ask rather than assume?* Practice it with a partner: ask how they
   prefer to be addressed, and honor the answer.
5. **Reflect (7 min).** In your journal: *one form of address my culture or family uses
   is ___, and it shows respect by ___; one thing I will ask rather than assume in a new
   setting is ___.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: respect is an intention made visible in the other
   person's own way of being spoken to. You leave this unit able to hear a speech with a
   critic's ear, speak with your own voice, read a whole book, write a sentence that
   does what you mean, build a message that reaches everyone, and greet a stranger with
   care. That is language, full strength.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one relationship (greeting an elder) and one culture; practice a
  single greeting and form of address with a partner.
- **Extension:** Compare honorific systems in three languages (titles, polite verb
  forms, kinship address), and write a short guide a newcomer could use to show respect
  in your own community.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + performance):** Can the learner name how two cultures differ in
  greeting and address, use the respectful check, and adapt one form of politeness to a
  partner?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The politeness-and-address grid plus the reflection,
  added to the portfolio as the "multicultural communication" closing entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust to teach you one form of address your family or community uses
to show respect — and when it is used. Write it down, then try using it with someone
who will appreciate it.
