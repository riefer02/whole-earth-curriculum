# Lesson 4 — From My Doorstep to the Whole World

## Summary

Learners explain how their local community connects to regional, national, and global
ones by tracing a single real thing — a food, a song, a person, a weather pattern —
through the nested circles of belonging. They locate themselves in that chain not as a
small dot at the bottom of a ladder, but as one living point in a web where every
level is connected, and where they have a place to stand and act. This is the
D02.S3.10.02 artifact.

## Objectives

- Explain how my local community connects to regional, national, and global ones, and
  name where I stand within those nested communities. _(D02.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

Lift almost anything near you and ask where it came from. The rice or maize on a table,
the song someone hums, the rain falling, the medicine in a cupboard, the news on a
phone — each one is a thread that runs from a doorstep to a region, a nation, and the
world. You are not standing outside that web looking in; you are already woven into it.
This lesson follows a few of your own threads, so you can see the whole cloth and your
place in it.

## Materials

- Nested-communities graphic (SVG)
- Writing journal or portfolio

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: earlier this year (Unit 3, Lesson 10), learners traced their street to
  the world. Today we deepen it into a celebration of belonging in nested communities.
- Prepare one worked example of tracing a single object through the chain.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **communities are
nested — local, regional, national, global — and they connect in both directions, so a
learner stands inside them, not beneath them.** "Nested communities, from family to
the global community of all life" is the Grade-12 summit of this strand; here we build
its foundation. A nation is, in Benedict Anderson's now-classic phrase, an "imagined
community" — its members will never meet most of their fellow-members, yet the image
of their communion lives in their minds (S-415). Held as description, not insult:
"imagined" does not mean unreal.

The **global/anthropological** lens is central: no community form is the default, and
none ranks above another — some lives are organized around a village, some around a
diaspora, some around a chosen family; the nested model is one map, not the only one.
The **egalitarian** lens: the chain is not a ladder with the world "above" the street;
power and resources move unevenly along it, and noticing who is connected and who is
left out is part of reading it honestly. The **ethics** lens: seeing the chain means
seeing that my choices ripple outward — a purchase, a vote, a kindness. The
**environment/technology** lens: the clearest living example of the chain is the Earth
itself — weather, water, and species cross every human border, and the technologies
that move food, goods, and messages are the threads that tie the circles together.
Distinguish evidence from values: "communities connect from local to global" is
descriptive; "every point in the web has dignity and a place to act" is a value.

Model tracing one object once (worked example, S-011), then let learners trace their
own.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** Name the communities you belong to — family, street,
   neighborhood, school, a faith or cultural group, an online group. Which is the
   smallest, and which reaches farthest?
2. **See the circles (8 min).** Look at the nested-communities graphic. The arrows run
   both ways: your street sends things out to the world, and the world sends things
   back to your street. A chain, not a ladder.
3. **Trace one thread (20 min).** Choose one real thing near you — a food you ate this
   week, a song, an object, a person's journey, a weather pattern. Trace it backward:
   where did it come from, what region, what nation, what part of the world? Then trace
   it forward: how did it arrive here? Write or sketch the chain.
4. **Share a surprising link (8 min).** With a partner, share the most surprising step
   in your chain. Ask each other: who in this chain might be invisible, and where is
   the strongest connection?
5. **Close (7 min).** Finish this sentence and write it in your portfolio: "I stand
   inside a web that reaches from ___ to ___, and one way I touch it is ___."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn chain with four empty circles and a word bank
  (local, regional, national, global); the learner fills in one example.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the nested circles aloud — me inside
  region, region inside nation, nation inside world, with two-way arrows — and offer
  four rings drawn in the dirt or four bowls nested one inside the next so the nesting
  can be felt.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Trace the chain by placing four
  objects or cards in nested order and pointing from local to global; the closing
  thought may be a drawing or a chosen symbol.
- **Extension:** Trace a second direction — a global event (a storm, a price change, a
  piece of news) and how it reaches your street — then compare the two directions.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner trace one real connection from local to
  global in both directions and name their own place in the chain?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The traced chain with the closing sentence, as the
  community artifact.

## Home connection

Ask an elder or neighbor where one everyday thing on your street comes from — the
water, the market goods, a family's journey. Add what they tell you to your chain.
