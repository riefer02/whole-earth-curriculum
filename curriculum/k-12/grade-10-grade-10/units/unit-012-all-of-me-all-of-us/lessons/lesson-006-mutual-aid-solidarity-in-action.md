# Lesson 6 — Mutual Aid: Solidarity in Action

## Summary

Learners examine mutual-aid networks across the world — from named traditions of
communal work to modern solidarity networks — and distinguish mutual aid from charity.
The core insight is **where the power sits**: charity is a one-way gift in which the
giver keeps the power; mutual aid is a circle of equals who give and receive and decide
together. This completes the D02.S4.10.02 capstone artifact.

## Objectives

- Examine examples of mutual-aid networks across the world and explain how mutual aid
  differs from charity by where the power and the deciding sit. _(D02.S4.10.02)_

## Connection

When a neighbor's roof is damaged, one response is charity: a better-off person gives
money, keeps the deciding, and the neighbor receives. Another response is mutual aid:
the whole street gathers — one brings tiles, one brings a ladder, one cooks, and the
neighbor gives back in turn when the next roof fails. Both repair the roof, but they
feel and work differently, because in the second the deciding and the dignity stay
with everyone. Mutual aid is not a new or distant invention; it is one of the oldest
human shapes, alive on every continent under many names.

## Materials

- Mutual-aid circle graphic (SVG)
- Writing journal or portfolio

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from Unit 3 (Lesson 12), the charity–mutual aid–cooperative spectrum;
  from earlier grades, examples of mutual aid across cultures and times (harambee,
  gotong royong, mink'a/minga, barn raising). Today we deepen the charity vs. solidarity
  distinction.
- Prepare the contrast: charity (one-way gift, power kept) vs. mutual aid (equals
  giving, receiving, and deciding together).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **mutual aid is
people meeting one another's needs together as equals — sharing both the giving and the
deciding — which is what separates it from charity, where the giver keeps the power.**
Dean Spade's *Mutual Aid* (S-345) makes this distinction precisely, and it is a
*descriptive* distinction: saying they differ is not saying "charity is bad." The term
"mutual aid" was popularized by Peter Kropotkin's 1902 *Mutual Aid: A Factor of
Evolution*, a collection of accounts of cooperation and reciprocity across human and
animal societies (S-163). Named traditions of communal work span the world: harambee
(Swahili, "all pull together," East Africa), gotong royong (Indonesia), mink'a/minga
(the Andes), and barn raising (North America), among many others (S-164) — present
each as one named tradition in one place, never as "all people there do this," and
invite learners to verify local details with their own community.

The **egalitarian** lens is central: who keeps the power, and who is treated as a full
equal, is the difference between the two forms. The **ethics** lens: dignity matters as
much as the roof. The **global/anthropological** lens: these traditions are not ranked
against one another and not a Western export — they are living practices on every
continent. The **technology** lens: modern mutual-aid networks often run on group
messages and shared documents — technology can coordinate solidarity across distance,
or it can be used to surveil and sort the "deserving" from the "undeserving." Distinguish
evidence from values: "mutual aid traditions are widespread and documented" is
descriptive; "sharing the deciding is a fairer way to help" is a value learners weigh.

Model the contrast once (worked example, S-011), then let learners examine examples.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** Think of a time neighbors or friends helped each other with no
   one in charge and no one being paid. Who decided how the help flowed? Say it in one
   sentence.
2. **See the circle (8 min).** Look at the mutual-aid graphic: everyone gives, everyone
   receives, and the arrows run both ways. Compare it to a one-way gift. Where does the
   power sit in each?
3. **Examine real examples (20 min).** Choose two. **Named traditions:** harambee in
   East Africa, gotong royong in Indonesia, mink'a/minga in the Andes, or barn raising
   in North America (S-164). **Modern networks:** neighborhood solidarity groups,
   disaster-response networks, or food-sharing circles. For each, write: who gives, who
   decides, and how the help flows back.
4. **Distinguish from charity (8 min).** Draw two columns: "charity" and "mutual aid."
   Place each example, and note who keeps the power. Remember: naming the difference is
   not saying charity is bad — it is saying the two are not the same.
5. **Close (7 min).** In your portfolio, write one sentence: "Mutual aid means ___, and
   what makes it mutual is ___."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fill-in frame — "In ___, people ___ together, and the help
  flows ___ instead of only one way."
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the graphic aloud — five people in a
  circle, each passing something forward with arrows flowing both ways — and offer a
  real circle where the group passes one object in both directions so reciprocity can
  be felt.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Sort pre-printed example cards
  into "charity" and "mutual aid" piles by placing or pointing, with a partner reading
  each card aloud.
- **Extension:** Analyze one example for how it keeps power with the people — who is
  invited to decide, and how the flow returns — and argue why that matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name a mutual-aid example, explain who
  gives/decides, and distinguish it from charity by where the power sits?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The examples-and-contrast page, as the second half of
  the mutual-aid artifact.

## Home connection

Ask an elder about a tradition of communal help they know — a harvest, a wedding, a
recovery, a shared task — and how the help flowed back. Add it to your examples.
