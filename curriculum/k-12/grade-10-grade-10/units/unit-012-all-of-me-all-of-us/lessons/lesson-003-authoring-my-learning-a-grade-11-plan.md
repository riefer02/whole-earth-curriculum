# Lesson 3 — Authoring My Learning: A Grade-11 Plan

## Summary

Learners practice directing their own learning by building a four-step plan for
Grade 11: **choose a goal, find resources, check progress, adjust.** They learn that
self-direction is a loop they steer, not a single heroic leap — and that "not yet" is
information, never a verdict. This is the D01.S3.10.01 artifact of the capstone.

## Objectives

- Direct my own learning by choosing a goal, resources, and ways to check my own
  progress, with an adjustment ready in advance. _(D01.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last thing you learned without anyone assigning it — a recipe, a trick on
a bike, a song, a game, a language phrase. You did not wait for permission; you picked
a goal, found something to learn from, and checked whether it worked. That is
self-direction, and you already do it. This lesson names that skill and puts it on a
four-step loop, so you can steer a whole year of learning — into Grade 11 and beyond —
on purpose instead of by accident.

## Materials

- Learning-cycle graphic (SVG)
- A one-page learning plan

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from earlier this year (Unit 1, Lesson 10), learners set goals,
  chose resources, and checked progress. Today we formalize it into one loop and point
  it at Grade 11.
- Prepare one worked example of the four-step loop.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **self-directed
learning is a four-step loop — choose a goal, find resources, check progress, adjust —
and the learner is the author at every step.** Goal-setting is well-evidenced
(Locke & Latham, S-223): specific, checkable goals outperform vague wishes. Checking
progress (self-testing) is one of the strongest learning techniques we know
(Dunlosky et al., S-039; Roediger & Karpicke, S-228). The "adjust" step is where
mastery lives: "not yet" is information, never a verdict (Dweck, S-096 — hold the
growth-mindset claim honestly; effects are real but modest, and effort is not a
guarantee).

The **ethics** lens: a plan you can check honestly is a plan you can be trusted with.
The **egalitarian** lens: resources are not evenly distributed — a plan that names a
fallback (a person, a free library, a neighbor's skill) works for more lives than one
that assumes money and devices. The **global** lens: self-paced, low-resource
schooling (Escuela Nueva, S-008) and teaching at the learner's level (TaRL, S-009)
show that self-direction works across very different places — it is not a privilege of
the few. The **technology** lens can appear briefly: a device is one resource among
many, never the only one. Distinguish evidence from values: the four-step loop and the
power of checking progress are descriptive; "every learner deserves to steer their own
learning" is a value we hold.

Model the loop once (worked example, S-011), then let learners plan their own. Keep it
agentic and hopeful.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** When you learned something on your own this year, what did you
   do first, and how did you know it was working? Name the steps you already use —
   today we give them names.
2. **Meet the loop (8 min).** Look at the learning-cycle graphic. The four steps —
   choose a goal, find resources, check progress, adjust and go on — repeat, like
   walking. A goal that cannot be checked is only a wish; a check tells you the truth
   so you can steer.
3. **Author your plan (20 min).** On your one-page plan, write: **(a) Goal** — one
   specific thing you want to learn in Grade 11, phrased so you could tell if you got
   there. **(b) Resources** — at least two: a person, a place, a book, a tool. Name a
   fallback if the first fails. **(c) Progress check** — how will you know it is
   working? A milestone, a small test, a thing you can do or show. **(d) Adjustment** —
   what you will change if the check says "not yet."
4. **Peer-check (8 min).** Trade plans with a partner. Ask them one question: "How
   will you know it is working?" Help them make the check specific.
5. **Close (7 min).** Read your plan once and circle the step you feel strongest in.
   Put it in your closing portfolio — it is your Grade-11 letter to yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence starter for each step ("By the end of Grade 11 I
  want to…", "I can learn from…", "I will know it is working when…", "If not, I will…").
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the loop aloud — choose a goal, find
  resources, check progress, adjust — and offer four folded-paper panels or cards the
  learner can touch in order.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Build the plan with four cards or
  found objects placed in order and pointed to; a partner reads each label and the
  learner confirms with a nod, gesture, or pointing.
- **Extension:** Add a second milestone and a note on how you will seek help at the
  right moment (balancing independence with asking — the thread begun in Unit 1,
  Lesson 11).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner state a specific goal, two resources
  with a fallback, a concrete progress check, and an adjustment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-page Grade-11 learning plan, as the
  self-direction artifact of the capstone.

## Home connection

Show your plan to an adult and ask them to be one of your resources — or to name
someone they know who could help. A plan with a person in it travels further than a
plan alone.
