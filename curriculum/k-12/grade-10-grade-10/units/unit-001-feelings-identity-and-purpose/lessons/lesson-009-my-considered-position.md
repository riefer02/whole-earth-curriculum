# Lesson 9 — My Considered Position

## Summary

Learners complete their **considered position on what defines them**, independent of
external labels and expectations. They build it from three pillars — **their values,
their story, and their choices** — support it with **evidence** from their own life, and
hold it as **theirs to revise**, not a finished verdict. This is the unit's
identity-writing artifact and a direct enactment of D01.S2.10.02.

## Objectives

- Develop a considered position on what defines them, independent of external labels
  and expectations. _(D01.S2.10.02)_

## Connection

Everyone lives with a version of the question "who am I, really?" — but few stop to
answer it on purpose, in their own words, and keep the answer. A **considered position**
is just that: a statement you have actually *considered* — looked at, tested against
your life, and written down — rather than a label you inherited or a mood you happened
into. It does not have to be final; in fact, the strongest positions are the ones their
authors know how to revise. What matters is that it is *yours*.

## Materials

- Position scaffold (what defines me — values, story, choices — my evidence, the part I am still deciding)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the position scaffold.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 6–8, recall the messages others send, the labels they attach,
  and the truths you know beneath them. Today we assemble the *positive* answer: not
  what I am not, but what I am.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a considered position
on what defines me is built from what is mine — my values, my story, my choices — is
supported by evidence from my own life, and is held as revisable, not final.** The
three-pillar frame is a teaching structure, not a psychological law; it gathers the
unit's threads (values, narrative identity, agency) into one writable artifact. Narrative
identity (S-404) and the exploratory, non-final nature of identity formation (S-403) are
the descriptive anchors; "you are the author, and you may revise" is the value
commitment (philosophy §6, §8, §11).

Model a complete worked example, then let learners practice (S-011). The **ethics** lens:
a considered position is honest — it names real evidence, including the parts still
undecided, and does not claim a certainty the learner does not yet have. The
**egalitarian** lens: every learner's position is worth writing and hearing; there is no
"best" way to be a person, and no position outranks another. The **global** lens: the
shape of a self is drawn differently in different cultures (recall Lesson 7) — a
considered position may be told as a story, a list, a song, or a vow, and all are valid
forms. The **technology & environment** lens: a position built to be *read back and
revised* resists the feed's demand for an instant, fixed, performable self — it is a
slow, honest thing in a fast, polished world. The **social-emotional** lens: this is
deeply personal work; invite, never force, let learners share only what they choose, and
allow "I'm still deciding" as a complete and dignified answer. This lesson is also the
unit's **summative** identity artifact — gather it into the portfolio.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From the last three lessons: some of what you "know" about
   yourself was written by other hands; some of it was labels; beneath it all is what is
   true. Today you write *that* down, on purpose.
2. **Meet the scaffold (10 min).** Look at the three pillars: **my values** (what I
   care about and how I try to live), **my story** (where I have come from and what it
   taught me), **my choices** (what I actually do, and choose next). Watch a worked
   example: a person who values *fairness*, whose story is *being the one who stood up
   when no one else did*, and whose choice is *to keep speaking for the one left out.*
   Notice how the three pillars hold each other up.
3. **Build your position (20 min).** Fill the scaffold. For each pillar, write one
   sentence that is true of you — and, beside it, one small **piece of evidence** from
   your own life (a moment, a habit, a choice) that shows it. Then write the sentence
   that holds them together: *what defines me is…*
4. **Test it (10 min).** Read your position back. Ask: *would this still be true if
   everyone's labels and expectations vanished tomorrow?* If not, revise it until it
   stands on what is yours. Then, in a pair, trade one kind, useful comment each — a
   thing that rings true, and a gentle question.
5. **Draft (7 min).** Write or record the final position in one short paragraph, ending
   with the part you are **still deciding** — because a considered position grows.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: this is your answer, in your words, and you hold the pen
   to revise it. No one else gets the final word on who you are.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-pillar version (values and choices) and a sentence starter
  ("What defines me is ___, and I know this because ___.").
- **Extension:** Add a **counter-consideration** — a label or expectation you weighed and
  set down, with a sentence on why it does not define you.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The completed position — the scaffold and the final
  paragraph — gathered into the unit portfolio as the "my considered position" artifact.
- **Formative (peer):** Can the learner's partner name one pillar with its evidence, and
  one part the learner is still deciding?

## Home connection

Read your position to a trusted adult, and ask: *what do you see in me that my position
leaves out — and should I add it?* Hold their words as a question, not a verdict.
