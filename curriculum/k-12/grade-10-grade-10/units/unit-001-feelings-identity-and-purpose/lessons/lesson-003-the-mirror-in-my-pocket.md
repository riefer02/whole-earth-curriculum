# Lesson 3 — The Mirror in My Pocket

## Summary

Learners **evaluate how online comparison shapes emotional life**. They learn that
**social comparison** is a normal human tendency (S-221) that **curated, edited feeds
can skew** — turning other people's highlight reels into a warped mirror. They keep a
**comparison-notice log**, name how comparison makes them feel, and choose a
**considered response** rather than a reactive one.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology and media influence emotional life, such as online
  comparison. _(D01.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

Every time you open a feed, you are handed a mirror — but a bent one. It shows the
polished, filtered, carefully chosen moments of other people's lives, next to the
unedited, ordinary, behind-the-scenes view you have of your own. No wonder a quick
scroll can leave a person feeling smaller. Comparison itself is not the problem —
humans have always looked sideways to understand themselves. The question is what we
compare, and what we do with the feeling afterward.

## Materials

- Comparison-notice log (the moment, what I compared, how I felt, a response I could choose)
- Screenshots or printed examples of a feed (optional)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the comparison-notice log.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, recall that history and culture shape feeling. Today we
  add a third shaper: **technology** — specifically the curated feeds that invite
  constant comparison.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) social
comparison is a normal, universal human tendency, not a personal defect; (2) online
feeds are curated and edited, so they skew comparison — typically toward "upward"
comparison with polished versions of others; (3) the healthy move is not to stop
feeling but to notice the comparison and choose a response.** The first comes from
Festinger's social-comparison theory (S-221); the second from the body of research on
media, technology, and adolescent development (S-226) and from WHO's balanced account
of social media's effects on adolescent mental health (S-311). Hold the causal claims
with care: the evidence says curated content *can* distort self-image and that
comparison *can* shape mood — not that screens simply "cause" illness (S-226, S-311).
This is a *balanced, non-alarmist* treatment (philosophy §11, §12).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **technology** lens is
the heart: name the mechanism — feeds rank and reward polished, high-engagement content
(S-481), and filter bubbles show a narrow slice of others' lives (S-482). The
**egalitarian** lens: whose lives are shown as "successful" in a feed is not neutral;
the same platform can lift some images and hide others. The **ethics** lens: honesty
about the gap between a person's feed and their real life is a kindness — to oneself and
to others, who are also showing only their highlights. The **global** lens: the pressure
to curate a perfect life is not the same everywhere; different cultures value different
kinds of "showing," and no single idea of success is universal. The **social-emotional**
lens: this topic can touch real sore spots — invite, never force; learners choose what
to share, and "pass" is always allowed. The **critical-thinking** lens: this is
practice in distinguishing the *image* from the *evidence* — a feed is a chosen story,
not a measurement.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of one time you scrolled and felt a little smaller
   afterward. You do not have to share — just notice that the feeling arrived *through*
   a comparison you did not consciously choose.
2. **Meet comparison (10 min).** Learn the word: **social comparison** is the human
   habit of measuring ourselves against others (S-221). It is normal — even useful.
   The problem online is that the feed is **curated**: people post their best, edited
   moments, so we compare our *whole*, unedited life to their *highlight reel*. Watch
   a worked example: a photo of a friend's "perfect" day, and the quiet, messy
   ordinary reality behind it.
3. **Notice your own (15 min).** Use the comparison-notice log. List two or three real
   moments from a recent day: what you compared, how it made you feel, and — for each —
   one honest fact the feed left out (the bad day, the filters, the years of practice,
   the luck).
4. **Choose a response (12 min).** For each moment, write one **response you could
   choose** next time — for example, closing the app, saying "that is a highlight, not
   a whole life," or messaging the person to ask how they *really* are. Notice: the
   goal is not to stop feeling; it is to stop letting the mirror decide your worth.
5. **Draft (10 min).** Write or record two or three sentences: *comparison is normal,
   but the feed is bent. Here is how I will hold it next time.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: the screen shows a story, not a measurement. You are
   allowed to be an unedited, ordinary, growing person.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a one-column log (the moment, the feeling) and a single sentence
  starter ("That post is a highlight, not ___.").
- **Extension:** Distinguish **upward**, **downward**, and **lateral** comparison, and
  explain which the feed most often triggers and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one comparison moment, the feeling it
  produced, and one considered response they would choose next time?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The comparison-notice log and the
  drafted reflection, added to the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: *have you ever felt worse after scrolling? What do you tell
yourself when that happens?* Trade one response that works for each of you.
