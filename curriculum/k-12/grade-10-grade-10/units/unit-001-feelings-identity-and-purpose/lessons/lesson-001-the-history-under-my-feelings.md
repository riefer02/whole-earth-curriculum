# Lesson 1 — The History Under My Feelings

## Summary

Learners trace how a **chapter of their own personal history** shapes the way they
**experience and name an emotion**. They build an **emotion-history map** — a memory
or life chapter on one side, the feeling and its name on the other — and name the
**meaning** they made from it. This opens the unit's first big idea: emotion is not
just a reaction to what is happening now; it is experienced *through* the history we
carry.

## Objectives

- Explain how personal history shapes the way a person experiences and names emotion.
  _(D01.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Two people can live through the same moment — a thunderstorm, a family move, a
farewell — and feel it differently, because each carries a different past into it.
The storm that frightens one person is the storm that soothes another who grew up
falling asleep to rain on a roof. Whether you come from a busy city, a quiet farm, a
coast, or a mountain town, your history is part of *how* you feel and *which name*
you give the feeling. That is not a weakness in you; it is how every human heart
works.

## Materials

- Emotion-history map worksheet (two columns: a memory / life chapter, and the feeling with its name)
- Writing journal
- Something from the past (optional)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the emotion-history map worksheet.
- Retrieval: from earlier grades, recall the idea that feelings have triggers,
  thoughts, and body sensations. Today we add the layer above them all: *history*.
  Recall too that the same situation can make different people feel differently — today
  we explain *why*, partly, through history.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a feeling is not a
bare event in the body; it is experienced and named through the personal history a
person brings to it.** That claim draws on the constructed-emotion account, which
holds that emotion concepts are built from past experience, culture, and language
(S-401), and on the broader emotional-literacy tradition that precise naming helps
regulation (S-015). Both are *descriptive theories*, not verdicts: a learner's feeling
is real and valid whether or not they can trace its history, and **"I can't find the
link yet" is a complete and honest answer.** Do not rank lives; there is no "best"
history to have had (philosophy §8, §11).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **ethics** lens: tracing history is about understanding,
never about blaming someone in your past — the goal is agency over the present, not a
verdict on the past. The **global** lens: every culture understands emotion through its
own history and words; the "event → feeling" shape is one useful frame, not a universal
law (S-401 is one account among many). The **egalitarian** lens: every learner's
history is worth mapping and hearing; no history is more "interesting" than another.
The **technology & environment** lens: the feeds and devices around us often present
emotion as an instant, sharable reaction — a like, a react emoji — while a feeling
actually arrives carrying years of history; noticing that depth is a small act of
resistance to the instant-and-forget pull of the feed. The **social-emotional** lens:
recalling the past can raise real feeling — invite, never force, and let learners
choose what to share (facilitation.md). Keep the tone gentle and forward-looking: the
point is a learner who feels like the *author* of their own emotional life.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back: have you ever felt something strongly and a friend
   felt the same moment *differently*? You do not have to share aloud — just notice
   that two people can stand in the same rain and feel two different things.
2. **Meet the map (8 min).** Look at the two columns: **a memory / life chapter** and
   **the feeling, with its name**. Watch a worked example: *then* — a move to a new
   place and the fear of starting over; *now* — an unexpected clench in the chest when
   meeting new people, named "anxious, like being new again." Notice the **link**: the
   past event is not just remembered; it is *re-experienced* inside the present feeling.
3. **Gather your chapters (15 min).** Fill your own map. On the left, write two or
   three real moments, people, or places from your life. On the right, name the
   feeling each one tends to stir in you now. You may use a photo or object to stand
   for a chapter.
4. **Find the meaning (12 min).** Read across a row and ask: *what did I learn, or
   come to believe, from that experience — and how does it color what I feel now?*
   Write one sentence of meaning for at least one row. If no meaning appears yet,
   write "still making sense of it" — that is honest, and it is enough.
5. **Draft (12 min).** Write or record one short paragraph that moves from a life
   chapter to the feeling it shapes today, ending with the name you give that feeling
   *now*, in your own words.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: your history is not a verdict on you. It is part of
   how you feel — and once you can see it, you can begin to choose how you respond to
   it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a one-row version with a sentence starter ("When ___ happened, I
  learned ___, and now I sometimes feel ___.").
- **Extension:** Connect *two* chapters to the *same* feeling and explain how they
  reinforce each other, or name a feeling you experience that a friend would name
  differently.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state, in one sentence, how a chapter of their
  history shapes a present feeling and the name they give it — even if the link is
  "still making sense of it"?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The emotion-history map and the
  drafted paragraph, added to the unit portfolio as the "history under my feelings"
  reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an older family member or trusted adult: *what is one memory from your past that
still shows up in how you feel today?* Listen for the link between their then and
their now. Then tell them one feeling of yours you can now trace to a chapter of your
own life.
