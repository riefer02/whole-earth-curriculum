# Lesson 4 — Feeling With Others, Through a Screen

## Summary

Learners **evaluate how technology shapes empathy**. They distinguish **empathic
concern** — feeling *with* someone, other-oriented and motivating of help — from
**personal distress** and distanced concern — feeling *for* someone from the outside
(S-230). They examine how screens can carry empathy across distance, and how they can
also flatten it into a quick "react" or a numb scroll, and they choose one way to keep
empathy alive online.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how technology and media influence emotional life, such as empathy.
  _(D01.S1.10.02)_

## Connection

A friend far away sends a message that they are hurting, and in a heartbeat you are
leaning toward them — even through a screen. That is empathy: feeling *with* someone,
not just *about* them. But a screen can also let a person scroll past a hundred small
sorrows until they stop landing, turning other people's pain into background noise.
Technology can carry our empathy a long way, or let it slip away — the difference is in
how we hold it.

## Materials

- With vs. for cards (short scenarios to sort)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the with/for scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall that feeds skew comparison. Today we ask how
  technology shapes *empathy* — the capacity to feel with others — for better and worse.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) empathy is not
one thing — "feeling with" (empathic concern) is other-oriented and moves us toward
help, while "feeling for" from a distance (and its overwhelmed cousin, personal
distress) is self-oriented and can lead to avoidance or burnout; (2) technology can
carry both — it extends empathy across distance but also risks flattening it into a
quick react or numbing it into compassion fatigue.** The with/for distinction draws on
Batson's empathic-concern vs. personal-distress work (S-230); the technology side draws
on the research on digital platforms, empathy, and echo chambers (S-226, S-481). Present
both as *descriptive*, not moralistic: a quick "react" is not a sin, and neither is
stepping back to protect your own heart — the point is *awareness and choice*, not guilt
(philosophy §8, §11).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **technology** lens:
explain the mechanism — a feed shows many people's pain at once, at a distance, with a
one-tap response, which can flatten empathy; algorithms may also show us mostly people
like us, shrinking the range of whom we imagine ourselves with (S-481, S-482). The
**global** lens: empathy reaches further than ever before — a person in one country can
feel with someone in another — yet that same reach can overwhelm; different cultures have
different norms for how feeling is shown, and none is the "right" amount. The
**egalitarian** lens: whose suffering is shown, and whose is left out, shapes whom we are
invited to feel with — ask that question out loud. The **ethics** lens: feeling with
someone carries a small obligation — to listen, to respond, to act when we can; empathy
without action can curdle into a performance. The **social-emotional** lens: this topic
can bring up real pain, including our own — invite, never force; "pass" is always allowed,
and the facilitator should watch for learners who may be carrying their own heavy story.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of a time a message from someone far away made you feel
   *with* them. Notice: the feeling reached you through a screen.
2. **Meet "with" and "for" (10 min).** Learn the difference. **Feeling with** (empathic
   concern): you take the other person's side, feel their feeling as theirs, and want to
   help (S-230). **Feeling for** (distanced concern) and its overwhelmed cousin
   **personal distress**: you feel *about* them from outside, often tangled with your own
   discomfort, and you may pull back. Watch a worked example: a friend's bad news —
   leaning in ("with") versus wincing and changing the subject ("for").
3. **Sort the cards (15 min).** In a small group, sort the scenario cards into "with,"
   "for," and "it depends." Discuss the close calls: when does a screen help you feel
   with someone, and when does it push you toward "for" — a quick react, a numb scroll?
4. **Name what the screen does (12 min).** Talk together: the screen can carry empathy
   far (a friend in another town, a stranger's story in another country) — and it can
   also flatten it (too many pains at once, one-tap responses, feeds full of people like
   us). Write down one thing the screen helps and one thing it hurts.
5. **Choose your practice (10 min).** Choose one small way to keep empathy *with* you
   online — for example, reply with a real sentence instead of a react, or pause on one
   person's story instead of scrolling ten. Write it down.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: empathy is not a limited resource you can run out of —
   but attention is. Choosing where to feel with someone is part of caring for them and
   for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer two starter scenarios with clear answers, and a sentence starter
  ("Feeling with means ___, while feeling for means ___.").
- **Extension:** Connect the with/for distinction to **compassion fatigue** — the
  exhaustion that comes from feeling *for* too many, too often — and name one boundary
  that protects empathy without turning it off.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Can each learner sort the scenarios and explain, for one close
  call, why it is "with," "for," or a mix?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The sorted cards or a written note on
  one screen-help and one screen-hurt, plus the chosen practice, added to the unit
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: *when has a screen let you feel with someone far away?* Then share
one way you will keep your empathy awake online, and ask what they do.
