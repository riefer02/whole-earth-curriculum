# Lesson 10 — Authoring My Own Learning

## Summary

Learners **direct their own learning** by choosing a **goal** that matters to them,
naming **resources**, and planning how they will **check their own progress**. They build
a **self-directed learning plan** — the unit's agency artifact — using a loop of
choose → gather → check → adjust, and commit to running it. This enacts D01.S3.10.01.

## Objectives

- Direct their own learning by choosing goals, resources, and ways to check their own
  progress. _(D01.S3.10.01)_

## Connection

Most of your life, someone else set the goal and checked the work. But the person who
can set their own goal, find their own way, and check their own progress has something
no one can take away: they can learn *anything*, anywhere, for the rest of their life.
This is not about being alone — it is about being the one who *decides*. A self-directed
learner is not the person who never asks for help (that is the next lesson); they are
the person who knows what they want to learn, and why, and how they will know they are
getting there.

## Materials

- Learning plan template (goal, why it matters, resources, milestones, self-check)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the learning plan template.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–9, recall that you are the author of your feelings and your
  identity. Today you become the author of your *learning* too. Recall, from the
  science-of-learning thread, that spacing and retrieval practice help learning stick
  (S-039) — your plan can use them.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **self-directed
learning is a skill with a structure — choose a goal, gather resources, check progress,
adjust — and it is teachable, not a mysterious gift.** The evidence: self-paced,
self-directed learning works in real classrooms, especially when learners have clear
guides and when they are grouped and supported appropriately (Escuela Nueva, S-008;
Montessori's mixed-age self-directed model, S-014), and mastery/feedback loops are among
the strongest learning levers (Bloom's 2-sigma work, S-007). Hold the nuance: the
"Hole-in-the-Wall" version of unsupervised self-teaching is *contested* — treat
self-direction as a *structured, supported* skill, not a miracle (S-013; philosophy §6,
§13). This lesson is the explicit-instruction side of a skill (S-011): model the plan,
then guide, then release.

Model a complete worked example, then let learners practice (S-011). The **intellectual**
lens: this is metacognition made concrete — planning, monitoring, and adjusting one's own
learning. The **ethics** lens: a good goal is chosen with honesty (is it really mine, and
is it fair to my own limits?) — not inflated to impress. The **egalitarian** lens:
self-direction is for *everyone*, not just the already-advantaged; a goal can be to read
one book, fix one thing, or learn one song — worth is not measured in scale. The
**global** lens: self-paced learning guides are a real, widely used method across the
world (Escuela Nueva, S-008) — this is not one culture's luxury but a global practice.
The **technology & environment** lens: digital tools can be powerful resources (and
distractions) — choose resources that serve the goal, and name the distraction you will
manage. The **social-emotional** lens: choosing a goal can stir both hope and
self-doubt; keep the goal small enough to start and real enough to matter, and honor
"not sure yet" as a valid place to begin.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of one thing you learned *because you wanted to*, not
   because anyone made you. Notice: you were already the author of that learning.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Learn the four-step loop: **choose a goal → gather
   resources → check my progress → adjust and try again.** Watch a worked example: *goal* —
   learn to cook one real meal from my family's tradition; *resources* — a grandparent, a
   recipe, one practice session; *check* — the meal is made and someone enjoys it;
   *adjust* — next time, the part that went wrong. Notice the loop keeps turning.
3. **Choose your goal (12 min).** Pick a goal that is *yours*, small enough to start this
   week, and real enough to matter. Write it as a single sentence. Then write **why it
   matters to you** — the fuel that keeps the loop turning when it gets hard.
4. **Name resources and checks (18 min).** List the **resources** you will use — a
   person, a place, a book, a video, a tool, practice time. Then write **two or three
   milestones**, and for each, how you will **check your own progress** — a thing you can
   see, do, or show that proves you are getting there (spaced practice and recall can be
   part of your check; S-039).
5. **Draft the plan (7 min).** Put it together in the template: goal, why, resources,
   milestones, self-checks. End with the first small step you will take *today or
   tomorrow*.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: you just did the thing adults call "taking ownership."
   The loop is yours now — turn it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a single-milestone version with a sentence starter ("My goal is ___;
  I will know I am getting there when ___.").
- **Extension:** Add a second self-check and a written plan for what you will do if a
  milestone is missed — adjusting, not quitting.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state a goal that is theirs, at least one
  resource, and one concrete way they will check their own progress?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed learning plan, added to
  the unit portfolio as the "authoring my own learning" artifact.

## Home connection

Tell a trusted adult your goal and your first step, and ask them to ask you about it in
a week — a human "milestone" on your loop.
