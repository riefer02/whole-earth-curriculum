# Lesson 11 — Independent and Connected

## Summary

Learners **balance independence with knowing when and how to seek help**. They sort
situations into **"try alone first"** and **"seek help now"**, practice the concrete
**how** of asking — who, when, and what to say — and come to see that healthy
independence is not isolation but **judgment**: the practiced skill of knowing which
way the moment calls. This enacts D01.S3.10.02.

## Objectives

- Balance independence with knowing when and how to seek help, and act on that
  judgment. _(D01.S3.10.02)_

## Connection

There are two opposite ways to get stuck: to give up and ask for help before you have
tried, and to refuse help until the small problem has grown huge. The skilled person
walks between them — they try hard on their own, and they know the moment to reach out.
A climber checks their own rope *and* calls to a partner before a risky move; a farmer
works their own field *and* borrows a neighbor's hands at harvest. Independence and
asking for help are not enemies — they are two hands of the same capable person.

## Materials

- Try alone / seek help cards (short scenarios to sort)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, recall your self-directed learning plan. Today we add the
  skill that makes self-direction *wise* rather than lonely: knowing when and how to
  seek help.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) seeking help is
a skill, not a weakness — and knowing when to seek it is part of mature independence;
(2) the skill has concrete parts: try alone first, recognize the limit, choose who to
ask, and ask clearly; (3) refusing help when it is needed is not strength but a
different kind of getting stuck.** The evidence: mastery/feedback loops and
relationships are among the strongest learning levers (S-007, S-012), and asking for
help is how a self-directed learner gets *feedback* — so help-seeking is continuous with
Lesson 10, not opposed to it. The "try alone first" instinct is honored; the point is
*joint judgment*, not endless independence (philosophy §6, §8).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **ethics** lens: asking
for help is also an act of fairness to oneself and to the task — pretending you don't
need help can waste others' time and your own. The **egalitarian** lens: not everyone
has been taught that it is safe or normal to ask; make asking ordinary and honored in
this space, and notice that some learners have fewer people to ask — which is why we
build a *community* help network, not just a private one. The **global** lens: cultures
differ in how asking is valued — in some, asking is a sign of humility and belonging; in
others, of weakness — and neither is the "true" one; the skill is to read the room and
still get what you need. The **technology & environment** lens: a help channel can be a
person, a library, a mentor, a hotline, or a trusted online source — name the real
channels available to *you*, and remember that not every online answer is trustworthy
(recall evaluating sources). The **social-emotional** lens: asking can feel vulnerable;
practice lowers the fear. Watch for learners who are quietly drowning rather than
asking — the facilitator's own invitation to ask is part of the lesson. Keep "I don't
have anyone to ask" a *solvable problem to build toward*, never a verdict.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of a time you needed help and either asked too late or
   didn't ask at all. Notice the cost of the delay — no blame, just noticing.
2. **Meet the balance (10 min).** Learn the frame: **try alone first** means giving your
   own best effort and your own judgment a real chance; **seek help now** means
   recognizing a limit — danger, stuckness, or a task too big for one person — and
   reaching out. Watch a worked example: a math problem (try alone first — reread, try a
   second way) versus a sign of real harm in a friend (seek help now — a trusted adult,
   today).
3. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** In a group, sort the cards into "try alone first,"
   "seek help now," and "it depends." Discuss the close calls — and for each "seek help
   now," name **who** you would ask and **what** you would say.
4. **Practice the ask (12 min).** In pairs, role-play one "seek help" scenario. Use a
   clear three-part ask: **what I need, what I have already tried, and one specific
   question.** Swap roles and try again — the point is practice, not performance.
5. **Map your network (8 min).** Write down two or three people or places you could
   actually ask — a family member, a teacher, a mentor, a library, a helpline — and one
   thing each is good for.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: the person who can do it alone *and* knows when to reach
   out is the strongest person in the room. Independence and help are two hands of the
   same capable person — you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-scenario sort with clear answers and a three-part ask
  sentence starter ("I need ___, I've tried ___, could you help me with ___?").
- **Extension:** Distinguish healthy independence from isolation, and write one
  situation where asking *early* would have been the stronger move — and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Can each learner sort the scenarios and, for one "seek help now"
  case, name who they would ask and give a clear three-part ask?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The help-network map and a one-line
  reflection, added to the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask a trusted adult: *when was a time asking for help made you stronger, not weaker?*
Then name one person or place on your help network to them.
