# Lesson 7 — Identity Across Time and Place

## Summary

Learners **examine how identity is historically and culturally constructed** by
**comparing examples across cultures and eras**. They ask how different peoples have
named their members — by kinship, clan, caste, craft, faith, language, age, or
achievement — what marked belonging, and what each valued. The comparison is not a
ranking but a mirror: seeing that identity has been built differently everywhere shows
the learner which of their own parts are *inherited*, which are *chosen*, and which are
up to them.

## Objectives

- Examine how identity is socially and historically constructed, comparing examples
  across cultures. _(D01.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

In one community, who you are may be told by your family line; in another, by the craft
your hands learned; in another, by the faith you keep; in yet another, by the school you
passed into or the team you joined. There is no single "right" way to be someone — human
beings have built identity out of every one of these materials, in every corner of the
world and every century. Studying how others have done it is like holding your own
identity up to a series of mirrors: you see more clearly which of your parts are gifts,
which are choices, and which you might remake.

## Materials

- Identity comparison chart (two cultures or eras, plus a "what I take / what I question" row)
- Short source excerpts (optional)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the comparison chart; gather the source excerpts.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall that identity is socially constructed. Today we add
  the second half: it is *historically* constructed too — it changes from place to place
  and era to era, and we can see that by comparing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **identity is not
fixed by nature alone; it is constructed differently across cultures and history — and
comparing examples makes the constructed, chosen, and inherited parts of our own
identity visible.** The framing is anthropological and global by design: no culture's
way of being someone is above another's (philosophy §4, §11). Keep examples accurate and
respectful — describe each culture's identity markers *from within* as far as possible,
credit real peoples and traditions, and avoid turning anyone into a "specimen" or a
cartoon. The standard (D01.S2.10.01) is explicitly comparative and cross-cultural, so
the comparison itself is the skill being taught.

Model the comparison skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **global**
lens is the heart: choose examples from more than one region and era, and hold them side
by side without ranking. The **egalitarian** lens: ask whose ways of being someone have
been treated as "the normal one" and whose have been called "exotic" — and name that
imbalance. The **ethics** lens: comparison is for understanding, not for judging another
people's way of life; respect means asking questions a community would recognize as its
own. The **technology & environment** lens: how identity is made is itself changing —
mass media and the internet now carry identity markers across the world, mixing and
sometimes flattening them (recall the feeds of Lesson 3); and the land and climate people
live in shape how they live and thus who they become. The **social-emotional** lens:
learners are themselves mid-formation (S-403 describes exploration as healthy and
normal); keep the tone affirming — there is no deadline for becoming, and uncertainty is
part of the work, not a failure.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: much of what you "know" about yourself was
   written by other hands. Today: those hands write *differently* in every time and
   place.
2. **Meet the comparison chart (10 min).** Look at two worked examples side by side —
   for instance, a community that names its members by **clan and lineage**, and one
   that names them by **chosen craft or profession**. For each, note: *how does it name
   its members? what marks belonging? what does it value?* Notice that neither is
   "more true" — each is a different answer to the question *what is a person?*
3. **Compare two cultures or eras (20 min).** In pairs, choose two examples from the
   source excerpts (or from your own and a neighbor's community). Fill the chart: how
   each names its members, what marks belonging, what it values — and what changed for
   each across history. If you use your own community, treat it with the same respectful
   curiosity you would bring to a distant one.
4. **Take and question (10 min).** Fill the final row: *from each example, what would I
   want to keep for myself, and what would I question?* Be specific — name one idea
   about being a person that you are glad to inherit, and one you would like to rewrite.
5. **Draft (7 min).** Write or record a few sentences: *identity has been built
   differently everywhere, which shows me that much of who I am is made, not fixed —
   and that some of it is mine to make.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: studying how others became themselves is not a search
   for the one right way. It is a search for the many ways — and a clearer view of your
   own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a single comparison with pre-filled rows and a sentence starter
  ("This community says a person is ___, while that one says a person is ___.").
- **Extension:** Explain *why* a culture's identity markers took their shape — connecting
  them to history, economy, belief, or environment — and name one way a modern technology
  is now changing that shape.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** Can each pair state, for two examples, how each names its
  members and what marks belonging — and name one thing they would keep and one they
  would question?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed comparison chart, added
  to the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an older family member or elder: *how did our community name people when you were
young, and how is it different now?* Listen for what changed and what stayed.
