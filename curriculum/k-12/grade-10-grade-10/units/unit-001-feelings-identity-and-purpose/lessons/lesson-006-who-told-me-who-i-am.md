# Lesson 6 — Who Told Me Who I Am?

## Summary

Learners **examine how identity is socially constructed**. They map the messages that
**family, peers, media, school, history, and place** send about who they are, and meet
the idea that **group belonging shapes identity** — that even arbitrary groups can
trigger in-group loyalty and out-group distance (S-232) — often without anyone noticing.
They begin to ask the unit's central question: *which of these messages did I choose,
and which were handed to me?*

## Objectives

- Examine how identity is socially and historically constructed, comparing examples
  across cultures. _(D01.S2.10.01)_

## Connection

Before you ever chose a word to describe yourself, other people had already begun
describing you — your family, your friends, the shows you watched, the school you sat
in, the history your community carried. "You are the quiet one." "You are so much like
your mother." "People like us don't do that." Some of those messages fit; some never
did. None of them, on its own, is the whole truth of you — but together they helped
build the person you see in the mirror. The first step to authoring your own identity is
seeing which parts were written by other hands.

## Materials

- "Who tells me who I am" map (central figure + surrounding circles)
- Writing journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the map.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 1–2, recall that history and culture shape *feeling*. Today we
  turn to *identity*: the sense of who we are, built — in part — by the people and
  systems around us.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **identity is socially
and historically constructed — built in relation to the groups, messages, and histories
around us, not simply found fully formed inside us.** The scientific anchor is social
identity theory and the minimal-group experiments: even arbitrary, content-free group
assignment can trigger in-group favoritism (S-232). Present that as a *descriptive*
finding about human group behavior, not a verdict on any person, and never as an excuse
for exclusion — the lesson's "open the circle" values are a *commitment*, not a claim
from the research (philosophy §4, §11).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **global** lens: how a
culture names its members — by clan, caste, craft, faith, language, birth order, or
achievement — varies enormously; there is no single "right" set of identity roles, and
comparing them is the point of the next lesson. The **egalitarian** lens: some identity
messages lift people and some limit them, and that is not distributed fairly — whose
messages are narrowing, and why, is a question worth asking out loud. The **ethics**
lens: we are all both *shaped by* and *responsible for* the messages we pass on to
others — the words we use to describe a person can build or confine them. The
**technology & environment** lens: media and feeds are now among the loudest voices
telling us who we are (recall Lesson 3's warped mirror) — include them in the map, and
note that the places we grow up in are voices too. The **social-emotional** lens: this
exercise can stir strong feeling about family or belonging; invite, never force, and let
learners choose what to share. Keep the tone curious, not accusing — the goal is
*awareness* and *agency*, not blame.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think of one thing you "know" about yourself that you first
   heard from someone else. Notice: it arrived from outside, then settled inside.
2. **Meet the map (10 min).** Look at the central figure and its surrounding circles:
   **family, peers, media, school, history, place, language**. Watch a worked example:
   the "family" circle filled with "the responsible one"; the "media" circle filled with
   "this is what a successful person looks like." Notice that these are *messages* — some
   true, some narrow, some simply inherited.
3. **Fill your circles (18 min).** In each circle, write two or three real messages you
   have received about who you are, from that source. For each, ask: *does this fit me,
   or was it handed to me?* Mark each message with a small ✓ (fits) or a small ? (handed
   to me, worth questioning).
4. **Meet in-groups and out-groups (10 min).** Learn this finding: people quickly form
   a sense of "us" and "them" — even over arbitrary groupings — and tend to favor their
   own group (S-232). Talk: *which of your circles feel like "us," and which feel like
   "them"? Who drew those lines, and who keeps them?*
5. **Draft (9 min).** Write or record a few sentences: *much of what I "know" about
   myself was written by other hands. Here is one message I accept, and one I am learning
   to question.*
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: seeing the hands that wrote your story is not an
   accusation — it is the beginning of holding the pen yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-circle map (family, media) and a sentence starter ("I first
  heard 'you are ___' from ___, and it ___.").
- **Extension:** Rank your circles by how much weight their messages carry for you now,
  and explain why the ranking has changed (or stayed) over time.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name two sources of identity messages and mark
  one message they accept and one they are learning to question?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed map, added to the unit
  portfolio as the "who told me who I am" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an older family member: *what is one message you were given about who you were
supposed to be — and did you keep it or rewrite it?* Listen for their "fits" and their
"?" marks.
