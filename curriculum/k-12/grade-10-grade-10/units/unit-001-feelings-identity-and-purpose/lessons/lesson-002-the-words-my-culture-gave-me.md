# Lesson 2 — The Words My Culture Gave Me

## Summary

Learners compare how different **languages and cultures name emotion**, discovering
that the **words a culture gives a person shape what they can notice, name, and value**
about their inner life. They meet the idea of **emotional granularity** — rich,
precise emotion words — and the idea of **display rules** — what a culture says may be
shown, hidden, or prized. They close by gathering emotion words from their own home
languages.

## Objectives

- Explain how culture shapes the way a person experiences and names emotion.
  _(D01.S1.10.01)_

## Connection

Some feelings are hard to put into words — until you meet a word from somewhere else
that fits exactly. A Portuguese speaker has *saudade*, a deep, sweet longing for
something absent; a Welsh speaker has *hiraeth*, a homesickness for a home that may
no longer exist. The language you grew up in gives you some drawers for your feelings
already open, and others you may not yet have a name for. Naming is not just
labeling — it is *seeing* more clearly what you feel.

## Materials

- Emotion-word chart (three columns: the feeling, one culture's word, what the word adds)
- Index cards or slips of paper

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the emotion-word chart.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall that personal history shapes how we feel. Today we
  add the second layer: *culture* — the shared words, norms, and values of the people
  around us — also shapes how we name and value feeling.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) emotion words
vary across languages and cultures, and a culture's words shape what its members can
easily notice and name; (2) precise emotion words ("emotional granularity") are
associated with finer-grained experience and better regulation.** The first is a
well-documented linguistic and anthropological fact; the second comes from the
constructed-emotion literature, which holds that richer emotion vocabulary is
associated with more differentiated experience and better regulation (S-401), and from
the labeling tradition (S-015). Hold both as *descriptions*, not laws: a person with
few emotion words still feels deeply, and **no language is "richer" or "better" than
another — each opens some doors and, like all languages, leaves others to be opened by
words or gestures** (philosophy §4, §11).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (S-011). The **global** lens is the
heart of this lesson: treat every home language as a bearer of dignity and wealth, and
never rank one language's feeling words above another's (S-019 frames multilingualism
this way). The **egalitarian** lens: a learner whose home language is not the school's
majority language is not "behind" — they carry a whole vocabulary of feeling others may
not have. The **ethics** lens: naming another culture's untranslatable word is an act of
respect — pronounce it with care, credit its speakers, and never turn a living word into
a specimen. The **technology & environment** lens: translation apps and feeds flatten
feeling into a small set of emoji and "react" options, which can shrink the very
granularity this lesson is building; notice both the gift and the flattening. The
**social-emotional** lens: feelings about language and belonging can run deep — invite,
never force, and let learners offer words only if they wish.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: your history shapes how you feel. Today, meet the
   second shaper: your **culture** — the words, norms, and values of the people around
   you.
2. **Meet the word chart (10 min).** Look at a worked example: *saudade* (Portuguese) —
   a deep, sweet longing for something or someone absent, more than "missing" and more
   than "sad." Notice what the word *adds*: it names a feeling a single English word
   can only circle around. Now look at the other examples on the card set (such as
   *hiraeth*, *mono no aware*, *ubuntu*) and ask: what does each word let its speakers
   see?
3. **Gather home words (15 min).** In a small group, share feeling words from your own
   home languages — or words you have heard older family members use. For each, write
   on a card: the word, the language, and what it names that a plain label misses. If
   you share no second language, describe a feeling *you* have no single word for.
4. **Name what your culture values (12 min).** Talk together: in your family or
   community, which feelings are *welcomed and shown*, and which are *hidden or
   discouraged*? These are **display rules**. Notice that they are rules, not truths —
   and that knowing them is the first step to choosing which to keep.
5. **Draft (10 min).** Write or record a few sentences: *the words my culture gave me
   shape what I notice and name when I feel.* Name one feeling you can now say more
   precisely.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: your language is a treasure, and so is every other
   person's. More words for feeling mean more ways to see yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a short list of example words with plain-language meanings, and a
  sentence starter ("In my family, we show ___, but we tend to hide ___.").
- **Extension:** Compare *two* words from different languages for near-neighbor
  feelings (for example, envy vs. jealousy, or *saudade* vs. *nostalgia*) and explain
  the difference each word draws.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** In pairs, can each learner explain one feeling word — theirs or
  an example — and what it adds that a simple label misses?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The emotion-word chart and the
  drafted reflection, added to the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: *what is a feeling word in our family's language that has no exact
translation — and what does it mean?* Write it down with its meaning, and bring it to
share if you wish.
