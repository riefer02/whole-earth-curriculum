# Lesson 7 — A Family or Cultural Tradition of Mine

## Summary

Learners identify one **family or cultural tradition** of their own and describe *when*
it happens and *what it means* to them. By hearing traditions from around the world —
meals, festivals, songs, greetings — they see that every culture repeats meaningful
practices, and that their own tradition is a real thread of belonging.

## Objectives

- Describe a family or cultural tradition and how it connects them to a larger community.
  _(D01.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

A **tradition** is something people do again and again because it means something. It
might be the stew your family cooks every year at harvest, the song you sing before a
journey, the lamps lit on a winter night, or the way you greet an elder. Traditions live
in every culture on Earth — a wedding dance, a naming ceremony, a new-year meal, a
prayer, a game. What makes something a tradition is not how grand it is; it is that it is
*repeated* and that it *means something* to the people who do it. Your tradition is one
small, real thread that ties you to your family and your people.

## Materials

- Traditions example chart
- My tradition page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the traditions example chart (several traditions, each with a meaning).
- Copy a "my tradition" page for each learner.
- Arrange space for sharing in a circle or small groups.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Traditions are deeply personal and
diverse — some learners may have several, some may feel they have none, and some may have
traditions tied to loss or migration. Hold all of it gently. The one requirement is a
*repeat with meaning*; it can be as small as "we eat together every Friday." Do not rank
traditions or treat any as "exotic" — the goal is that every learner sees their own
practice as worthy of the same respect as any other. Model your own tradition first
(worked example). See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics and cultural
power dynamics.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have been drawing the map of who you are. Today you add one
   more thread: a **tradition** — something your family or culture does again and again
   because it means something.
2. **Meet traditions from around the world (10 min).** Look at the chart. One family
   shares a big meal at harvest; another lights lamps on a winter night; another sings a
   song before a journey; another greets an elder in a special way. Different practices,
   one thing in common: each is **repeated** and each **means something**.
3. **Hear a worked example (10 min).** "My tradition is that we cook a special bread on
   the new year. We do it every year, together. It means a fresh start, and it reminds me
   I belong to my family." Notice the three parts: **what** we do, **when** we do it, and
   **what it means**.
4. **Describe your own tradition (15 min).** On your page, draw or write your tradition:
   what you do, when you do it, and what it means to you. If you can think of more than
   one, choose the one that means the most to you.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your tradition is a thread that ties you to your family and your
   culture. Next time, you will follow that thread out to see how it connects you to a
   much bigger community.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer tradition idea cards (a meal, a song, a festival, a greeting, a
  game, a story) and scribe the learner's answers.
- **Extension:** Compare your tradition with a partner's and name one thing the same and
  one thing different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe a family or cultural tradition,
  including when it happens and what it means to them?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks that their tradition is something really repeated
  and meaningful to them, not just a one-time event.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about the tradition you chose — where it came from and what it has
meant to them — and add one new detail to your page.
