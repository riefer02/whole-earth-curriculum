# Lesson 3 — Three Big-Grown Feelings: Jealousy, Guilt, and Pride

## Summary

Learners meet three **complex feelings** — **jealousy**, **guilt**, and **pride** — that
go beyond simple happy or sad. They learn what each one feels like, a real situation
where it shows up, and the useful signal each one carries: jealousy points to what I
want, guilt to what I did, and pride to what I worked for.

## Objectives

- Recognize complex feelings such as jealousy, guilt, and pride and connect them to real
  situations. _(D01.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

Some feelings are more than "happy" or "sad." They are bigger, more layered — like
feelings with a story inside. When a friend gets a new bike and you wish it were yours,
that is **jealousy**. When you remember a hurtful word you said and wish you could take
it back, that is **guilt**. When you finish something hard you practiced for weeks, that
is **pride**. These feelings show up everywhere — in a city, a village, a desert, or a
mountaintop — because every human who cares about something feels them. They are not bad
or good by themselves; they are **signals** that tell you what matters to you.

## Materials

- Complex feelings chart
- My complex feeling card
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the complex feelings chart.
- Copy a "my complex feeling" card for each learner.
- Arrange space for sharing in pairs and a quiet space for drawing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The key teaching move is to frame each
complex feeling as a *signal*, not a *verdict*: jealousy points to a want, guilt points
to a past action, pride points to effort. This keeps the lesson warm and non-shaming —
important, because jealousy and guilt can feel heavy. Teach all three aloud (worked
example) before learners connect their own. Never label a learner "jealous" or "guilty";
talk about the *feeling*, not the *person*. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding
difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned that your body tells you how strong a
   feeling is. Today you meet three feelings that are bigger and more layered than happy
   or sad: **jealousy**, **guilt**, and **pride**.
2. **Meet the three feelings (15 min).** Look at the chart. **Jealousy** is the wishing
   feeling — a friend gets a new bike and you wish it were yours. It points to *what you
   want*. **Guilt** is the heavy feeling after you did something you wish you had not —
   you said a hurtful word. It points to *what you did*. **Pride** is the warm, full
   feeling after you worked hard — you finished a drawing you practiced for days. It
   points to *what you worked for*. These are **complex feelings**: they mix several
   things at once, and they are signals, not bad or good by themselves.
3. **Act them out (10 min).** With a partner, take turns showing each feeling with your
   face and body — the reach of jealousy, the heavy shoulders of guilt, the lifted chest
   of pride — while your partner guesses which one it is.
4. **Connect to your own life (10 min).** On your card, draw or write one real moment
   for each: "I felt jealous when…", "I felt guilty when…", "I felt proud when…". You do
   not have to share anything you want to keep private.
5. **Close (5 min).** Complex feelings are messengers. Jealousy says "I want something."
   Guilt says "I want to do better." Pride says "I did something hard." Listening to
   them — without being ruled by them — is a skill you will use your whole life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer ready-made example cards to choose from for each feeling, and
  scribe the learner's own moment for each.
- **Extension:** Name a fourth complex feeling (such as embarrassment, relief, or
  hope) and say what it points to.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe what jealousy, guilt, and pride
  each mean and connect at least one to a real situation?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their card against the "what does it point to" test
  (want / did / worked for).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they felt proud of something they worked for, and share
one of your three feeling moments with them if you like.
