# Lesson 8 — My Tradition Connects Me to a Larger Community

## Summary

Learners trace how their family or cultural tradition connects them **outward** — to the
people they share it with, to their local community, and to a wider world where other
people do similar things. They discover that the same human needs — to celebrate, to
welcome, to remember — appear in traditions everywhere, turning one personal thread into
a sense of shared belonging.

## Objectives

- Describe a family or cultural tradition and how it connects them to a larger community.
  _(D01.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

A tradition is rarely yours alone. The meal you cook at harvest is cooked by other
families on your street, in your town, and by families in other lands who also harvest.
The way you greet an elder echoes the way greetings work in cultures far away. When you
follow your one small tradition outward, you find **threads** — to the people who do it
with you, to your **community**, and to a **wider world** full of people doing something
like it. You are not a single dot; you are tied into a web. A fisher child's blessing of
the boats is one thread in a worldwide web of people who honor the sea.

## Materials

- Concentric circles chart
- My connection web page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the concentric circles chart.
- Copy a "my connection web" page for each learner.
- Arrange space for pair sharing and quiet writing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The aim is belonging without ranking: no
tradition is "bigger" or "more connected" than another, and every thread is real. The
key discovery is that the *same human needs* appear across cultures — which is an
egalitarian and global point, not a competition. Model your own web first (worked
example). Be ready for learners whose tradition connects them to a community they have
left or lost; honor that thread gently as still real. See `docs/facilitation.md` on
cultural power dynamics and difficult topics.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you named a tradition of yours. Today you follow it
   outward to see how it connects you to a much bigger community.
2. **Meet the concentric circles (10 min).** Look at the chart. In the center is **me**.
   The next ring is **my family tradition**. The next is **my community** — the people
   nearby who share it. The outer ring is **the wider world** — people far away who do
   something like it. The dotted lines are **threads** tying you outward.
3. **Find the shared needs (10 min).** People everywhere celebrate, welcome, remember,
   and give thanks. A festival of light, a harvest feast, a naming ceremony, a song for
   the sea — different forms, but the same human needs underneath. Find the need *your*
   tradition answers.
4. **Fill your own web (15 min).** On your page, write or draw each ring: your
   tradition, who you share it with, your community, and who else in the world does
   something like it. Name at least one real person or group in each ring.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your tradition is a thread, and it reaches far beyond you. Knowing
   that is belonging: you are one person, tied to many, in a world where everyone is
   tied to someone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a ring-by-ring word bank and scribe the learner's answers; let them
  draw a person or place for each ring.
- **Extension:** Find one tradition in another culture that is like yours and name the
  shared human need behind both.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe how their tradition connects
  them to a community beyond their family, naming at least one real person or group?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks that every ring of their web holds something real,
  not made up.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home who else in the world shares a tradition like yours, and add what you
learn to your outer ring.
