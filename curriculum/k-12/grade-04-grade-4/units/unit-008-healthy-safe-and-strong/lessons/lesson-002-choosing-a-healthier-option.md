# Lesson 2 — Choosing a Healthier Option

## Summary

Learners use the label-reading skill from Lesson 1 to **compare two similar foods** and
make a healthier choice. They practice a simple rule — more fiber, and less added sugar
and salt — through worked examples and guided practice. The choice is always information
for themselves, never a judgment of a food, a family, or a body.

## Objectives

- Read simple nutrition information on food packages and use it to make a healthier
  choice between two foods. _(D03.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Last time you learned to read a label like a map. Now you get to *use* the map. When two
foods look alike on the shelf — two yogurts, two breads, two juices — the label helps you
notice what is different inside them, so you can choose the one that serves your body
better. Choosing is not about being "good" or "bad"; it is about picking what helps you.

## Materials

- Two side-by-side sample labels (a similar pair of foods that differ in sugar, salt, and
  fiber)
- A "choose" worksheet with the sentence frame "I would choose ___ because it has ___."
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare two large, simplified labels for a similar pair of foods (for example, two
  yogurts: one higher in fiber and lower in sugar and salt, one the reverse). Keep the
  numbers simple and round.
- Prepare the sentence frame and, for enrichment, pairs of real packages.
- Reaffirm the no-shame tone: a "healthier choice" is information for one's own body,
  never a verdict on anyone.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Model the comparison step-by-step before
independent work** (a worked example for a new skill): read the serving size of both,
compare fiber, compare sugar, compare salt, then decide. The rule is gentle and honest:
for many bodies, **more fiber and less added sugar and salt** is a helpful direction —
but no single food is "bad," a treat now and then is a normal part of life, and different
bodies, budgets, and lives make different choices, all worthy of respect. Never let the
lesson slide into ranking a learner, a family, or a body by what they eat; the choice is
about the *food's* label, not the *person*. Honor food scarcity: a family that eats what
is available and affordable is not making a "wrong" choice — access is not the same as
preference, and every fed child is a win. A learner who is blind or has low vision
compares by hearing the numbers read in the same order each time; a learner who does not
use a speaking voice points to the label or fills the frame on a card; a learner who is
deaf or hard of hearing follows the side-by-side labels and the demonstration, since
every step is shown. This lesson practices D03.S3.04.01 toward mastery and leads into
the cultural-foodways lessons that follow.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember the five parts of a label: serving size, energy, sugar,
   salt, and fiber. Today you will use them to **compare** two foods and choose.
2. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** Look at the two labels. First read the serving
   size of both — are they the same amount? Next compare **fiber**: which has more?
   Then compare **sugar** and **salt**: which has less? A helpful choice for most bodies
   is the one with **more fiber** and **less added sugar and salt**. Watch and follow as
   we do the first pair together.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, do a second pair the same way: read the
   serving size, compare fiber, compare sugar, compare salt. Decide together and say why
   using the frame: "I would choose ___ because it has ___."
4. **Your own choice (10 min).** Do one more pair on your own. Fill in the frame and
   check yourself: did you compare all three — fiber, sugar, salt — before deciding?
5. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "I read the label, I compare, and I choose what
   helps my body. No food is bad, and no body is judged."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare only one thing at a time (fiber first); model the sentence frame
  and accept spoken, pointed, or drawn answers.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the label that has more fiber or less sugar, and
  build the sentence from word cards.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear the numbers read in the same order and repeat the
  comparison aloud.
- **Extension:** Compare a third pair and explain how serving size matters — why you must
  check that both labels are talking about the same amount before comparing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner compare two labels and name a healthier
  choice with a reason (more fiber, less added sugar or salt)?
- **Self:** The learner names the rule ("more fiber, less added sugar and salt") and one
  choice they made today.

## Home connection

At home, compare two similar foods your family has (or remembers) — two breads, two
juices, two snacks. Which has more fiber? Which has less added sugar or salt? Tell a
grown-up your choice and why.
