# Lesson 1 — Reading a Food Label

## Summary

Learners open the unit by reading a simplified nutrition label. They name its parts —
serving size, energy, sugar, salt, and fiber — and say what each one tells them about a
food. The label is taught as a tool for **information**, never for judging a body or a
way of eating.

## Objectives

- Read simple nutrition information on food packages, naming the parts of a label and
  what each tells us. _(D03.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Most packaged foods have a small box of writing on the back or side — a **nutrition
label**. You have probably seen one on a bag, a box, or a tin at home or in a market,
even if you never stopped to read it. That little box is a map: it tells you what is
inside the food, so you can make your own choices. Today you will learn to read that
map.

## Materials

- A large sample nutrition label chart (serving size, energy, sugar, salt, fiber)
- A few cleaned, real food packages (or printed pictures) from many kinds of food
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Draw or print a large, simplified nutrition label with five parts circled: serving
  size, energy, sugar, salt, and fiber. Use a different *shape* (circle, square,
  diamond, star, triangle) around each part, not only color, so it reads clearly in
  grayscale.
- Gather a few clean food packages from different cuisines.
- Set the tone: a label gives information, never shame. No food and no body is "bad."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **The label is a tool for information,
never for judging.** Teach the five parts plainly: **serving size** (how much the label
is talking about), **energy** (calories/kcal — how much fuel), **sugar** (a kind of
carbohydrate, often sweet), **salt** (sodium), and **fiber** (part of plant food that
helps digestion). Do not rank foods as "good" or "bad," and do not connect any number on
the label to a body's size or worth. Different bodies and different lives need different
amounts, and a food is not a person. Honor that not every learner has packaged food at
home, and that fresh, home-cooked, and market food often has no label at all — that is
normal and fine. A learner who is blind or has low vision reads the label by having the
parts described in order and by feeling raised shapes on the chart; a learner who does
not use a speaking voice points to each part; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing
follows the chart and demonstrations from the front, since every part is shown, not only
told. The nutrition facts reflect widely used labeling (energy, sugar, salt, fiber, and
serving size), as described in World Health Organization guidance on food labels. This
lesson opens the unit's D03.S3.04.01 label-reading path and leads into choosing a
healthier option.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a packaged food you have seen — a bag of rice, a box of
   cereal, a tin of beans, a packet of biscuits. Did you ever notice the small box of
   writing on it? That is a **nutrition label**, and it is a map of what is inside.
2. **Meet the five parts (15 min).** Look at the big label. A label usually tells you
   five things. The **serving size** says how much of the food the label is talking
   about. **Energy** (calories) says how much fuel the food gives your body. **Sugar**
   is a kind of carbohydrate that tastes sweet. **Salt** (sodium) is the mineral that
   makes food salty. **Fiber** is the part of plant foods that helps your digestion
   work well. Point to each part as we name it.
3. **Say what each part tells you (10 min).** Match the part to its job: serving size
   tells you *how much*; energy tells you *how much fuel*; sugar and salt are things to
   notice; fiber is something many bodies need more of. Tell a partner one part and
   what it tells you.
4. **Label hunt (10 min).** Take a real package (or a remembered one). Can you find the
   serving size? The energy? The sugar, salt, and fiber? Point to each one. If your
   food has no label — fresh fruit, home-cooked food — say so: that is normal, and you
   can still know it is a healthy part of your day.
5. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "A nutrition label is a map. I can read serving
   size, energy, sugar, salt, and fiber — and use them to make my own choices."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Introduce one or two parts at a time (serving size and energy first);
  accept spoken, pointed, or drawn answers.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to each part on the chart and match part cards to
  their places.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear each part named in order and feel raised shapes
  on the chart; repeat the names and jobs aloud.
- **Extension:** Read the label on a real package from home and name all five parts,
  plus one more thing the label lists (such as protein or fat).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner point to and name the parts of a
  simplified label and say, in their own way, what one part tells them?
- **Self:** The learner names the part they can now read and one food whose label they
  want to check next.

## Home connection

With a grown-up, find one packaged food at home and read the label together. Point to
the serving size, energy, sugar, salt, and fiber, and name what each one tells you.
