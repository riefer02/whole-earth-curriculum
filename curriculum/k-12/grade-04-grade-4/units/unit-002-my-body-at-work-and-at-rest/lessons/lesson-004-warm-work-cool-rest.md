# Lesson 4 — Warm Work, Cool Rest

## Summary

Learners compare their warmth **at rest** and **at work**. They feel their cheeks and
hands while still, move hard, and feel again, discovering the body warms with activity.
They learn **why**: working muscles make heat — the way any engine warms as it runs —
and the body cools back down with rest and with sweat.

## Objectives

- Describe how body warmth changes with activity and rest, and explain why moving
  muscles make the body warm. _(D03.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever run so much that your face felt hot and your shirt stuck to your back?
Then, after you sat still in the shade, you cooled down. That warmth is your muscles
making heat as they work — like your hands warming when you rub them together, or an
engine warming as it runs. A child herding goats in the sun, a swimmer climbing out of
cool water, a dancer resting between songs — every body warms with work and cools with
rest.

## Materials

- A warmth chart (warm from work / cool at rest)
- A clear space to move
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the warmth chart.
- Clear a safe space to move.
- If outdoors, note a warm sunny spot and a cool shady spot to feel the difference.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Model the worked example — feel cheeks at
rest, move, feel again — then let learners try. Feeling warmth with hands is enough; a
thermometer is an optional enrichment, not a requirement. Keep the movement inclusive:
seated arm-circles warm the body too. Be warm and matter-of-fact about sweat — it is a
healthy cooling system, never something to be embarrassed about, and no body is judged
for how much it sweats. Some bodies run warmer or cooler than others; all are normal.
This lesson retrieves the heartbeat and breathing signals from Lessons 1–3 and adds the
third: warmth.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have read two signals: heartbeat and breathing. Today you read
   the third: your **warmth**. Feel your cheeks and your hands with your own fingers.
   Are they warm or cool?
2. **Feel at rest (10 min).** Sit very still and quiet. Feel your cheeks, your hands,
   your arms. Write or say how warm you feel. This is your body at rest.
3. **Move (10 min).** Stand and move hard for about two minutes — march, jump, or pump
   your arms. Choose the way your own body moves.
4. **Feel after moving (10 min).** Stop. Feel your cheeks and hands again. Are they
   warmer than before? Write or say what changed.
5. **Ask why (10 min).** Your muscles were working, and working muscles make **heat** —
   like hands rubbing together, or an engine running. That heat spreads through your
   body and warms you. When you rest, the muscles slow, and your body cools down. When
   you are very warm, you may **sweat**; as the sweat dries, it cools your skin.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "when I work, my body warms; when I rest, it
   cools." Now you have read all three signals — heartbeat, breathing, and warmth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Feel only the cheeks before and after moving, and use "warm" and "cool"
   words rather than writing.
- **Extension:** Measure the air temperature before and after moving and describe what a
   thermometer tells you — and what it cannot tell you about your own body's warmth.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner notice a change in their own warmth
  from rest to activity and explain that working muscles make heat?

After you play hard at home, feel your cheeks, then rest in the shade and feel them
again. Tell someone what your body does — it warms with work and cools with rest.
