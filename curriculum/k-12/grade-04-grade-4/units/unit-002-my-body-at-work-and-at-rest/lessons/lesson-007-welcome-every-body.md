# Lesson 7 — Welcome Every Body

## Summary

Learners turn respect into action by designing play that welcomes every body. They play
a simple passing game, notice who might be left out, and add or change a rule — such as
passing to the side so a seated player can reach, or using a hand signal everyone can
see — so every kind of body can join and belong.

## Objectives

- Notice and respect differences in how bodies sense and move, and adapt activities so
  everyone can join. _(D03.S1.04.02)_
- Apply movement skills to a variety of activities, including adapted or seated
  versions. _(D03.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

A game is only truly fun when everyone can play. If a rule leaves someone out — a game
only for the fastest runners, or only for people who can stand — then the rule can be
changed, not the person. Around the world, people have invented ways to include every
body: sitting volleyball, boccia for wheelchair players, goalball for players who cannot
see. Welcoming every body is not just kind; it makes the game better for everyone.

## Materials

- A "welcome every body" design sheet
- A soft ball or rolled cloth
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the design sheet.
- Clear a wide, flat space so players who use wheels or chairs can move freely.
- Have a soft ball or rolled cloth ready for the passing game.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The key move is *adapting the game, not
the child*: a rule that leaves someone out is the problem, not the body. Model one
worked example aloud — "if we only pass over our heads, someone seated can't reach, so
let's pass to the side" — then let learners design. Keep it joyful and concrete. If a
learner uses an aid, honor their expertise: they know best what works for them, and
their ideas lead. Never single anyone out as "the example." This lesson carries forward
Lesson 6's respect for diverse bodies and sets up the next two lessons on rules and fair
play.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Yesterday you met many ways bodies sense and move. Today you ask:
   how do we make a game everyone can play?
2. **Play the passing game (10 min).** Stand or sit in a circle. Pass the soft ball
   around the circle in any way your body can — toss, roll, or hand it to the next
   person. Notice: did everyone get a turn? Was anyone left out?
3. **Notice who might be left out (10 min).** Talk with a partner: if a friend could not
   stand, or could not see the ball, or could not hear a "go!" shout, could they still
   play? What would stop them?
4. **Change one rule (15 min).** On your design sheet, add or change one rule so more
   bodies can join. For example: pass to the side so a seated player can reach; roll the
   ball instead of throwing it; use a hand signal instead of only a shout. Write or draw
   your new rule and why it is fair.
5. **Try the new rule (10 min).** Play the passing game again with your new rule. Did
   more bodies join? Did it feel more welcoming?
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "when a game leaves someone out, we change the
   game, not the person." A welcoming game is a fairer game for everyone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Change the rule together aloud and model it once before playing; use a
  single simple adaptation.
- **Extension:** Redesign a familiar game with two new rules so still more kinds of
  bodies can join, and explain each change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner name who might be left out of a game
  and propose one rule change that lets them join?

## Home connection

Think of a game your family plays. Tell someone at home one rule you could change so a
neighbor, a younger child, or someone who moves differently could join too.
