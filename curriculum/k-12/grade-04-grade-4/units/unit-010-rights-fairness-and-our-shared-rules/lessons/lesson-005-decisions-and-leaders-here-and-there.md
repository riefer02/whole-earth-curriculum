# Lesson 5 — Decisions and Leaders: Here and There

## Summary

Learners describe how decisions are made and leaders chosen in **their own
community**, then compare with **one other place in the world**. They meet several real
ways groups decide — consensus, majority vote, elders' councils — and learn that no
single way is the only right one; the fair test is whether every voice counts. This
builds on Grade 3's "having a voice in the group" toward comparing how different
communities actually decide and choose leaders.

## Objectives

- Describe how people make decisions and choose leaders in their community and compare
  with one other place in the world. _(D09.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Every group — a family, a class, a village, a nation — must decide things together and
sometimes choose someone to lead. But groups do this in **different ways**, and no
single way is the only right one. Some groups **talk until everyone agrees** (this is
called **consensus**); some take a **vote** and follow the majority; some listen to a
**council of elders** who carry long memory and wisdom. Think of your own community:
how are decisions made, and how is a leader chosen? Now think of another place in the
world — the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) nations, for example, have long made decisions in
councils where leaders chosen by clan mothers talk until all agree; a village in the
Andes might gather everyone in a circle to talk it through; a town far away might elect
a mayor by vote. Today you will describe how it works where you live, and compare it
with one other place on Earth.

## Materials

- A "ways to decide" chart (consensus, majority vote, elders' council, drawing lots)
- A "here and there" comparison sheet per learner
- Crayons or pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the "ways to decide" chart, or decide which ways to act out aloud.
- Prepare the two-column "here and there" comparison sheet.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson introduces **ways of deciding and choosing
leaders** — and it must **never rank one political system above another**. Present
several real ways side by side (consensus, majority vote, elders' council, drawing
lots) as different traditions that all aim at fairness, each with strengths and limits.
The Haudenosaunee example is accurate and useful: leaders (called royaner, or
chiefs) are chosen by clan mothers, and councils work by consensus under the Great Law
of Peace — a real tradition that also influenced later ideas about confederation. Keep
the learner's own community open and undetermined: some learners live under monarchies,
some in elected republics, some in communities governed by councils or elders — all are
welcomed and described *descriptively*, never as "better" or "worse." The fair test we
teach is not "which system" but "does every voice count, and is anyone left out?" —
hold that as a question. A learner who does not speak aloud points to a way on the
chart; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing follows the chart through sign and
caption; a learner who is blind or low-vision hears each way described and compares
aloud; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to fill the two columns.
A learner may always pass, and may describe their community privately to a grown-up if
speaking about it publicly is unsafe. The goal is a learner who can say, in their own
way, "here is how we decide and choose leaders, and here is how they do it in one other
place."

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last time you tested whether a rule is fair to
   everyone. Who do rules affect? Today we look at who *makes* the rules — how people
   decide and choose leaders.
2. **Meet "ways to decide" (10 min).** Look at the chart. Some groups decide by
   **consensus** — talking until everyone can agree. Some take a **vote** — the side
   with more hands wins. Some turn to a **council of elders** — people whose long
   memory and wisdom guide the choice. Some draw lots. None is the only right way.
3. **Describe "here" (10 min).** Think of your own community — your class, family,
   village, or town. How are decisions made? How is a leader chosen (if there is one)?
   Write or draw it in the "here" column, or tell it aloud.
4. **Compare with "there" (15 min).** Pick one other place from the chart or a story —
   the Haudenosaunee council, an Andean village circle, an elected town far away. Write
   or draw how *they* decide and choose leaders. Wonder together: how are the two ways
   alike? How are they different? Does every voice count in each?
5. **Close (5 min).** People decide and choose leaders in many fair ways. The test is
   not which way — it is whether every voice counts.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each way of deciding to its picture with a grown-up; a
  non-speaking learner points to the way their community uses; a learner with limited
  movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to compare two *other* places with each other, and
  wonder what each way is good at and what it might miss.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe how their community decides and
  chooses leaders, and compare it with one other place?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's completed "here and there" comparison sheet.

## Home connection

Ask a grown-up: "How does our community decide things, and how is a leader chosen?"
Then share one other way from today's chart and compare.
