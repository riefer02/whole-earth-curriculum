# Lesson 9 — Restorative Practice: Repair, Not Only Punish

## Summary

Learners describe **restorative practice**: listen to the person who was hurt, **take
responsibility** for what happened, and **repair the harm**. They explain why it can
work better than punishment alone — punishment stops an act; restorative practice also
heals the hurt and keeps the person accountable for making it right. This deepens
Grade 3's "repairing harm" into the named practice and its *why*.

## Objectives

- Describe restorative practice (listening, taking responsibility, and repairing harm)
  and why it can work better than punishment alone. _(D09.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

When harm happens — a tower knocked over, a hurt word, a borrowed thing returned
broken — there are two ways to answer. **Punishment alone** stops the act (the child is
sent away) but leaves the tower broken and the hurt untouched. **Restorative practice**
asks three things instead: **listen** to the person who was hurt, **take
responsibility** for what happened, and **repair the harm** — help rebuild, mean the
sorry, fix the break. It does not let anyone off — it holds the person *accountable*
for making things right. That is why it can work better than punishment alone: the harm
is healed, and the person who caused it grows instead of only paying a price. People
and schools use this around the world — a circle where both sides are heard, and a
repair that makes it right. Today you will learn the three steps and why they help.

## Materials

- A "restorative circle" picture (listen, take responsibility, repair)
- A "restore vs. only punish" comparison strip
- Two puppets or soft toys

## Preparation

- Prepare the restorative circle picture and the "restore vs. only punish" strip.
- Set up a small space for acting out the restorative steps.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **restorative practice** — the
fuller form of justice this unit builds toward. The three moves are **listen** (hear
the one who was hurt), **take responsibility** (own what happened, without shame — name
the *act*, never the child), and **repair the harm** (do something that makes it
right). Crucially, restorative practice **does not dismiss accountability** — it
*requires* it: the person who caused harm must own it and fix it, which is harder and
more honest than sitting out a penalty. Teach it as *both/and*, not *either/or*:
punishment alone stops; restoration heals *and* holds accountable. Hold it as a
question ("what does each path do for the person who was hurt — and for the person who
caused it?"), never as a forced ritual: never make a learner confess a real, private
harm or perform a scripted apology — invented or acted harms are welcome, and a learner
may always pass. A learner who does not speak aloud points to the three steps or signs;
a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing follows the circle through sign and caption; a
learner who is blind or low-vision hears the steps described and names why repair
heals; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to do the repair. The
aim is that every learner can say, in their own way, "restorative practice means
listening, taking responsibility, and repairing harm — and that can heal better than
punishment alone."

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last time you practiced "I" statements and active
   listening. How do those help when there is a disagreement? Today we go further: what
   happens *after* harm, so things are made right.
2. **Meet restorative practice (10 min).** **Restorative practice** answers harm with
   three steps: **listen** to the person who was hurt; **take responsibility** for what
   happened; **repair the harm** — fix it and make it right. It holds the person
   accountable, and it heals.
3. **Compare the two paths (15 min).** Look at the strip: one harm, two paths. For
   "only punish," name what is still broken. For "restore," name what got fixed — and
   who stayed accountable. Wonder: why can repair work better than punishment alone?
   What does it give the person who was hurt? What does it ask of the person who caused
   it?
4. **Act a repair (10 min).** With the puppets, show a small harm, then walk the three
   steps: listen, take responsibility, repair. A learner who does not move the puppets
   can point to a step or direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** Restorative practice listens, takes responsibility, and repairs.
   Punishment alone stops; restoration heals — and keeps the person accountable for
   making it right.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Order the three steps with pictures and model "repair fixes the hurt,
  punishment alone does not"; a non-speaking learner points to the repair step; a
  learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to wonder about a harm that cannot be fully undone (a
  broken vase, a hurt word) and what "taking responsibility and repairing" means when
  the thing cannot be put back exactly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe restorative practice (listen,
  take responsibility, repair) and why it can work better than punishment alone?
- **Performance:** The acted-out restorative steps, explained aloud.

## Home connection

At home, if a small harm happens, wonder together: would punishment alone heal the
hurt, or is there a repair — a listen, a taking-responsibility, a making-it-right —
that would work better?
