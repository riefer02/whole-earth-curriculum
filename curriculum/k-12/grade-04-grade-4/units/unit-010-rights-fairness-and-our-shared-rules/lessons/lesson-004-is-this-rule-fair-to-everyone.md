# Lesson 4 — Is This Rule Fair to Everyone?

## Summary

Learners learn to put a rule "on trial": ask **who it affects**, whether it **treats
anyone worse**, and whether there is a **fairer way** — then state a judgment with a
reason. A rule that seems fine can be unfair to someone it was never checked against,
and the fix is to change the rule, not the person. This deepens Grade 3's "rules keep
us safe and fair" into *evaluating* a rule's fairness to everyone it touches.

## Objectives

- Evaluate whether a rule or practice is fair to everyone it affects and explain the
  judgment. _(D09.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Rules are meant to keep a group safe and fair — but a rule can be fair to some people
and unfair to others without anyone meaning harm. Think of a rule that says "everyone
must reach the top shelf to get a book." For a tall person it is easy; for a shorter
person it is a stretch; for a person who uses a wheelchair it is impossible. The rule
was not made to be unfair — it just was never checked against **everyone it affects**.
People everywhere test rules this way — a village deciding whether its one water tap
works for the family far uphill, a bus company asking whether its step works for
everyone, a class asking whether its game leaves anyone out. Today you will learn to
put a rule on trial and judge, with a reason, whether it is fair to everyone.

## Materials

- "Fairness check" question cards (who does it affect? does it treat anyone worse? is
  there a fairer way?)
- A "rule on trial" sheet per learner
- Crayons or pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the fairness-check question cards and a few everyday rules to test.
- Prepare the "rule on trial" sheet with the three checkboxes and a judgment line.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **evaluating a rule's fairness to
everyone it affects** — the unit's core fairness skill. Model the move first as a
worked example: pick a rule, run the three questions out loud, and reach a judgment
with a "because." Emphasize that an unfair rule is usually *not made with bad intent* —
it is a rule that was never checked against everyone, so the fix is to **change the
rule, not blame the person**. This keeps the lesson from shaming anyone. Use invented,
gentle rules (the shelf, the game, the queue, the water tap); avoid rules that could
single out a real child. Note that "fair" does not always mean "the same": fairness to
everyone sometimes means *different* support for *different* needs (a low shelf, a
ramp, a helper) — revisit Grade 2's "same treatment is not always fair." A learner who
does not speak aloud points to a checkbox or to the affected figure; a learner who is
deaf or hard of hearing follows the three questions through the chart and sign; a
learner who is blind or low-vision hears the rule and its effects described and gives
their judgment aloud; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to mark
the sheet. A learner may always pass. The goal is a learner who can say, in their own
way, "this rule is fair (or not) to everyone because..."

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last time we met dignity — being treated as a
   person of worth. What does it mean? Today we test whether a rule treats everyone
   with worth.
2. **Meet the fairness check (10 min).** To judge a rule, ask three questions: **who
   does it affect?** **does it treat anyone worse?** **is there a fairer way?** A rule
   is fair to everyone only if it passes all three.
3. **Put a rule on trial (15 min).** Pick a rule and run the three questions. Name who
   is helped and who is left out or treated worse. Then state your judgment: "this rule
   is fair (or not) to everyone because..." Wonder together: is there a fairer version —
   a low shelf, a ramp, a helper — that fixes it *without* blaming anyone?
4. **Judge your own rule (10 min).** On your sheet, write or draw a rule, mark the
   three checkboxes, and write or tell your judgment and its reason. If writing is not
   your way, act it out or have a partner scribe while you direct.
5. **Close (5 min).** Ask who a rule affects, whether it treats anyone worse, and
   whether there is a fairer way. Then you can judge — and you can always change the
   rule, not the person.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Test one rule with a partner and a sentence frame ("this rule leaves out
  ___ because ___"); a non-speaking learner points to the affected figure; a learner
  with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to rewrite an unfair rule into a fairer one, and
  explain who is now included and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner evaluate whether a rule is fair to
  everyone it affects and explain the judgment with a reason?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's completed "rule on trial" sheet.

## Home connection

At home, pick one family rule and run the three fairness questions together. Wonder
aloud: who does it affect, does it treat anyone worse, and is there a fairer way?
