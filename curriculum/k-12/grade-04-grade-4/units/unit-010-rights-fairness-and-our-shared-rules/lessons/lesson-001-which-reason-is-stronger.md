# Lesson 1 — Which Reason Is Stronger?

## Summary

Learners move from listing many reasons to the sharper skill of comparing **two**
reasons for one choice and deciding **which is stronger and why**. A strong reason
stands up when you ask "because of what?" — it helps a person, keeps a promise, or
does the most good with the least harm. This deepens Grade 3's "reasons for and
against" into judging *strength*, not just *counting*.

## Objectives

- Weigh two reasons for a choice and decide which is stronger and why.
  _(D09.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of a moment when two reasons pulled you opposite ways: "I should help carry the
water home" and "I want to keep playing." You cannot do both. When two good reasons
collide, you need more than a list — you need to judge **which reason is stronger**.
A reason is stronger when it stands up to the question "because of what?": it keeps a
word you gave, protects someone who needs it, or does the most good with the least
harm. People everywhere face this — a herder deciding whether to take the flock to
distant grass or stay near water, a shopkeeper deciding whether to sell cheaply to a
neighbor in need or hold the price, a class deciding whether to use its one shared ball
for the match everyone wants. Today you will practice naming two reasons and deciding,
honestly, which is stronger — and why.

## Materials

- Two-reason decision cards (each with exactly two competing reasons)
- A "stronger reason" balance sheet per learner
- Crayons or pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the two-reason decision cards, or decide on the choices to act out aloud.
- Prepare the two-pan "stronger reason" sheet, or draw a scale on the ground, cloth, or
  a shared sheet.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **judging the strength of a
reason**, the unit's first moral-reasoning skill. Model the move first as a worked
example: name the two reasons, then think aloud — "I ask *because of what?* — this
reason helps someone who is counting on me, so it is stronger." Then do guided practice
before independent work. Keep choices small, everyday, and blame-free; there is no
single correct answer, and a learner may decide either way as long as they can defend
it with a "because." A reason's strength is not about who says it, and no learner's
reason is "better" than another's — we compare reasons, never people. The
**balance-scale** is a picture, not a rule: sometimes the reason that helps a person
outweighs the reason that is only about wanting something, and that is worth wondering
about — but there are honest disagreements, and that is the point. Hold "what is the
strongest reason?" as a question, never a command. A learner who does not speak aloud
places a stone on the heavier pan or points to a card; a learner who is deaf or hard of
hearing follows each reason through the chart and sign; a learner who is blind or
low-vision hears each reason described and states their "because" aloud; a learner with
a motor difference can direct a partner to place their stone. A learner may always
pass. The goal is the learner's own honest judgment — "this reason is stronger
because..." — not a memorized answer.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last year you weighed reasons *for* and *against* a
   choice. Recall it: what does "weighing reasons" mean? Today you will go one step
   further — you will compare **two** reasons and decide **which is stronger**.
2. **Meet "stronger reason" (10 min).** A **reason** is a "because." When two reasons
   pull opposite ways, you ask **"because of what?"** about each one. The reason that
   holds up — because it helps a person, keeps a promise, or does the most good with the
   least harm — is the **stronger** one.
3. **Weigh it together (15 min).** Pick a decision card and read its two reasons. Place
   each reason on a pan of the scale. Wonder together: which pan should sink? Which
   reason is stronger, and **why**? Say, sign, or point to your answer with a "because."
4. **Weigh your own (10 min).** On your sheet, draw or write one small choice with two
   competing reasons. Mark which is stronger and write or tell your "because." If
   drawing is not your way, build it with stones, act it out, or have a partner scribe
   while you direct.
5. **Close (5 min).** When two reasons collide, ask "because of what?" The reason that
   holds up is the stronger one — and you can always say why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one choice and model the two reasons aloud with a gesture for each,
  then model the "because of what?" question; a non-speaking learner places a stone on
  the heavier pan; a learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to compare two reasons that are *both* strong (keeping
  a promise to a friend vs. keeping a promise to a family) and defend which is stronger
  and why, even when it is hard.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name two reasons for a choice and state
  which is stronger, with a "because"?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's "two reasons, which is stronger, and why"
  sheet (or a built, acted, or partner-scribed version).

## Home connection

Tonight, when a small home choice has two sides, stop and name both reasons, then ask
aloud "because of what?" and tell a grown-up which reason is stronger and why.
