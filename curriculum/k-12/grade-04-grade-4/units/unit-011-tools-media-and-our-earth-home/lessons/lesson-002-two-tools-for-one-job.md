# Lesson 2 — Two Tools for One Job

## Summary

Learners **compare two tools for the same job** and say **which works better and why** for a
given person or situation. They learn that "better" is not one fixed answer — it depends on
who is using the tool and what the situation is (a wide-mouth pot is easy to scoop and pour at
home; a narrow-neck bottle with a lid carries water without spilling). They sort pairs, fill a
"better for whom and why" chart, and defend one choice.

## Objectives

- Compare two tools for the same job and say which works better and why for a given person or
  situation. _(D10.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

Two people can do the very same job with two different tools — and both be right. A wide-mouth
clay pot and a narrow-neck bottle both **carry water**, but the pot is easier to scoop from a
well and pour into a cooking pot, while the lidded bottle is easier to carry on a long walk
without spilling. Saying "which is better" always means asking *better for whom, and in which
situation?* That is the habit of a careful thinker.

## Materials

- Tool-pair cards (pot vs. bottle; broom vs. hand brush; spoon vs. ladle)
- A "which works better, for whom, and why" chart
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the tool-pair cards, each labeled with the job and the two tools.
- Prepare the chart with three columns: *the job*, *the two tools*, and *better for whom /
  which situation, and why*.
- Think of a second pair to do together (two spoons of different sizes; a basket vs. a sack).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Grade 3 compared an *old* tool and a *new* tool
for the same job; Grade 4 compares any **two tools** and must say **which works better and why
for a given person or situation**. The key idea is that "better" is *situational and personal*,
not absolute — this is both a thinking skill and a small lesson in fairness (a tool that suits
one body or one life may not suit another). Model the compare-and-defend sentence once ("The
lidded bottle works better for a person carrying water a long way, because it does not spill;
the wide pot works better for someone scooping and pouring at home"), then guide one pair
together before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep it **global and
egalitarian**: use pairs from many lives — a pot and a jerrycan, a broom and a brush, a basket
and a sack — so no single culture's tools are the default, and ask "who is this tool easier
for?" to honor different bodies and abilities. Honor every body: describe each tool in words
for a learner who is blind or has low vision; a non-speaking learner points to the better tool
and to the person or situation; acting out the job with each tool works for learners who learn
best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sometimes two different tools do the same job. Is one always "better"?
   Not always — it depends on *who* and *where*. Today you learn to compare fairly.
2. **Meet comparing (10 min).** To compare two tools for one job, ask three questions: *What is
   the job? What are the two tools? Which works better — for whom, and in which situation —
   and why?* Follow the worked example: carrying water, a wide pot vs. a lidded bottle.
3. **Compare together (8 min).** For a second pair — sweeping: a long broom vs. a short hand
   brush — say the job, the two tools, and who each is better for. ("The long broom is better
   for sweeping a big floor; the hand brush is better for a small, low space.")
4. **Your own comparison (12 min).** Pick one job and two tools for it. Fill the chart: the
   job, the two tools, and *which works better for whom or which situation, and why*.
5. **Defend your choice (5 min).** Tell a partner your answer and your "because." Can they
   think of a person or situation where the *other* tool is better?
6. **Close (5 min).** "Better" is never one answer. A careful thinker always asks: better for
   *whom*, in *which* situation, and *why*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare one given pair aloud using the frame "For the job of ___, the ___ works
  better for ___, because ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear each tool described and say which works better, for whom, and why;
  a partner scribes the chart.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Point to the better tool and to the person or
  situation; the learner's answer may be drawn and labeled with pictures.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to handle or draw the tools while the learner
  says or points to the better tool and why.
- **Extension:** Compare a third pair and name a **trade-off** — something each tool gives up to
  gain something else.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner compare two tools for the same job and say which
  works better and why for a given person or situation?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed "better for whom and why" chart.

## Home connection

At home, find two tools that do the same job (two knives, two ways to carry water, two ways to
sweep). Tell a grown-up which works better for which person or situation, and why.
