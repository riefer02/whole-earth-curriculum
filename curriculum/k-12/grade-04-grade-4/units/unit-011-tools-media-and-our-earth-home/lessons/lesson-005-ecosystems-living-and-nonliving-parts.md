# Lesson 5 — An Ecosystem's Living and Nonliving Parts

## Summary

Learners describe an **ecosystem** as **living** and **nonliving** parts that depend on each
other, and name **two of its parts**. They sort parts into living and nonliving, arrange a pond
or garden scene, and draw one ecosystem of their own with two living and two nonliving parts
labeled, plus arrows showing who depends on what.

## Objectives

- Describe an ecosystem as living and nonliving parts that depend on each other, and name two of
  its parts. _(D10.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Look out a window or step outside. You are standing inside an **ecosystem**: the **living** parts
(a tree, a bird, a worm) and the **nonliving** parts (sunlight, water, soil, rock) all around
you, **depending on each other**. The tree cannot live without the sun, the water, and the soil;
the bird needs the tree for shelter and food; the worm needs the soil, and the soil is kept rich
by the worm. Every place on Earth — a pond, a desert, a forest, a field — is an ecosystem like
this, made of living and nonliving parts woven together.

## Materials

- Ecosystem part cards (living: tree, bird, worm, fish; nonliving: sun, water, soil, rock)
- A large ecosystem diagram (pond or garden)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the part cards, each labeled "living" or "nonliving."
- Prepare the large diagram with living and nonliving parts labeled and dependence arrows.
- If possible, choose a real nearby spot (a garden, a tree, a puddle) to observe.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Grade 3 built a food chain of plants and animals;
Grade 4 widens to the whole **ecosystem** — living *and nonliving* parts that depend on each
other. The key new idea is that nonliving parts (sun, water, soil, rock, air) are not just
scenery; they are *parts of the system* that living things depend on, and living things change
them in return (worms enrich soil, plants shade and cool, roots hold soil). Model the concept
with the worked diagram, then guide sorting before learners draw their own (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Keep it **concrete and global**: offer ecosystems from many biomes — a pond, a
desert, a mangrove, a forest floor, a field — so no single place is the default, and note that
people are part of ecosystems too, depending on them and caring for them. A light ethical thread
returns throughout the unit: because every part depends on every other, caring for one part
cares for the whole (this grows in Lessons 6–8). Honor every body: describe the cards and
diagram in words for a learner who is blind or has low vision; a non-speaking learner sorts the
cards and points the dependence arrows; acting out an ecosystem works for learners who learn
best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You are standing inside an ecosystem right now. What living things are
   around you? What nonliving things? Today you name its parts and see how they depend on each
   other.
2. **Meet the ecosystem (10 min).** An **ecosystem** is all the **living** parts and the
   **nonliving** parts in a place, depending on each other. Look at the diagram: the **sun**,
   **water**, and **soil** are nonliving; the **tree**, **bird**, and **worm** are living. The
   tree depends on sun, water, and soil; the bird depends on the tree; the worm depends on the
   soil — and the soil is made richer by the worm.
3. **Sort living and nonliving (8 min).** With a partner, sort the cards into two piles: living
   (tree, bird, worm, fish) and nonliving (sun, water, soil, rock). Name two parts from each
   pile.
4. **Draw your own ecosystem (12 min).** Draw one ecosystem you know — a pond, a garden, a field,
   a forest edge. Label **two living** parts and **two nonliving** parts, and draw one arrow
   showing who depends on what.
5. **Share (5 min).** Show your drawing to a partner. Can they find your two living and two
   nonliving parts, and follow your dependence arrow?
6. **Close (5 min).** An ecosystem is living and nonliving parts depending on each other. Every
   part matters — even the soil and the sunlight.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort the given cards and say two living and two nonliving parts aloud, using the
  frame "The ___ is living; the ___ is nonliving; the tree depends on the ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear the parts described and name two living and two nonliving parts by
  voice; a partner builds the scene by feel or describes it.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Sort the cards and point to two living and two
  nonliving parts, then point the dependence arrow.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to sort and draw while the learner says or
  points to the living and nonliving parts.
- **Extension:** Add a third living and third nonliving part and a second dependence arrow, or
  explain what might happen to the bird if the tree were cut down.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe an ecosystem as living and nonliving
  parts that depend on each other, and name two of its parts?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The drawn ecosystem with two living and two nonliving parts labeled
  and one dependence arrow.

## Home connection

At home or nearby, find one small ecosystem — a tree, a puddle, a garden bed. Name two living
and two nonliving parts in it, and tell a grown-up one way they depend on each other.
