# Lesson 3 — Author, Purpose, and Audience

## Summary

Learners identify the **author**, **purpose**, and **audience** of a message and explain **how
they shape it**. They see that a message's **author** is who made it, its **purpose** is what it
is for (to *inform*, *persuade*, *entertain*, or *warn*), and its **audience** is who it is for —
and that author and audience shape *how* the message is said: a health poster for families uses
plain words and pictures, while a toy ad for children uses bright colors and exciting words.
They read messages, name all three, and make one of their own.

## Objectives

- Identify the author, purpose, and audience of a message and explain how they shape it.
  _(D10.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Every message you meet — a sign, a song, a poster, a game — was made by someone, for a reason,
and for somebody. The **author** is the person or group who made it; the **purpose** is what it
is for; the **audience** is who it is for. These three shape each other: a health worker writing
for families uses plain words and pictures, while a toy company writing for children uses bright
colors and exciting words. When you can name all three, you can read any message with clear
eyes — you see *who* is speaking, *why*, and *to whom*.

## Materials

- Message cards (a health poster, a toy ad, a road-safety sign)
- An "author–purpose–audience" chart
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the message cards, each labeled with its author, purpose, and audience.
- Prepare the chart with four columns: *author*, *purpose*, *audience*, and *how the author and
  audience shape it*.
- Think of one message to model ("a school lunch menu: author, the cook; purpose, inform;
  audience, students — so it lists dishes and times in simple words").

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Grade 3 named a message's **purpose** and
**audience**; Grade 4 adds the **author** and, crucially, asks learners to **explain how author
and audience shape the message**. Model the three questions — *who made it? what is it for? who
is it for?* — and the "how it shapes it" sentence once, then guide a second message before
independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the media stance warm, not suspicious:
naming an author, purpose, and audience is *understanding*, not catching someone out — the
health worker wants to help you stay well, and the toy company wants you to want the toy; both
are honest once you can see them. Use messages from many places and lives — a market notice, a
road sign, a song, a menu — so no one home is the default; a spoken story or song is a real
message with an author, purpose, and audience too. Honor every body: read each message aloud
for a learner who is blind or has low vision and let them name author, purpose, and audience by
voice; a non-speaking learner points to the author, purpose word, and audience picture; acting
out each message works for learners who learn best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Every message was made by someone, for a reason, and for somebody. Today
   you learn to name all three — and see how they shape the message.
2. **Meet author, purpose, and audience (10 min).** The **author** is who made it; the
   **purpose** is what it is for (*inform, persuade, entertain, warn*); the **audience** is who
   it is for. Look at the health poster: author, a health worker; purpose, to inform; audience,
   families. Because its audience is families, it uses plain words and pictures.
3. **Name all three together (8 min).** For each card, ask the three questions — *who made it?
   what is it for? who is it for?* — and say how the author and audience shape it: "The toy ad
   is by a toy company, its purpose is to persuade, and its audience is children — so it uses
   bright colors and exciting words."
4. **Make your own message (12 min).** Make a simple message of your own — a poster, a notice, a
   story title, a sign. Label its **author**, its **purpose**, and its **audience**, and write or
   say one way those shaped it.
5. **Share and guess (5 min).** Show your message to a partner. Can they name its author,
   purpose, and audience? Tell them one way the audience shaped how you made it.
6. **Close (5 min).** Name the author, purpose, and audience of any message, and you can see who
   is speaking, why, and to whom — and nothing can speak to you unseen.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name author, purpose, and audience of one message together aloud, using the frame
  "The author is ___, the purpose is to ___, and the audience is ___, so ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear each message described and name its author, purpose, and audience by
  voice; a partner scribes the learner's own message.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Read or see each message and point to the author, the
  purpose word, and the audience picture; the learner's own message may be drawn and labeled with
  pictures.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to draw and label while the learner says or
  points to the author, purpose, and audience.
- **Extension:** Explain how changing the audience would change the same message (a road-safety
  sign written for young children instead of drivers), or make a second message with the same
  purpose for a different audience.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify the author, purpose, and audience of a
  message and explain how they shape it?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's own message, labeled with author, purpose, audience,
  and one "how they shape it" note.

## Home connection

At home, find one message — a label, a notice, a song, a poster. Tell a grown-up its author,
its purpose (inform, persuade, entertain, or warn), and its audience, and one way the audience
shaped how it was made.
