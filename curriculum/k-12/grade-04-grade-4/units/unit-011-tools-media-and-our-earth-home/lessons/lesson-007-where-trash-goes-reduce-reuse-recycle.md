# Lesson 7 — Where Trash Goes: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle

## Summary

Learners explain **what happens to trash after it is thrown away** and name **"reduce, reuse,
recycle" in order of what is best to do first**. They trace the path of a thrown-away wrapper —
to a bin and a landfill, or littered, down a drain to a river and the sea — and learn that
**reduce** comes first (make less waste), then **reuse** (use it again), then **recycle** (turn it
into something new). They sort actions and rank the three R's.

## Objectives

- Explain what happens to trash after it is thrown away and name "reduce, reuse, recycle" in
  order of what is best to do first. _(D10.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

When you throw something away, it does not disappear. A wrapper thrown in a **bin** is carried to
a **landfill** or a dump, where it sits for a very long time. A wrapper dropped on the ground can
be **litter** — washed by rain down a drain, into a river, and out to the sea. Because making
things and throwing them away has real effects, people have a smart order: **reduce** first (don't
take or make what you don't need), then **reuse** (use it again or pass it on), then **recycle**
(turn it into something new). Reduce is best to do *first*, because making less waste is better
than managing waste.

## Materials

- A "where trash goes" picture sequence
- Action cards (reduce, reuse, recycle)
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the picture sequence: thrown away → bin → landfill; or littered → drain → river → sea.
- Prepare the action cards, each with a short note: *reduce — don't take or make what you don't
  need; reuse — use it again or pass it on; recycle — turn it into something new*.
- If you can, find out one true fact about where your own town or village's trash goes.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Grade 2 followed what happens to a piece of litter;
Grade 3 named resources that run out versus come back and ways to use less; Grade 4 puts it
together: explain **where trash goes** and name the **reduce–reuse–recycle order**. Teach the
order explicitly and give the reasoning — reduce is best *first* because making less waste avoids
the problem, reuse second because it keeps an item's value, recycle last because it still costs
energy and water (the "waste hierarchy," U.S. EPA). Model the trash path and the ranking once,
then guide practice before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). **Keep it
stewardship-framed, never doom or guilt**: the message is "we can do this, and many people
already do," not "you are ruining the Earth." **Honor no-wealth and global contexts**: reduce,
reuse, and recycle are *universal* practices — mending clothes, carrying a reusable bag, saving
containers, feeding scraps to animals, passing things on — not something that requires buying
"eco" products; many of the world's most careful stewards are people with the least. Name that
some places have no curbside recycling at all, and that is not a failing of the people there.
Frame the "recycle last" idea without shaming places that lack systems. Honor every body:
describe the sequence aloud for a learner who is blind or has low vision; a non-speaking learner
points to the three R's and ranks them; acting out the three R's works for learners who learn
best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** When you throw something away, where does it go? It does not vanish. Today
   you follow it — and learn the smart order of reduce, reuse, recycle.
2. **Follow the trash (10 min).** Look at the sequence. A wrapper thrown in a **bin** is carried
   to a **landfill**. A wrapper dropped on the ground can be **litter** — rain washes it down a
   drain, into a river, and out to the sea. The first choice is not "where do I put it" but "did
   I need it at all?"
3. **Meet the order (8 min).** The smart order is **reduce, reuse, recycle**. **Reduce** first:
   don't take or make what you don't need. **Reuse** second: use it again or pass it on.
   **Recycle** third: turn it into something new. Say it together, in order, with a reason for
   each.
4. **Sort and rank (10 min).** Sort the action cards into the three R's. Then rank them
   best-first: reduce, reuse, recycle. Write one sentence for why reduce comes first.
5. **Your own path (7 min).** Draw the path of one thing you used today — a wrapper, a bag, a
   bottle — from your hand to where it could end up, and write which R would be the better first
   choice.
6. **Close (5 min).** Reduce, reuse, recycle — in that order. Make less first; it is the kindest
   thing to do for the Earth and for each other.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Say the three R's in order aloud using the frame "First ___, then ___, then ___,
   because ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear the sequence described and say the order and reasons by voice; a
  partner scribes the drawing.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Point to the three R's in order and rank the cards;
  the learner's path may be drawn and labeled with pictures.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to sort and draw while the learner says or
  points to the three R's in order.
- **Extension:** For each R, name a second example from your own life, or explain why recycling
  still costs energy and water even though it is good.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain what happens to trash after it is thrown
  away and name reduce, reuse, recycle in order of what is best to do first?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The drawn trash path with the three R's ranked in order.

## Home connection

At home, follow one thing you were about to throw away. Could you reduce (not take it in the
first place), reuse (use it again or pass it on), or recycle it? Tell a grown-up the order and
your choice.
