# Lesson 4 — Check It With a Second Source

## Summary

Learners **check whether a simple claim is true by finding a second source and comparing
them**. They learn the habit: don't trust one voice alone — find a **second source**, note
whether the two **agree** or **disagree**, and only then **conclude**. They work a modeled
example (an octopus has three hearts), check a claim against two sources, and write a
conclusion that says what to believe and why.

## Objectives

- Check whether a simple claim is true by finding a second source and comparing them.
  _(D10.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Someone tells you something surprising — "an octopus has three hearts!" or "it always rains
more in the afternoon." How do you know whether to believe it? You don't have to decide on one
voice. You **check**: find a **second source** — another book, another person, another page — and
**compare** them. If both say the same thing, you can believe it more; if they **disagree**, you
look closer. This is what careful people everywhere do, from a market seller checking a price to
a scientist checking a result.

## Materials

- Claim cards with two short "source" notes each
- A "two-source check" chart
- Paper and pencils

## Preparation

- Prepare the claim cards, each with two short written sources that agree or disagree. For the
  modeled claim, use "an octopus has three hearts" with two agreeing sources (a nature book and
  an aquarium website). For a second card, use a partly false claim such as "camels store water
  in their humps" with two disagreeing sources (one says "store water," a better one says "store
  fat, not water").
- Prepare the chart: *claim, Source 1, Source 2, agree or disagree?, conclusion*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Grade 3 learned that messages have purposes and
audiences; Grade 4 learns the core fact-checking habit: **check a claim against a second source
and compare**. Teach this as an explicit skill with a worked example first, then guided practice
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model "read the claim → read Source 1 → read Source 2 → do
they agree? → conclude" once, then do a second claim together. Keep the stance calm and
empowering, not fearful: checking is not distrust of people; it is care for the truth, and two
sources that agree make us *more* confident, not less trusting. Use claims from many places and
lives — about animals, weather, plants, food — so no single culture's knowledge is the default,
and honor that oral knowledge (a grandparent's account) is a real source that can be checked
against a book just as a book can be checked against another book. Be honest about uncertainty:
if two sources disagree, the conclusion may be "I am not sure yet — I need a third source," and
that is a fine, honest answer. Honor every body: read sources aloud for a learner who is blind
or has low vision; a non-speaking learner points to "agree" or "disagree" and to the conclusion;
acting out "one voice vs. two voices" works for learners who learn best by moving.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** How do you know something is true? Not by one voice alone. Today you
   learn to **check a claim with a second source**.
2. **Meet the two-source check (10 min).** A **claim** is something someone says is true. To
   check it: (1) read or hear the claim, (2) find **Source 1**, (3) find **Source 2**, (4) ask
   *do they agree?*, (5) **conclude**. Follow the worked example: "an octopus has three hearts."
   Source 1 (a nature book) says yes; Source 2 (an aquarium website) says yes. They agree — so
   we conclude the claim is likely true.
3. **Check together (8 min).** Take the claim "camels store water in their humps." Source 1 says
   "store water"; Source 2 says "store fat, not water." Do they agree? No. What do you conclude?
   ("I need a third source — I am not sure yet.")
4. **Check your own claim (12 min).** Pick a claim card. Read both sources, mark whether they
   agree or disagree, and write your **conclusion**: what you now think and why.
5. **Compare conclusions (5 min).** Trade with a partner. Did you reach the same conclusion? If
   not, what did one of you notice that the other missed?
6. **Close (5 min).** One voice can be mistaken; two sources that agree are stronger; two that
   disagree mean look closer. Checking is caring for the truth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Check one claim together aloud using the frame "Source 1 says ___, Source 2 says
  ___, they agree/disagree, so I conclude ___."
- **Blind/low vision:** Hear the claim and both sources read aloud and say "agree" or "disagree"
  and the conclusion by voice.
- **Deaf/hard of hearing / non-speaking:** Read or see the claim and both sources and point to
  "agree" or "disagree" and to the conclusion.
- **Motor differences:** Direct a partner's hands to write the chart while the learner says or
  points to agree/disagree and the conclusion.
- **Extension:** Check a claim of your own choosing in two real places (a book and a website, or
  two people) and write the conclusion with both sources named.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner check a simple claim by finding a second source
  and comparing them, and state a conclusion?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed two-source check chart with a conclusion.

## Home connection

At home, hear a claim — something a family member or a screen says. Find a second source (another
person or a book) and compare. Tell a grown-up whether they agree or disagree and what you
conclude.
