# Lesson 5 — Keeping Knowledge Alive

## Summary

Learners discover **how** a knowledge tradition survives across generations: by being
**watched, practiced, remembered, and taught** to the next person. They hear the true
story of **Hōkūleʻa**, a Hawaiian voyaging canoe that in 1976 sailed from Hawaiʻi to
Tahiti guided only by the stars, waves, and the teaching of master navigator **Mau
Piailug** — a living example of knowledge kept alive and passed from teacher to
apprentice.

## Objectives

- Explain how a knowledge tradition is kept and passed from one generation to the next.
  _(D08.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of something you learned by watching someone, then practicing, then doing it
yourself — riding, cooking, counting, a game. That is how knowledge passes on: someone
shows, you try, you remember, and one day you show someone else. Knowledge traditions
around the world pass the same way, hand to hand across the years. When a tradition
almost disappears, the same chain — teacher to learner — can bring it back.

## Materials

- A "how knowledge passes" flow card set, including the Hōkūleʻa story cards
- A "passing it on" flow chart (watch, practice, remember, teach)
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the flow cards: **watch** (a child watches an elder), **practice** (the child
  tries it), **remember** (the child holds it in mind), **teach** (the child shows the
  next child).
- Prepare the Hōkūleʻa story cards: a double-hulled canoe, a star chart, a master
  navigator, an apprentice pointing at the sky.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Teach the four-step chain as a worked
example first** (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model "watch → practice → remember
→ teach" with a simple skill, then connect it to the Hōkūleʻa story. **Tell the
Hōkūleʻa story accurately and with respect** (see Resources): the Polynesian Voyaging
Society built Hōkūleʻa and, in 1976, sailed her from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti using
traditional wayfinding; the master navigator Mau Piailug of Satawal taught the art to
others, reviving a tradition that had nearly been lost. **This is a story of survival
and revival, not a museum piece** — hold the navigators as living experts, and never
suggest their knowledge was "beaten by" modern tools. Honor every body: describe the
cards in words for a learner who cannot see them; a learner who does not use a speaking
voice points or acts. If a learner's own family has a passed-down skill, celebrate it
as a living knowledge tradition.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of something you learned by watching someone and
   practicing. Who taught you? How did you learn it?
2. **Meet the four steps (10 min).** Knowledge passes in a chain: **watch** someone who
   knows, **practice** it yourself, **remember** it, then **teach** it to the next
   person. Walk the chain together with a small skill (a clap pattern, a knot, a song).
3. **Hear the story of Hōkūleʻa (15 min).** Long ago, Polynesian navigators crossed the
   ocean reading the stars, waves, clouds, and birds. For a time, this knowledge almost
   disappeared. Then, in 1976, the canoe **Hōkūleʻa** sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti —
   about 2,500 miles of open ocean — guided not by instruments but by master navigator
   **Mau Piailug**, who read the sky and sea. He taught apprentices to do the same, so
   the knowledge would not be lost again. Watch → practice → remember → teach, across
   the ocean and the years.
4. **Trace the chain (10 min).** On your chart, under each step, write or draw how
   wayfinding (or another tradition) moved through it: Mau watched and learned, he
   practiced, he remembered, and he taught the next navigators.
5. **Pass something on (5 min).** Teach one small thing you know to a partner — a word,
   a move, a fact. Notice the chain working in you.
6. **Close (5 min).** Knowledge stays alive only when people pass it on. Every teacher
   was once a learner, and every learner can become a teacher.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Trace the chain with just two steps (watch, teach) and heavy picture
  support, saying them together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a small object passed hand to hand and describe
  the four steps aloud as it travels.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Order the four flow cards and point to each step, instead
  of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — order them and sign "watch," "practice,"
  "remember," and "teach" as you go.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use larger cards and a stable chart; point or eye-gaze
  while a partner moves each card.
- **Extension:** Choose a knowledge tradition from your own or another culture and write
  its watch-practice-remember-teach chain with the names of the people who carry it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order the four steps (watch, practice,
  remember, teach)?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner explain how one tradition is kept
  and passed on — by speaking, signing, writing, or acting?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to teach you a small skill they learned from an older person, then
teach it to someone else. Notice the chain: who taught them, and whom will you teach?
