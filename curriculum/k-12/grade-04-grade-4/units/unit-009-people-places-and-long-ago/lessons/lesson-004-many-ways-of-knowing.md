# Lesson 4 — Many Ways of Knowing

## Summary

Learners meet the idea of a **knowledge tradition** — a body of knowledge a people has
built, tested, and kept over many generations — through three examples: **herbal
medicine** (knowing which plants heal), **star navigation** (reading the sky to find
one's way across the sea), and **oral history** (keeping a people's story alive by
telling it). They learn that these are real and deep ways of knowing, held with respect
and never ranked as "lesser" than any other knowledge.

## Objectives

- Describe a knowledge tradition of a specific people and what it helps them know and
  do. _(D08.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of something your family knows how to do and has known for a long time — a way of
cooking, a song, a way to read the weather or fix a thing. That is a small knowledge
tradition. Around the world, whole peoples have built much larger bodies of knowledge
over thousands of years: which plants heal, how to cross an ocean by the stars, how to
remember a people's history by telling it. Each is a real way of knowing, built by
observing, trying, and passing on — and each deserves respect.

## Materials

- "Knowledge traditions" picture cards (herbal medicine, star navigation, oral history)
- A three-part chart with a line under each tradition
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the three cards. **Herbal medicine:** a healing plant. **Star navigation:** a
  star chart above a canoe. **Oral history:** a storyteller with listeners.
- Prepare the chart with three boxes and a line under each to write what the tradition
  knows.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Hold all three traditions as real
knowledge, never as "primitive" or "less than" science.** Herbal medicine, star
navigation, and oral history are careful, tested ways of knowing, built over
generations — and modern medicine, astronomy, and history still learn from them.
(UNESCO calls such living knowledge "intangible cultural heritage" — see Resources.)
This is exactly the philosophy commitment to **holding many ways of knowing side by
side, never ranking one above another.** Introduce the concept with a worked example
first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model one tradition fully, then guide the
others. Use the names of real peoples where you can — Ayurveda and other South Asian
traditions, Traditional Chinese Medicine, Amazonian and Aboriginal plant knowledge;
Polynesian and Micronesian wayfinding; West African griots and Aboriginal Australian
songlines. Honor every body: describe each card in words for a learner who cannot see
it; a learner who does not use a speaking voice points or acts. If a learner's own
family has a knowledge tradition, welcome it with honor.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of something your family or community knows how to do well
   and has known for a long time. How did people learn it?
2. **Meet the idea (5 min).** A **knowledge tradition** is knowledge a people has built
   and kept over many generations. People observe, try, remember, and pass it on.
3. **Meet three traditions (20 min).** For each card, say what the tradition knows and
   does:
   - **Herbal medicine:** peoples across the world have learned which plants heal —
     Ayurveda in South Asia, traditional Chinese medicine, and the plant knowledge of
     Amazonian and Aboriginal peoples. They know which leaf soothes, which root helps,
     and how to prepare them.
   - **Star navigation:** Polynesian and Micronesian navigators read the stars, waves,
     clouds, and birds to cross thousands of miles of open ocean without instruments.
   - **Oral history:** West African griots and Aboriginal Australian storytellers keep a
     people's history alive by telling and singing it, so it is not lost.
4. **Write what each knows (10 min).** On your chart, under each tradition, write or
   draw one thing it knows or does.
5. **Close (5 min).** There are many ways of knowing. Herbal medicine, star navigation,
   and oral history are deep and real — each is a people's careful answer, kept and
   passed on.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one tradition with heavy picture support and one shared sentence
  said together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a leaf, a boat-shaped object, and tell or hear a
  story — describing each tradition aloud.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to each card and to what it knows, instead of
  speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — point to each and sign "plant,"
  "stars," and "story" as you go.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use larger cards and a stable chart; point or eye-gaze
  while a partner writes.
- **Extension:** Choose one tradition and name the specific people who practice it, and
  one thing it has taught the wider world.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner match each tradition to what it knows
  (plant healing, sky reading, story keeping)?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner describe one tradition and what it
  helps its people do — by speaking, signing, writing, or acting?
