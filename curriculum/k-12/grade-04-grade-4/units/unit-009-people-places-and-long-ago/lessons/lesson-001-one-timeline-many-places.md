# Lesson 1 — One Timeline, Many Places

## Summary

Learners build a **timeline with dates** and place events from **several world
regions** in order. The new skill this year is the *date*: Grade 3 ordered local
landmarks by clue; Grade 4 puts events from Egypt, the Indus Valley, Mesoamerica,
India, and the Andes on one line and uses **dates** — counted in **BCE** ("Before
Common Era") and **CE** ("Common Era") — to know which came first. They also learn
that this is **one way of counting years among many** that peoples have used.

## Objectives

- Use a timeline with dates to sequence events from different places in order.
  _(D08.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

A timeline is a line that puts events in order, from furthest back to today. You can
line up the order of your own day — wake, eat, learn, play, rest — but to line up big
events across the whole world, people use **dates**, year numbers written on the line.
Dates let us place a pyramid in Egypt and a city by the Indus River and a carved head
in Mesoamerica on the *same* line and see which is older. Dates are one way — among
many — that people count and record time.

## Materials

- A long timeline strip (one per learner or pair)
- A set of "world events" cards for the worked example, each with a drawing, a place, and a date
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the six event cards. **Great Pyramid** (Giza, Egypt), about 2560 BCE. **Grid
  city** (Mohenjo-Daro, Indus Valley), about 2500 BCE. **Carved stone head** (Olmec,
  Mesoamerica), about 1200 BCE. **Stone pillar** (Ashoka, India), about 250 BCE. **Lake
  city** (Tenochtitlan, Mesoamerica), founded 1325 CE. **Mountain roads** (Inca,
  Andes), about 1450 CE.
- Draw the timeline strip with a "long ago" left end and a "today" right end, and a
  mark where the year-count changes from BCE to CE.
- Decide the line reads left-to-right and keep it the same for everyone.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Sequencing by date is a foundational
skill, so teach it with a worked example first** (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006):
model placing two cards, saying the date and checking "2560 BCE is further back than
2500 BCE, so the pyramid goes before the city." Then guide the rest before learners
work independently. **Teach BCE/CE simply and honestly:** "CE means Common Era — the
years most of the world now counts forward from; BCE means Before Common Era, the
years before that point." **This is one calendar among many** — different peoples mark
years in their own ways (Islamic, Hebrew, Chinese, and many others), and a timeline
using BCE/CE is a shared tool, not the only truth about time. This is a **global
story, never one people's triumph**: the events are chosen from Africa, South Asia,
and the Americas so no single region is the "main" story. Honor every body: describe
each card in words for a learner who cannot see it; a learner who does not use a
speaking voice points or places the card. Dates are approximate ("about") — that
honesty is part of doing history well.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Think of one very old thing you have heard about
   from anywhere in the world, and one thing that happened recently. Which is older?
   How do you know?
2. **Meet the timeline and the dates (10 min).** Look at the worked example: a line
   with a "long ago" end and a "today" end. The year-count changes from **BCE** (Before
   Common Era) to **CE** (Common Era) partway along. In BCE, a bigger number means
   *earlier*. In CE, a bigger number means *later*.
3. **Place the world events (15 min).** One by one, place the six cards by their dates:
   - **Great Pyramid** (Egypt), about 2560 BCE
   - **Grid city** (Indus Valley), about 2500 BCE
   - **Carved stone head** (Mesoamerica), about 1200 BCE
   - **Stone pillar** (India), about 250 BCE
   - **Lake city** (Mesoamerica), 1325 CE
   - **Mountain roads** (Andes), about 1450 CE
   For each, say the date and check it against its neighbors: "2560 BCE is earlier than
   2500 BCE, so the pyramid comes first."
4. **Build your own order (10 min).** Mix the cards. Put them back in date order on
   your own line, and say or write each date as you place it.
5. **Close (5 min).** You just did what historians do: you used **dates** to order
   events from many places on one line. One timeline, many places — and many ways of
   counting the years.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only three cards with clear dates far apart, and place them together
  saying each date aloud.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sequence real objects by touch and say or sign each
  date and place; a partner reads the labels.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Order the cards and point to the date on each, or use
  picture-only cards, instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — read the dates with your eyes and sign
  "before," "after," and "long ago" as you place each one.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large, stable line and bigger cards; point or
  eye-gaze to each spot while a partner moves the card.
- **Extension:** Add two more events from your own region or family with approximate
  dates, and place them on the line.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place the six events in date order from
  furthest back to most recent?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner read or say each date and explain
  how they know one event is earlier than another — by speaking, signing, pointing, or
  acting?

## Home connection

At home, ask someone to help you write down three family or community events with
their dates (a birth, a move, a festival) and place them in order on a line. Notice
which came first and how the dates tell you.
