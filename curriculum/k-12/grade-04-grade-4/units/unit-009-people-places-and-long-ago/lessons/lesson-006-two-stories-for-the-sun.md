# Lesson 6 — Two Stories for the Sun

## Summary

Learners hear **two peoples' stories** for the same natural event — the Sun's daily
journey across the sky — the Japanese story of **Amaterasu** and the Egyptian story of
**Ra**. They see that both peoples **observed the real Sun closely** and told stories to
make meaning of what they saw. The lesson holds story and observation **side by side,
with equal respect**, never ranking one people's story or one way of knowing above
another.

## Objectives

- Compare how two cultures explain the same natural event through story and observation.
  _(D08.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Every day the Sun appears to rise in the morning and set at night. You have probably
watched its light and shadow change across a day. People everywhere have watched this
same event and asked what it means. Some tell stories; some measure and observe; many do
both. Two peoples — in Japan and in Egypt — watched the very same Sun and told two
different, beautiful stories about it. Both are ways of honoring the same mystery.

## Materials

- "Two stories of the Sun" cards (Amaterasu and Ra)
- A two-column chart (Japan / Egypt; rows for "story" and "what people observed")
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare two cards with short, respectful tellings. **Japan — Amaterasu:** the Sun
  goddess; when she hid in a cave, the world went dark, and the other spirits lured her
  out with a mirror and laughter, so light returned. **Egypt — Ra:** the Sun god who
  sailed his boat across the sky each day and through the dark beneath the world each
  night, to rise again at dawn.
- Prepare the chart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **This is the unit's most sensitive
lesson — treat both stories with full respect, and never rank "myth" against
"science."** These are two peoples' living cultural stories, told to make meaning of
the Sun, not "wrong guesses" that science later "corrected." The Japanese and Egyptian
peoples also observed the sky with real care (temples aligned to sunrise; solstices
marked for rice-growing and the seasons). Modern astronomy observes the same Sun and
explains the apparent rising and setting by Earth's rotation. **Hold all three — story,
careful observation, and modern astronomy — side by side, as different ways people make
sense of the same event, none "better" than the others.** This follows philosophy §11
(inquiry, not creed). Tell both stories warmly and without ranking; let learners weigh
them with growing respect. Honor every body: tell the stories aloud in words for a
learner who cannot see the cards; a learner who does not use a speaking voice acts or
draws. If a learner's own culture has a Sun story, welcome it with honor.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Watch the light today, or remember the sky: where does the Sun
   seem to rise, and where does it set? What do you notice?
2. **Hear two stories (15 min).** Listen to both tellings:
   - **Japan — Amaterasu:** the Sun goddess grew so sad and angry at her brother's
     misdeeds that she hid in a cave, and the world went dark. The other spirits set a
     mirror outside and began to laugh and dance. Curious, she peered out, saw her own
     bright light in the mirror, and came out — so light returned to the world.
   - **Egypt — Ra:** the Sun god sailed his boat across the sky by day, and through the
     dark lands beneath the world by night, to be reborn each dawn. The rising and
     setting of the Sun was his daily journey.
3. **Notice what both peoples observed (10 min).** Both peoples watched the *same real
   Sun*: it appears in the east, crosses the sky, and disappears in the west, and its
   light and warmth make life possible. Each story is one way of making meaning of what
   they saw.
4. **Compare on your chart (10 min).** For each column, write or draw the **story** and
   the **observation** behind it. Then say one way the two stories are different and one
   way they are alike (both honor the Sun's light and its daily return).
5. **Close (5 min).** The same Sun, two peoples' stories — and both watched it closely.
   Story and observation are both ways of knowing the world, and both deserve respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Hear one story fully and retell just its ending with heavy picture
  support.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear both stories told aloud and feel a warm lamp (the
  Sun) and a mirror; describe each story's Sun in your own words.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to each card and to the Sun's path (rise, cross,
  set), instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — act each story's Sun with your hands and
  body, and sign "Sun," "rise," and "set."
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large chart; point or eye-gaze to each part while
  a partner writes.
- **Extension:** Add a third people's Sun story you know or find, and tell what it
  observes and what it means.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner retell each story's main idea and name
  what both peoples observed (the Sun's daily path and light)?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner compare the two stories without
  ranking one above the other — by speaking, signing, drawing, or acting?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Does our family or culture have a story about the Sun, the Moon,
or the stars?" Listen to it, and tell it back. Notice what it observes and what it
means.
