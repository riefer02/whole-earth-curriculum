# Lesson 10 — Peaceful Resistance

## Summary

Learners meet **three peaceful resistances from three world regions** — the Māori
village of **Parihaka** in Aotearoa New Zealand (1881), **Gandhi's Salt March** in India
(1930), and the **Defiance Campaign** in South Africa (1952) — and explain what people
did and **why they chose peaceful, nonviolent ways**. They see that refusing unfairness
without hurting others is a real, brave, and effective path that people have chosen
again and again.

## Objectives

- Describe a peaceful way people have resisted unfair treatment in history and why they
  chose it. _(D08.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever seen something unfair and wanted to change it? There are many ways to
resist unfairness. Some people fight; but many peoples have chosen a different way —
**peaceful resistance**: refusing, marching, boycotting, and welcoming, without hurting
anyone. From Aotearoa New Zealand to India to South Africa, ordinary people chose peace
to win dignity. Their choice still teaches us today.

## Materials

- "Peaceful resistance" story cards (Parihaka, Salt March, Defiance Campaign)
- A "why peaceful?" chart (what they did / why they chose peaceful ways)
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare three cards. **Parihaka (Aotearoa New Zealand, 1881):** Māori leaders Te
  Whiti-o-Rongomai and Tohu Kākahi led their people in peaceful resistance when their
  land was taken — they plowed the land and, when soldiers came, welcomed them with
  bread and children's songs, refusing to fight. **Salt March (India, 1930):** Mohandas
  Gandhi led a long walk to the sea to make salt peacefully, breaking an unfair law that
  taxed and controlled salt. **Defiance Campaign (South Africa, 1952):** thousands of
  people calmly refused unjust "pass" laws that controlled where Black South Africans
  could go, accepting arrest with dignity rather than fighting.
- Prepare the chart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **This is the unit's closing lesson — tell
all three stories with respect and accuracy, never as one people's triumph over another,
but as three peoples' choice for dignity.** Teach the three with a worked example first
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): tell Parihaka fully, then guide the Salt March and
the Defiance Campaign. **Why peaceful?** Each people chose it for both moral and
practical reasons — a belief in non-harming (Gandhi's *ahimsa*; Parihaka's commitment to
peace), and because nonviolence wins supporters and avoids hurting others. **Hold the
tension honestly but age-appropriately:** these were real struggles against real
unfairness; do not soften the injustice, but do not dump adult despair — always connect
to the people's courage and agency, and to the hope of fairness. Distinguish fact from
value: *what happened* is history; *that peaceful resistance is good* is a value many
people share. Honor every body: tell the stories in words for a learner who cannot see
the cards; a learner who does not use a speaking voice acts or draws. If a learner's own
people have a story of peaceful resistance, welcome it with honor.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you saw something unfair. What are some ways a
   person could change it? Which ways hurt others, and which do not?
2. **Hear three peaceful stories (20 min).** Listen to each:
   - **Parihaka (Aotearoa New Zealand, 1881):** When their land was taken, the people of
     Parihaka resisted *peacefully* — they plowed the land in protest, and when soldiers
     arrived, they welcomed them with bread and children's songs, refusing to fight.
   - **The Salt March (India, 1930):** Gandhi and thousands walked about 240 miles to the
     sea to make their own salt, peacefully breaking an unfair law, as part of the long
     movement for India's freedom.
   - **The Defiance Campaign (South Africa, 1952):** thousands calmly refused unjust
     "pass" laws that controlled where Black South Africans could go, accepting arrest
     with dignity rather than violence.
3. **Ask why peaceful (10 min).** For each, say why the people chose peaceful ways: they
   believed in not harming others, and they knew nonviolence wins supporters and keeps
   the focus on the unfairness itself.
4. **Fill your chart (10 min).** For each of the three, write or draw **what they did**
   and **why they chose peaceful ways**.
5. **Close (5 min).** Three peoples, three places, one brave choice: to resist unfair
   treatment without hurting anyone. Peaceful resistance is a real and powerful path —
   and it is still walked today.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Hear one story fully and retell just its peaceful act and one reason, with
  heavy picture support.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear the stories told aloud and hold a seed, a pinch of
  salt, and a folded paper "pass," describing each peaceful act in your own words.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Act or point to each peaceful act, instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are visual — act each peaceful act with your hands and
  body, and sign "peace," "march," and "refuse."
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large chart; point or eye-gaze to each part while
  a partner writes.
- **Extension:** Choose one movement and find one more thing it achieved over time, and
  one peaceful method it used that you noticed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner match each movement to its peaceful act?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner describe one peaceful resistance and
  why its people chose it — by speaking, signing, writing, or acting?
