# Lesson 9 — Rules, Leaders, and Fair Decisions

## Summary

Learners explore how groups make decisions through **rules and leaders** — by
**proposing, discussing, and deciding** — and what people can do when a decision is
**unfair**: speak up, gather others, and propose a better rule. They meet the
**Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Great Law of Peace**, a long-lived example of
decision-making built to include many voices, and connect it to their own classroom and
community.

## Objectives

- Explain how rules and leaders make decisions in a group, and identify what people can
  do when a decision is unfair. _(D08.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

Every group you are part of — a class, a team, a family — has rules and some way of
deciding things: who goes first, who shares what, how to settle a question. Some ways of
deciding are fair (everyone has a say) and some are not (a few decide for everyone). The
question of how to decide fairly is one people everywhere have asked — and people can
always speak up when a decision hurts some and helps others.

## Materials

- A "how a group decides" flow chart (one per learner or pair)
- A fairness scenario card (one per pair)
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare the flow chart: **propose** → **discuss** → **decide**, with a branch from
  "decide" to "fair" (everyone can live with it) and "unfair" (speak up, gather others,
  propose a better rule).
- Prepare the scenario card: a shared garden where the group decides who waters on which
  day, but one child is given no turn because they are the youngest.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Model the decide-then-check flow with a
worked example first** (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): walk one decision from propose
to discuss to decide, then model the "unfair" branch ("if the rule leaves someone out,
they can speak up, gather others, and propose a fairer rule"). **Teach the Haudenosaunee
Great Law of Peace accurately and with respect** (see Resources): the Haudenosaunee
confederacy of several nations made decisions by long discussion aimed at agreement
(consensus), with clan mothers holding an important role and the voices of different
nations all included — a living tradition, not a museum piece. **Connect to the child's
own life:** the classroom and family are real groups where fairness can be practiced.
Honor every body and every family shape: describe the chart in words for a learner who
cannot see it; a learner who does not use a speaking voice points, signs, or writes. Keep
the "unfair decision" example gentle and close to home, and always connect it to agency —
something the learner can do.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a rule in your class or family. Who made it? How was it
   decided? Was everyone's voice heard?
2. **Meet how groups decide (10 min).** A group decides in steps: **propose** an idea,
   **discuss** it so many voices are heard, then **decide**. Leaders help guide this —
   but a fair decision includes the people it affects.
3. **Meet a fair-way example (10 min).** The **Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) peoples** of
   North America built the **Great Law of Peace**, a way of deciding by long discussion
   aimed at agreement, with clan mothers and the voices of several nations all included.
   It aimed for decisions everyone could live with.
4. **What to do when it's unfair (10 min).** Read the garden scenario. Walk the
   "unfair" branch: the child left out can **speak up**, **gather others** who see the
   unfairness, and **propose a better rule** (a turn for everyone). Discuss: which step
   would you take first, and why?
5. **Practice in your own group (10 min).** Choose one real question in your class or
   family. Propose, discuss, and decide it — then check: is it fair to everyone? If not,
   what will you do?
6. **Close (5 min).** Fair groups decide with many voices, and unfair decisions can be
   changed. Speaking up, gathering others, and proposing a better rule is how fairness
   grows.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Walk only the "unfair" branch with the garden story and one shared
  sentence said together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear the scenario told aloud and decide the steps
  orally, with a partner recording them.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the flow-chart steps and to the three "unfair"
  actions, instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The chart is visual — point to the steps and sign "speak up,"
  "gather," and "propose" as you go.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large, stable chart; point or eye-gaze to each
  step while a partner writes.
- **Extension:** Write a short "fair decision" plan for a real group you belong to,
  naming who proposes, who discusses, and how everyone's voice is heard.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order the steps (propose, discuss,
  decide)?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner name at least one thing people can
  do when a decision is unfair — by speaking, signing, writing, or acting?

## Home connection

At home, think of one rule your family has. Ask who made it and whether everyone's voice
was heard. If it feels unfair to someone, talk together about one step you could take to
make it fairer.
