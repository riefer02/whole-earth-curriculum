# Lesson 4 — Survival Body Parts and Behaviors

## Summary

Every animal has what it needs to live where it lives. Learners meet the idea of an
**adaptation**: a **body part** or a **behavior** that helps an animal **survive** in its
**environment**. A bird's beak reaches food, a camel's hump stores energy for long dry
stretches, a frog's strong legs power its leap, a polar bear's thick coat holds heat.
Learners sort body parts and behaviors onto a chart, then describe one animal of their
own, discovering that each living thing carries its own solutions — and none is "better"
than another, only fitted to its place.

## Objectives

- Describe how animals' body parts and behaviors help them survive in their environment.
  _(D06.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Look at a bird pecking seeds, or a gecko clinging to a wall, or a dog panting on a hot
day. Each one is doing what helps it stay alive where it lives. A bird's **beak** is
shaped for the food it eats; a gecko's **feet** grip smooth surfaces; a dog's **panting**
cools its body. These are **adaptations** — body parts and behaviors that fit an animal
to its home. The same idea lives everywhere people do: people in cold places build warm
homes and wear thick coats, while people in hot places seek shade and drink lots of
water. Every living thing solves the problem of its own environment.

## Materials

- Animal picture cards (a bird with a beak, a camel, a frog, a polar bear or arctic fox,
  an insect that camouflages)
- Paper and crayons
- A chart with two columns: "Body part" and "Behavior"

## Preparation

- Prepare and shuffle the animal picture cards.
- Draw the two-column chart where everyone can see it.
- Have a short list of animals from many places ready (desert, forest, ocean, ice).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Teach the idea with a **worked example** —
model one animal ("a bird's beak helps it reach food") — then let learners sort the cards
(guided practice) before describing their own animal. Guard against ranking: an
adaptation is not a "superpower" that makes one animal better than another; every animal
is fitted to *its* environment, and a camel and a polar bear each solve a different
problem brilliantly (the egalitarian and ethics lenses). Use animals from many biomes and
continents so no single region is the default (global lens). This is a factual,
evidence-based idea — scientists describe adaptations from long observation of animals in
the wild — and it is also a way to wonder at the living world with care. Keep it warm,
never frightening.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** You have built honest experiments. Now meet the
   living world: how does an animal stay alive in its own place?
2. **Meet an adaptation (10 min).** An **adaptation** is a body part or behavior that
   helps an animal **survive**. A **body part** is something the animal *has* (a beak, a
   hump, thick fur). A **behavior** is something the animal *does* (burrowing, migrating,
   hiding). Say each word and point to its example.
3. **Sort them (15 min).** Match each animal card to its survival job. Which body parts
   help? Which behaviors help? Place each on the chart under "Body part" or "Behavior"
   and say how it helps: "The camel's hump stores fat for long, dry journeys."
4. **Describe your own animal (15 min).** Choose any animal, draw it, and label two body
   parts or behaviors. Beside each, write how it helps the animal survive in its home.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every animal carries its own solutions for its own place. None is
   "better" — each is fitted. Tomorrow you will see how those animals depend on the rest
   of their home.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match one body part to one survival job with a partner; say it aloud.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the body part on the card and to the survival job
  it helps with.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a real shell, a feather, or a fur-like cloth, and
  describe how it might help the animal.
- **Extension:** Compare two animals from different environments and tell how the same
  job (staying warm, finding food) is solved in two different ways.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner match a body part or behavior to how it
  helps the animal survive?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The labeled animal drawing with two survival notes, kept for
  the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, watch one animal with a grown-up — a pet, a bird, or an insect — and name one
body part or behavior that helps it live, and why.
