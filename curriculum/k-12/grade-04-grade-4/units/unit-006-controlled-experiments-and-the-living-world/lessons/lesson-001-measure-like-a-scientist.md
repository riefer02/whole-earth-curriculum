# Lesson 1 — Measure Like a Scientist

## Summary

Last year learners recorded observations with drawings, words, and simple tables. This
year their observations become **measurements**: a **number** with a **unit**. Learners
meet four tools — a **ruler** (length, in centimeters), a **scale** (mass, in grams), a
**thermometer** (temperature, in degrees Celsius), and a **magnifier** (to look closely).
They measure one object with the right tool, write the number *with its unit*, and
compare numbers with a partner to see why a shared unit — not "big" or "small" — makes a
record someone else can trust.

## Objectives

- Use simple tools (such as a ruler, scale, thermometer, or magnifier) to measure and
  record observations with units. _(D06.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever said a thing is "long" or "heavy" or "hot"? Those words are a start, but
they do not tell anyone exactly *how* long or heavy or hot. If you say a fish is "long"
and your friend says it is "very long," you cannot compare. A **measurement** uses a
number *and* a unit — "30 centimeters long," "250 grams," "21 degrees Celsius" — so two
people, in two different places, can agree on exactly the same fact. From a market stall
weighing rice to a nurse taking a temperature, measurement is how people everywhere share
observations that anyone can check.

## Materials

- Rulers marked in centimeters
- A scale (balance or kitchen scale)
- A thermometer
- A magnifier
- A small object to observe (a leaf, seed, stone, or coin)

## Preparation

- Set out the four tools where learners can reach them.
- Choose one shared object for the whole group to measure first (a coin or a stone).
- Prepare a simple record space (paper or board) with columns: **Object | Tool |
  Number | Unit**.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"), so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. The core skill — reading a tool and writing a number *with a
unit* — is foundational, so teach it with a **worked example and guided practice**
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model measuring the shared object once on each tool,
say the number and unit aloud, then let pairs measure their own object before recording
alone. Emphasize the *unit*; a number without a unit is half a measurement. Use units
that are shared worldwide — centimeters, grams, degrees Celsius — so a record is legible
to a learner in any country (the global and egalitarian lenses); note gently that people
have used many units across history and places, and standard units exist so everyone can
trust and compare. Where learners still find a tool hard, "not yet" is information, not a
verdict — they try again tomorrow.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last year you recorded what you saw with drawings
   and words. Today you will make your records exact: a **measurement** is a **number**
   with a **unit**.
2. **Meet the tools (10 min).** Point to each tool and say what it measures and its
   unit: a **ruler** measures length in **centimeters**; a **scale** measures mass in
   **grams**; a **thermometer** measures temperature in **degrees Celsius**; a
   **magnifier** helps you look closely at tiny details. Say them together.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch and join in: measure the shared object. Say the
   number aloud, then say the unit. "This coin is about 2 centimeters across." "This
   stone has a mass of about 30 grams." Write each as **number + unit**.
4. **Measure your own (20 min).** Choose your object. Pick the right tool, measure, and
   write the **number and its unit**. Measure it a second time. Do your two numbers
   match? If not, measure again and decide which reading is most careful.
5. **Close (5 min).** Compare with a partner: did you get the same number and unit?
   When you both use the same unit, you can trust each other's record. Keep your
   measurements — you will use them again.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Measure length only, with a partner reading the ruler marks aloud.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the correct tool for each measurement; a partner
  writes the number and unit.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a ruler with raised marks, a talking scale, and
  touch to feel length and warmth; describe the number aloud.
- **Extension:** Measure the same object three ways (length, mass, and temperature, if
  safe) and write a sentence comparing your two readings of each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner choose the right tool and say the number
  *with its unit*?
- **Self:** The learner checks their record: "Did I write a number **and** a unit?"

## Home connection

At home, measure one thing with a grown-up — the length of a shoe in centimeters, the
mass of a cup of rice in grams, or the warmth of bath water with a hand — and write the
number with its unit.
