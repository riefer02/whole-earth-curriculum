# Lesson 10 — Fossils and Rock Layers: Earth's Long Story

## Summary

Earth keeps a diary in stone. **Sediment** settles over time and hardens into stacked
**rock layers** — the **oldest at the bottom**, the **youngest at the top**. **Fossils** —
the preserved remains or traces of plants and animals — are caught inside those layers,
so a fossil buried deep is older than one near the surface. Learners build a layered model
with a "fossil" in each layer, order them oldest to youngest, and close the unit by
recalling that measurement, repetition, and the patient reading of clues all build
knowledge we can trust.

## Objectives

- Explain how fossils and rock layers give clues about Earth's long history.
  _(D06.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever stacked books one on top of another? The book at the bottom was put there
first. Earth works the same way: **sediment** — tiny bits of sand and mud — settles and
hardens into layers, the **oldest at the bottom** and the **youngest at the top**. Caught
inside those layers are **fossils**: the shells, bones, and leaf prints of living things
from long ago. A fossil deep down is older than one near the surface. It is how we read
Earth's long story — not from a book someone wrote, but from clues the Earth left behind,
the way a footprint in mud tells you someone walked there before you.

## Materials

- A "rock layers" model (stacked colored papers or play-dough slabs) with a "fossil" in each
- A real or pictured fossil (a shell, a leaf print, or a photo)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Build (or have ready) a stack of three or more layers, each with a different "fossil"
  (a leaf print, a shell, a drawn bone) placed inside.
- Set out a real or pictured fossil for learners to observe closely.
- Prepare a space to draw a labeled cross-section.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"), and it is the unit's closing lesson, so
weave in **retrieval practice** — ask learners to recall the tools of measurement, the
reason for repeated trials, and the water cycle — before and after the new idea. Teach
layer order with a **worked example** (the stacked-books model), then let learners build
their own layers (guided practice). Hold origins gently, as philosophy §11 asks: the fossil
record is a story scientists read from evidence gathered over centuries, held as a story
grounded in evidence — alongside other traditions' stories of beginnings, which we honor
without ranking. Keep the global and environment lenses: fossils are found on every
continent and belong to the shared history of all life, and caring for the Earth is caring
for a story still being written. This lesson's labeled cross-section is a **summative**
portfolio artifact for the living-world and Earth strands.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** This whole unit you have measured, repeated tests, and
   followed energy and water. Today: how do we read the Earth's own long story?
2. **Meet the layers (10 min).** **Sediment** — sand and mud — settles and hardens into
   **rock layers**. The **oldest** layer is at the **bottom**; the **youngest** is at the
   **top**. Say it together: bottom is oldest, top is youngest.
3. **Meet a fossil (10 min).** A **fossil** is the preserved remains or trace of a living
   thing from long ago — a shell, a leaf print, a bone, a footprint. Hold or look at the
   fossil. What living thing might have left this trace?
4. **Build your layers (15 min).** Stack three or more layers, placing a "fossil" in each.
   Point to the **oldest** and the **youngest** fossil, and say why: the deeper the layer,
   the older the fossil.
5. **Read the story and close (10 min).** What does each layer's fossil tell us about what
   lived then? Draw and label your cross-section, oldest at the bottom. Close by naming
   one thing from this whole unit you can now do — measure, repeat, build, or read clues —
   that makes your knowledge trustworthy.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Stack three layers and say "bottom is oldest" with a partner; match one
  fossil to its layer.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Place the fossils in order from bottom to top and point to
  the oldest and youngest.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the stacked layers (different textures) and each
  "fossil" (a shell, a leaf, a stick), and describe which is deepest.
- **Extension:** Infer, from the fossils' order, which living thing lived earliest and
  which latest, and write one sentence of evidence for your answer.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order layers oldest-to-youngest and explain
  why a deeper fossil is older?
- **Summative (portfolio):** The labeled rock-layer cross-section with fossils, kept as the
  unit's closing portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

At home, look with a grown-up for layers and traces — a stack of soil in a pot, rings in a
tree stump, or old photos stacked in a drawer — and say which is oldest and how you know.
