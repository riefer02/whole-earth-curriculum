# Lesson 2 — Rounding & Comparing to One Million

## Summary

Learners **round** multi-digit numbers to the nearest ten, hundred, thousand,
ten-thousand, or hundred-thousand by finding the nearer landmark on a number line,
and **compare** two big numbers by reading their digits left to right. The key ideas:
rounding is a choice between two neighbors (and the midpoint decides which way), and
comparing starts at the largest place and moves right.

## Objectives

- Use place value to 1,000,000 to read, write, round, and compare multi-digit whole
  numbers. _(D05.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Sometimes you do not need the exact number — you need a quick one that is close
enough. A farmer says the harvest is "about forty thousand" sacks; a traveler says
the next town is "about five hundred" kilometers away; a report says a city has "nearly
half a million" people. Rounding turns a precise number into a neighbor you can hold
in your head. Comparing is different: it asks *which is bigger, and how do I know?* —
the same careful reading you use to see which pile of coins, which field, or which
number is larger.

## Materials

- A number line strip to one million (per pair)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool
- A set of six-digit number cards (per pair)

## Preparation

- Give each pair a number line strip and a set of number cards.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) a worked example of rounding 463,215 to the nearest
  hundred-thousand.
- Clear space to move and compare aloud.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Rounding is a **place-value skill**,
not a memorized trick: teach it with a number line so learners *see* the two
neighbors and the midpoint, then generalize to "look at the digit one place to the
right" (Van de Walle, Karp & Bay-Williams, 2019). Model once as a **worked example**
(philosophy §13), then guide practice before independent work. Comparing is the same
left-to-right reading of digits they learned in Grade 3, grown to six digits. **A
number's size is a fact about a collection, never a worth about a person; and
rounding a number does not make a learner "careless" — it is a different, useful
question.** The decimal system we use is one number system among many (Ifrah, 2000);
where a home language reads numbers in a different order, honor that reading. For
learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, sign the comparison symbols and midpoint;
for learners who are blind or have low vision, use a tactile number line and read
places by touch. Learners with limited movement direct a partner to mark the line.
Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — quick numbers (5 min).** When someone asks "how far?" or "how many?",
   you often give a round, close number. Name a time you said "about…" instead of the
   exact count. That is rounding.
2. **Worked example — rounding to the hundred-thousand (10 min).** On the number line,
   find 463,215. Its two hundred-thousand neighbors are 400,000 and 500,000. Where is
   the midpoint? 450,000. Is 463,215 closer to 400,000 or 500,000? It is past the
   midpoint, so it rounds **up** to 500,000. Say it: "463,215 is about 500,000."
3. **Guided practice — round to any place (15 min).** Round 463,215 to the nearest
   ten-thousand, then thousand, then hundred, then ten. Each time, find the two
   neighbors, find the midpoint, and choose the nearer one. Record each rounded number.
4. **Compare two numbers (8 min).** Take two number cards, like 463,215 and 463,150.
   Read left to right: hundred-thousands equal (4 = 4), ten-thousands equal (6 = 6),
   thousands equal (3 = 3). Now the hundreds: 2 is greater than 1, so we already know
   463,215 is the greater number — no need to look further. Write it with the symbol:
   463,215 > 463,150.
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade cards. Round each other's number and compare
   two numbers. Do you agree on the nearer landmark and which number is larger? Fix it
   together, kindly.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: rounding picks the nearer neighbor; the midpoint
   decides the tie. Comparing reads the digits left to right, largest place first.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Round to the nearest ten and hundred first; add bigger places another
  day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a tactile number line and movable markers to
  feel the nearer neighbor.
- **Extension:** Round a number to two different places and explain which rounded
  number is more precise and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner round a six-digit number to a given
  place and explain the two neighbors and midpoint?
- **Self:** Can the learner compare two six-digit numbers, write the correct symbol,
  and tell how they know?

## Home connection

At home, round a real big number — a price, a distance, a crowd size — to two
different places and tell a grown-up which rounding is closer to the exact number and
why.
