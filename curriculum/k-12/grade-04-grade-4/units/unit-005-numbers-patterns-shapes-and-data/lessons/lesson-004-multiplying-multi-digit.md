# Lesson 4 — Multiplying Multi-Digit Numbers

## Summary

Learners **multiply** multi-digit whole numbers by breaking one factor into its place
values and finding **partial products** — often drawn as an **area model** — then
adding the parts. For example, 23 × 45 becomes (20 + 3) × (40 + 5) = 800 + 100 +
120 + 15 = 1,035. Multiplication becomes a rectangle you can see and count.

## Objectives

- Add, subtract, multiply, and divide multi-digit whole numbers, and use estimation
  to check that answers are reasonable. _(D05.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

Multiplication answers "how many in all?" when the same group repeats: rows of
seedlings in a field, boxes of fruit on a truck, tiles on a floor, people in rows of
a bus. A big count is often a rectangle — a number of rows times a number in each
row. When you split that rectangle into its tens and ones, the big problem becomes
four small, friendly ones you already know. That is the whole trick of multiplying
big numbers: break it into parts you can see.

## Materials

- Grid paper (per learner)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool
- A place-value chart (per learner)

## Preparation

- Give each learner grid paper and a place-value chart.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) the worked example: 23 × 45 as an area model.
- Clear space to draw and count aloud.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The new skill is multiplying a
two-digit by a two-digit number; the key move is **distributing across place value**
— breaking a factor into tens and ones and finding partial products (Van de Walle,
Karp & Bay-Williams, 2019). Teach the **area model** as a worked example (philosophy
§13) before the compact algorithm: the rectangle shows *why* the partial products
exist. Build on Grade 3's multiplication within 100; the facts stay the same, only
the numbers grow. **The speed of a learner's recall of facts is not a measure of
their worth, and a product is a fact about quantities, never about a person.** The
decimal system that makes this tidy is one system among many (Ifrah, 2000); people
have multiplied by doubling, by grouping, and with lattice and other methods across
traditions. Count in the home language. For learners who are Deaf or hard of
hearing, sign each partial product; for learners who are blind or have low vision,
use a tactile grid and count sections by touch. Learners with limited movement
direct a partner to draw. Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — the same group repeats (5 min).** Recall a rectangle of things you have
   seen — a grid of tiles, rows of plants, a tray of cups. How many in all? Rows ×
   in-each-row is multiplication.
2. **Worked example — the area model (10 min).** Draw a rectangle for 23 × 45: one
   side 23, the other 45. Split 23 into 20 and 3, and 45 into 40 and 5. You get four
   parts: 20 × 40 = 800, 20 × 5 = 100, 3 × 40 = 120, 3 × 5 = 15. Add them: 800 + 100
   + 120 + 15 = 1,035. So 23 × 45 = 1,035. Say it together.
3. **Guided practice — draw and split (15 min).** Try 34 × 27. Split 34 into 30 and 4,
   and 27 into 20 and 7. Find the four partial products and add them. Check: does your
   answer make sense? (30 × 20 = 600, and there is more, so it should be bigger than
   600.)
4. **Connect to the written method (8 min).** Record the same partial products in
   writing, lined up by place value, then add. Notice the written method is the same
   four products, just stacked.
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade a problem. Draw the rectangle and compare
   partial products. Do you agree on the product? Fix it together, kindly.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: split a big multiplication into parts you know, count
   each part, and add. A rectangle is a multiplication you can see.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Multiply a one-digit number by a two-digit number first; add a
  two-digit by two-digit another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a tactile grid; feel the four sections and
  count each.
- **Extension:** Multiply a three-digit by a two-digit number (like 234 × 27) using
  six partial products.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner draw an area model for a two-digit by
  two-digit product, find the partial products, and add them correctly?
- **Self:** Can the learner check their product by estimation or by a second method?

## Home connection

At home, find a real rectangle of things (tiles, rows of a garden, a tray) and
multiply its rows by its count, splitting into tens and ones. Tell a grown-up your
partial products.
