# Lesson 13 — Perimeter of Rectangles

## Summary

Learners find the **perimeter** of a rectangle as the distance around its four sides,
then discover the shortcut — add the length and width and double, or 2 × length +
2 × width — and apply it to real problems like fencing a garden or framing a picture.
Perimeter becomes "how far around."

## Objectives

- Apply formulas for the area and perimeter of rectangles to solve real-world and
  mathematical problems. _(D05.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

How much fence to go around a field? How much trim to border a rug? How far is one
lap around a yard? Each asks the same thing: **how far around** a shape. That is
perimeter. Walk the four sides once and you have it; notice the two lengths and two
widths, and you get a shortcut. People everywhere measure perimeter to fence fields,
border cloth, and build walls — the distance around is the first thing a builder
needs to know.

## Materials

- String or rope and a ruler (per pair)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each pair string and a ruler.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) the worked example: an 8-by-5 rectangle.
- Clear space to walk and measure.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Perimeter is the **distance around**:
teach it by walking and measuring, then generalize to **2 × length + 2 × width** (or
2 × (length + width)) as a shortcut (Van de Walle, Karp & Bay-Williams, 2019). Model
once as a **worked example** (philosophy §13), then guide practice. Contrast
perimeter (around) with area (cover) from Lesson 12 — naming the difference is a key
retrieval-and-comparison moment. **A learner's pace at distinguishing area and
perimeter is not a measure of their worth; a perimeter is a fact about a shape, never
about a person.** Measuring the boundary of land is a human and cross-cultural
practice, from ancient field markers to modern fences. Count in the home language.
For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, sign the walk-around; for learners who
are blind or have low vision, feel the four sides of a tactile rectangle and count
along the edge. Learners with limited movement direct a partner to walk the border.
Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — how far around (5 min).** If you walked all the way around your desk or
   the room, how far would that be? That distance is the **perimeter**.
2. **Worked example — an 8-by-5 rectangle (10 min).** Walk the four sides of a
   rectangle that is 8 long and 5 wide: 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26. Notice the two lengths
   and two widths. Shortcut: **2 × 8 + 2 × 5 = 16 + 10 = 26.** Same answer, fewer
   steps. Say the formula: **perimeter = 2 × length + 2 × width.**
3. **Guided practice — walk, then compute (15 min).** Measure several rectangles with
   string, add the four sides, then check with the formula. Do the two ways always
   agree?
4. **Real problems (10 min).** Solve: a garden is 12 meters by 7 meters — how much
   fence goes around it? A picture is 30 cm by 20 cm — how much border to frame it?
   For each, say the formula and the answer.
5. **Check with a partner (3 min).** Trade a problem. Do you agree on the perimeter?
   Walk it together, kindly.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: perimeter is the distance around — add the sides, or
   double the length plus the width.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Add the four sides only today; add the formula another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the edges of a tactile rectangle and count the
  distance around.
- **Extension:** Given a perimeter (like 30) and one side (like 9), find the other
  side and explain how you know.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner find the perimeter of a rectangle by
  adding its sides and by the formula, and state the answer with units?
- **Self:** Can the learner explain the difference between perimeter (around) and
  area (cover)?

## Home connection

At home, measure the distance around a table, a rug, or a room with your steps or a
string, and tell a grown-up the perimeter.
