# Lesson 14 — Converting Length & Distance

## Summary

Learners **convert** measurements of length and distance within a system — mostly the
**metric** system, which most of the world uses — by counting how many smaller units
fit in a bigger one: 10 millimeters (mm) = 1 centimeter (cm), 100 cm = 1 meter (m),
and 1,000 m = 1 kilometer (km). They solve distance problems by choosing the unit
that fits and converting when needed.

## Objectives

- Convert measurements within a system and solve problems involving distance, time,
  volume, and money. _(D05.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

"About how far?" needs the right-sized unit. You would not measure a journey in
centimeters or a pencil in kilometers. Most of the world measures with meters and
kilometers, where each unit is ten or a thousand of the next — a system built to make
converting easy (Van de Walle, Karp & Bay-Williams, 2019). Some places also use older
units like inches, feet, and miles; knowing how to convert *within* a system is what
lets you move between "about this long" and "exactly this long." Whether you pace a
field or read a road sign, converting is just counting smaller units inside bigger
ones.

## Materials

- A meter stick or a measured strip (per pair)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each pair a meter stick or measured strip.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) the conversion ladder as a worked example.
- Clear space to measure and count.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Conversion is **counting within a
system**: teach it concretely — count 100 centimeters in a meter, then generalize the
metric ladder (10 mm = 1 cm, 100 cm = 1 m, 1,000 m = 1 km) (Van de Walle, Karp &
Bay-Williams, 2019). Model once as a **worked example** (philosophy §13), then guide
practice. The metric system is the global default and is egalitarian in spirit — one
coherent system for everyone, not a patchwork of region-specific units; where
customary units (inches, feet, miles) are in local use, treat them respectfully as
one system among several, never ranked. **A learner's pace at memorizing conversion
facts is not a measure of their worth; a conversion is a fact about units, never
about a person.** Count in the home language. For learners who are Deaf or hard of
hearing, sign each conversion; for learners who are blind or have low vision, feel a
meter stick and count the centimeter marks by touch. Learners with limited movement
direct a partner to measure. Mastery arrives at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — the right-sized unit (5 min).** Would you measure a road in hand-widths
   or a pencil in kilometers? You pick the unit that fits, then convert when you need
   to change units.
2. **Worked example — the metric ladder (10 min).** Look at a meter stick: count 100
   centimeters in it. Learn the ladder: **10 mm = 1 cm, 100 cm = 1 m, 1,000 m = 1 km.**
   Convert together: 3 m = 300 cm (count three hundreds). 2 km = 2,000 m.
3. **Guided practice — convert both ways (15 min).** Convert by counting: 5 cm = ___
   mm; 400 cm = ___ m; 2.5 km = ___ m; 7,000 m = ___ km. For each, say how many
   smaller units fit in the bigger unit.
4. **Distance problems (10 min).** Solve: a race is 3 km — how many meters? A table is
   150 cm — how many meters and centimeters? A runner goes 2 km then 500 m more — how
   many meters in all?
5. **Check with a partner (3 min).** Trade a conversion. Do you agree? Count it
   together, kindly.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: converting is counting how many smaller units fit in a
   bigger one. Pick the unit that fits, and convert by counting.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Convert cm to mm and cm to m today; add km another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the centimeter marks on a meter stick and count
  by touch.
- **Extension:** Convert a decimal distance (2.5 km) and explain the decimal's meaning
  in meters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner convert among mm, cm, m, and km by
  counting, and solve a distance problem with the right unit?
- **Self:** Can the learner explain why they chose a particular unit for a given
  distance?

## Home connection

At home, measure something in one unit (a room in meters, a pencil in centimeters)
and convert it to a smaller or bigger unit. Tell a grown-up how you counted.
