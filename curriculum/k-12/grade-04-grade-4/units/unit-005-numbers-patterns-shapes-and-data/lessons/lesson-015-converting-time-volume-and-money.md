# Lesson 15 — Converting Time, Volume & Money

## Summary

Learners convert within three systems — **time** (60 seconds = 1 minute, 60 minutes =
1 hour), **volume** (1,000 milliliters = 1 liter), and **money** (in most currencies,
100 smaller units = 1 larger unit) — and solve problems like how long a journey takes,
how much water a jug holds, and how much change to give. Conversion becomes counting
within a system.

## Objectives

- Convert measurements within a system and solve problems involving distance, time,
  volume, and money. _(D05.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

"How long until dinner?" "How much water is left in the jug?" "How much change should
I get back?" These everyday questions are conversions. Time counts in sixties, water
in thousands, and money in hundreds — and each system has its own rhythm you must
count carefully. Money is especially human: people have used cowrie shells, beads,
salt, and many other things before coins and bills (Davies, 2002), and today most
currencies split into 100 smaller units, which makes change a matter of counting in
tens and hundreds.

## Materials

- A clock face or timer (per pair)
- A liter bottle and a small cup (per pair)
- Play money or real coins and small bills (per pair)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Gather the clock, the liter bottle, and the money for each pair.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) the three conversion tables as a worked example.
- Clear space to count, pour, and trade.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). These three systems have **different
bases** — 60 for time, 1,000 for liters, 100 for money — so teach each with its own
concrete anchor (a minute of tapping, a liter of pours, a tray of coins) (Van de
Walle, Karp & Bay-Williams, 2019). Model each as a **worked example** (philosophy
§13), then guide practice. For money, use the **home currency** and its real
subunits; in most currencies 100 smaller units make one larger unit, but honor local
exceptions. Money's history is global and varied — cowries, beads, salt, and metals
all served as exchange before and alongside coins (Davies, 2002; Zaslavsky, 1999) —
and no currency is "better" than another. **A learner's pace at conversion is not a
measure of their worth; a conversion is a fact about units, never about a person.**
Count in the home language. For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, sign each
conversion; for learners who are blind or have low vision, feel the coins and pour
the water by touch. Learners with limited movement direct a partner. Mastery arrives
at its own pace.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — three rhythms (5 min).** Time counts in sixties, water in thousands,
   money in hundreds. Each system has its own rhythm — you just have to count it
   carefully.
2. **Worked example — time (8 min).** Tap one beat each second for a full minute — 60
   beats. Learn: **60 seconds = 1 minute, 60 minutes = 1 hour.** Convert: 2 hours =
   120 minutes; 90 minutes = 1 hour 30 minutes.
3. **Worked example — volume (7 min).** Pour the small cup into the liter bottle: it
   takes many cups — 1,000 milliliters make 1 liter. Convert: 2 L = 2,000 mL; 500 mL
   = half a liter.
4. **Worked example — money (10 min).** Count the coins: 100 smaller units make one
   larger unit in most currencies. Convert: 250 of the small unit = 2 large units and
   50 small. Now practice making change: the price is 1 large unit and 25 small; you
   pay 2 large units — how much change?
5. **Guided practice (10 min).** Solve mixed problems: a journey is 2 hours 15 minutes
   — how many minutes? A jug holds 3 L — how many mL? You buy two items and pay with a
   larger bill — how much change? For each, say how you counted.
6. **Check with a partner (3 min).** Trade a problem. Do you agree? Count it together,
   kindly.
7. **Close (2 min).** Remember: converting is counting within a system — know its
   rhythm (60, 1,000, 100) and count.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Convert time only today; add volume and money another day.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Count real coins by feel and pour water to feel the
  volume.
- **Extension:** Solve a two-step money problem (buy two items, give change from a
  larger bill) and explain each step.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner convert within time, volume, and money
  systems and solve a real problem in each?
- **Self:** Can the learner explain which "rhythm" (60, 1,000, or 100) belongs to
  which system and why?

## Home connection

At home, convert a real amount — the minutes in a task, the milliliters in a bottle,
or the change from a purchase — and tell a grown-up how you counted.
