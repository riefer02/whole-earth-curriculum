# Lesson 5 — Music of Another Culture

## Summary

Learners meet music from more than one part of the world and learn that every
tradition has its **own instruments**, **scales**, and **rhythms**. They listen (or
read descriptions) of West African drumming, Indian classical music, and Indonesian
gamelan, describe what they hear, and name one respectful question — treating the
music as a living tradition, not a museum piece.

## Objectives

- Describe how the music of another culture uses its own instruments, scales, or
  rhythms, and name one respectful question about it. _(D07.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Every music has its own voice. A goblet-shaped drum from West Africa speaks with
interlocking rhythms; a sitar and tabla from India ride a melody-framework called a
**raga** over a rhythm-cycle called a **tala**; bronze gongs of an Indonesian
**gamelan** ring together in its own five- and seven-note scales. None of these is
"better" — each is a whole world of sound built by living musicians who learned it from
the people before them. When you hear music you have never heard before, the kindest
thing you can do is listen closely and ask, "How does this music work, and what does it
mean to the people who make it?"

## Materials

- Audio or video of music from more than one culture
- Instrument guide graphic (djembe, sitar and tabla, gamelan gongs)

## Preparation

- Choose short, respectful recordings made or shared by the communities themselves —
  for example, West African djembe drumming, Indian classical music (sitar, tabla,
  tambura), and Indonesian gamelan. If audio is not available, the descriptions and
  guide graphic are enough.
- Prepare a simple listening chart: instruments | scale or melody | rhythm | one
  respectful question.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The stance is the same as Lesson 2:
these are **living traditions**, and the goal is accurate, respectful description plus
curiosity. Model the skill: after one recording, name the instruments you hear, say
something about the melody's shape or the rhythm's pattern, and pose one respectful
question. Be honest that a short recording can only hint at a tradition, and the
deepest learning comes from the musicians themselves — if you can invite a local
musician, do. Guard against "exotic" language ("weird," "strange," "primitive"); use
neutral description ("fast," "steady," "ringing," "many parts at once"). Some music is
sacred or for specific occasions only — honoring that boundary is part of respect.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You know how to read rhythm and melody. Today you listen to how
   other cultures build music with their **own** instruments, scales, and rhythms.
2. **Meet three traditions (15 min).** Look at the guide graphic and listen to (or
   read about) each one:
   - **West African drumming:** a **djembe** and low drums play **interlocking
     rhythms** — different patterns that fit together into one groove.
   - **Indian classical music:** a **sitar** plays a **raga** (a melodic framework)
     while a **tabla** keeps a **tala** (a rhythm cycle), with a drone underneath.
   - **Indonesian gamelan:** bronze **gongs** and metal keys ring together in
     **slendro** (five-note) and **pelog** (seven-note) scales, often for ceremonies
     and shadow-puppet plays.
   For each, describe what you notice: its instruments, its scale or melody, its
   rhythm.
3. **Listen closely (10 min).** Listen again to one tradition. Fill your chart: what
   instruments do you hear? Does the melody step or leap? Is the rhythm steady, quick,
   or layered? Use calm, neutral words.
4. **Ask a respectful question (10 min).** Write one question you would ask a living
   musician of that tradition — something you truly want to understand, like "When is
   this music played, and what does it mean for the occasion?" Share it with a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every music is a living world. Listen closely, describe
   honestly, and ask with respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a word bank (instruments, fast/slow, steady, ringing, layered)
  and sentence starters ("I hear…", "I wonder…").
- **Extension:** Compare two traditions on the same chart and name one thing they
  share and one thing that makes each its own.
- **Support (deaf/hard-of-hearing):** Describe rhythm through felt vibration (a drum
  on the floor) and read the visual labels to learn the instruments and scales.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner describe a tradition's instruments,
  scale or melody, and rhythm, and offer a respectful question?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed listening chart with the learner's
  respectful question.

## Home connection

At home, listen to music from a culture different from yours with a grown-up and
describe together what instruments, scale, and rhythm you hear, then write one
respectful question about it.
