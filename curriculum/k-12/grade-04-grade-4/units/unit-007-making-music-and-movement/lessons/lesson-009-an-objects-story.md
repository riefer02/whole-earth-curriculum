# Lesson 9 — An Object's Story

## Summary

Learners discover that every designed object tells a story about its place. Using a
three-part frame — **materials** (what it is made of), **needs** (what it is for), and
**ideas** (what it means) — they study objects from more than one culture, such as a
Māori woven flax basket, a gourd calabash, and an Andean woven cloth, and explain how
each reflects the culture that made it.

## Objectives

- Explain how an object from another culture reflects that culture's materials, needs,
  and ideas. _(D07.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

An object is never just a thing — it is an answer to a place. A woven basket is made
of what grows nearby and is shaped for what people must carry. A dried gourd becomes a
bowl, a cup, or a rattle because it is light, hollow, and everywhere in a hot, dry
place. A thick woolen cloth tells you the maker's home is cold and that they herd
animals, and its patterns carry the weaver's community and beliefs. Read an object
carefully and you can hear the land, the weather, and the people who made it.

## Materials

- Images or real examples of objects from more than one culture
- Object-story frame (materials, needs, ideas)

## Preparation

- Choose a few objects to study — for example, a Māori **kete** (a basket woven from
  harakeke, New Zealand flax), a **gourd calabash** (used as bowl, cup, or rattle
  across many regions), and an **Andean woven cloth** (llama or alpaca wool with
  natural dyes). Prefer images and accounts from the communities or cultural
  institutions.
- Copy an object-story frame for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Like Lessons 2 and 5, it carries the
"living heritage" stance: objects are read with respect, not labeled "primitive" or
"clever-for-them." Model the skill with a worked example — take one object and name
its materials (flax leaves), its needs (carrying and storing food), and its ideas
(weaving patterns and protocols that carry care for the plant and the community). Then
have learners do the same. Use neutral, accurate language and give credit to the
specific culture by name. Remind learners that an object's "ideas" can include beliefs,
stories, or values we must *ask about* rather than assume. If a local elder or maker
can share an object's true story, invite them — that is the most faithful source. The
goal is that a learner can say, in three clear parts, *why this object looks the way it
does*.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You designed something for a user last time. Today you read
   objects other people have designed — and each one tells a story.
2. **Meet the three-part frame (10 min).** Look at the graphic. Every object can be
   read in three parts:
   - **Materials:** what is it made of? (What grows or is found nearby?)
   - **Needs:** what is it for? (What problem did it solve?)
   - **Ideas:** what does it mean? (What beliefs, stories, or values does it carry?)
3. **Read a worked example (10 min).** A Māori **kete** basket is woven from
   **harakeke** (New Zealand flax), a plant that grows there — that is its material.
   It is used for **carrying and storing food** — that is its need. Its weaving
   patterns and the respectful way the plant is cut carry the community's **care and
   knowledge** — that is its idea. See how the object reflects its place?
4. **Read another object (15 min).** Choose a gourd calabash or an Andean cloth. Fill
   your frame: What is it made of, and why is that material available? What need does
   it serve? What ideas — beliefs, stories, values — does it carry? Write one sentence
   for each part.
5. **Close (5 min).** Share your three-part story. An object is a message from its
   place: what grows there, what people need, and what they value.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a word bank (plant, wool, gourd, carry, store, keep warm, care,
  community) and sentence starters for each part.
- **Extension:** Study a fourth object and compare how two different cultures solve the
  same need (e.g., carrying water) with different materials.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the material, act out the need, and point to a
  symbol for the idea.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner explain an object's materials, needs,
  and ideas, connecting each to the culture that made it?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed object-story frame.

## Home connection

At home, choose one everyday object and tell its three-part story with a grown-up:
what is it made of, what is it for, and what idea or value does it show? Then wonder
how another culture might meet the same need with different materials.
