# Lesson 6 — A Dance from Another Culture

## Summary

Learners learn a simple, openly-taught dance from another culture — **tinikling**, the
national folk dance of the Philippines, in which dancers step in and out between two
tapping bamboo poles, imitating the tikling bird stepping between grass stems. They
practice the foot pattern over marked lines, perform it in time, and describe its
meaning and occasion — honoring it as a living tradition, not a costume.

## Objectives

- Learn and perform a simple dance or movement form from another culture and describe
  its meaning or occasion. _(D07.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Dance is a way a people tells who they are. In the Philippines, dancers step lightly
between two long bamboo poles that clap together in rhythm, the way the quick-footed
**tikling** bird steps between grass stems and rice-plant traps. People dance tinikling
at fiestas, school days, and celebrations — it is joyful, shared, and very much alive.
When you learn another people's dance, you are borrowing a story, so you treat it with
care: learn the real steps, know what it is for, and remember the deepest lesson comes
from the people who dance it every day.

## Materials

- Video or images of a simple dance form (tinikling, or another openly-shared dance)
- Floor marks, tape, or chalk (or two sticks as "poles")

## Preparation

- Choose a short, community-made or community-approved video or image of tinikling, or
  prepare an accurate description. (Alternates: Mexican folklórico zapateado footwork
  or an Irish céilí dance — choose what you can teach accurately and respectfully.)
- Mark two parallel lines on the floor about a shoulder-width apart, or place two
  sticks as "poles."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Teaching another culture's dance is a
responsibility: teach what is accurate, give credit, and be clear that the ideal
teacher is a living dancer from that community — invite one if you can. Use a worked
example for the foot pattern (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): demonstrate
"in, in, out" slowly while counting, then let learners mirror. If using real poles,
clap them very slowly and flat to the ground, and never on anyone's feet — lines of
tape are the safest first step. Do not turn the dance into a costume or a caricature;
the meaning (a festive bird-like stepping dance for celebration) matters more than
perfect steps. A learner who uses a wheelchair or has limited mobility can perform the
upper-body rhythm and arm pattern, or clap the pole rhythm — every body joins the
dance. If a learner cannot step, the rhythm role is a full role, not a lesser one.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have made depth, read rhythm and melody, and listened to
   other cultures' music. Today you *move* — you learn a dance from another culture.
2. **Meet tinikling (10 min).** Watch or read about tinikling. Dancers step in and out
   between two bamboo poles that two clappers tap together in a steady three-beat
   rhythm. The dance imitates the **tikling** bird stepping lightly between grass
   stems. Its occasion is **celebration** — fiestas, gatherings, school days.
3. **Learn the feet (15 min).** Stand beside your two marked lines. Count the beat
   slowly and practice the step: **in, in, out** — step between the lines, step
   between the lines, step outside. Watch a partner or the group first, then try it
   yourself. Keep your feet light and quick, like a bird.
4. **Perform it (10 min).** With a partner or small group, perform the pattern in time
   — one or two people clap the three-beat rhythm while you step. Take turns: dancer,
   clapper, dancer, clapper.
5. **Close (5 min).** Describe it back: what dance did you learn, and what is its
   meaning and occasion? Remember — it belongs to a living people, and learning it is
   a way of saying "I honor your story."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice the step at half speed with a strong verbal count; step over a
  single line first, then two.
- **Support (mobility):** Perform the upper-body rhythm or the pole-clapping rhythm —
  a full role in the dance.
- **Extension:** Add a second movement (an arm wave or a clap on the "out" step) and
  teach the whole pattern to a partner.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner perform a simple movement form in time
  and describe its meaning or occasion?
- **Performance (peer):** A partner checks the step pattern against the beat and names
  the dance's occasion.

## Home connection

At home, show someone the tinikling foot pattern over two lines and tell them what the
dance imitates and when it is danced. Ask if they know a dance from their own culture
and what it means.
