# Lesson 7 — Creating a Character

## Summary

Learners invent an original **character** — not an imitation of a real person or a
stereotype — and bring it to life with three tools: **voice** (pitch, volume, speed,
tone), **body** (posture, gesture, movement), and a simple **prop** or costume piece.
They perform the character and explain what each choice was meant to communicate.

## Objectives

- Create a character using voice, body, and a simple prop or costume, and explain what
  they chose to communicate. _(D07.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

You already "play characters" every day without noticing — you soften your voice for a
baby, straighten up when you are proud, and pick up a certain object to show what you
are doing. An actor makes these choices on purpose. A slow, low voice says something
different from a quick, high one; a hunched body says something different from a tall
one; a scarf can be a cape, a blanket, or a bag. When you choose each part on purpose,
you are **communicating** — sending a message without saying it out loud.

## Materials

- A simple prop or costume piece per learner (scarf, hat, stick, bag, cloth)
- Character planning page (voice, body, prop, "what I want to communicate")

## Preparation

- Gather simple props and costume pieces.
- Copy a character planning page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Creating a character is a **skill**, so
model a worked example first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): become a character
for a few seconds and narrate each choice — "I am an old tree-keeper, so my voice is
slow and creaky, my body is bent, and I hold this stick like a walking staff." Then
invite learners to try. Set a clear boundary: characters must be **invented**, not
imitations of real people, accents, disabilities, or cultural stereotypes — those
imitations can mock or misrepresent, which harms real people. A character can be a
person, animal, object, or idea. After each performance, ask the two questions that
matter: "What were you trying to communicate?" and "What did the audience read from
it?" — the gap between them is the learning. Every body can perform: a wheelchair or
a mobility aid can be part of the character, and a voice or a gesture alone can carry
a whole character.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have made, read, listened, and danced. Today you become
   someone new — a **character** you invent, built from three tools.
2. **Meet the three tools (10 min).** Look at the guide graphic.
   - **Voice:** pitch (high or low), volume (loud or soft), speed (fast or slow), tone
     (warm or sharp).
   - **Body:** posture, gesture, movement.
   - **Prop:** one simple object — a scarf, a stick, a hat — that the character uses.
   Watch the worked example: a bent body, a slow creaky voice, a stick as a staff — an
   old tree-keeper. What did each choice tell you?
3. **Plan your character (10 min).** Choose a character you invent — not a real person
   or a stereotype. On your planning page, write: What will your **voice** do? What
   will your **body** do? What **prop** will you use? And most important: **what do you
   want to communicate** — a feeling, an idea, a moment?
4. **Perform (15 min).** Perform your character for a partner or small group for about
   thirty seconds. Then explain your choices: "I made my voice low to show…, I hunched
   to show…, I held the scarf like…"
5. **Close (5 min).** Ask your audience what they read from your character. Did it
   match what you meant? Every choice is a message — choose on purpose.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a character idea menu (a storm, a shy fox, a proud captain) and
  one tool to focus on at a time.
- **Extension:** Add a second prop or a costume piece, and write one line of dialogue
  for the character to speak.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Build the character entirely with body and prop, and
  point to pictures to explain what each choice communicates.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner use voice, body, and a prop, and
  explain what each choice was meant to communicate?
- **Performance (peer):** The audience reports what they understood, and the learner
  compares it to their intention.

## Home connection

At home, create a character with a family member — one voice choice, one body choice,
and one object — and guess what each other's character was trying to communicate.

## Resources

- No external resources required; the claims in this lesson are about the learner's own
  creative choices and the everyday truth that voice, body, and objects carry meaning.
