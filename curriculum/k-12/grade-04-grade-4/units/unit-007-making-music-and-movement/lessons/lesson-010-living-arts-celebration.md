# Lesson 10 — The Living Arts Celebration

## Summary

Learners bring the whole unit together in a **living-arts celebration**. In four
stations — **see, hear, move, make** — they share their depth artwork, their read-and-
played rhythms and melodies, their dance and character, and their design prototype.
They finish by reflecting on the unit's two big ideas: craft deepens expression, and
the arts of other cultures are living traditions to honor with a respectful question.

## Objectives

- Create an artwork that shows depth or space by overlapping, size, or placement.
  _(D07.S1.04.01)_
- Identify the subject, materials, and likely purpose of an artwork from another
  culture and name one respectful question about it. _(D07.S1.04.02)_
- Read and play or sing a simple melody or rhythm from basic notation. _(D07.S2.04.01)_
- Describe how the music of another culture uses its own instruments, scales, or
  rhythms, and name one respectful question about it. _(D07.S2.04.02)_
- Learn and perform a simple dance or movement form from another culture and describe
  its meaning or occasion. _(D07.S3.04.01)_
- Create a character using voice, body, and a simple prop or costume, and explain what
  they chose to communicate. _(D07.S3.04.02)_
- Design and make something for a specific user and need, and gather feedback to
  improve it. _(D07.S4.04.01)_
- Explain how an object from another culture reflects that culture's materials, needs,
  and ideas. _(D07.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

All around the world, people end their work with a celebration — a harvest feast, a
festival, a market day where everyone shows what they made and taught each other. A
celebration is not the opposite of work; it is the part where work is *shared*, and
sharing is how knowledge travels. Today is that day for you: you show what you made,
you see what others made, and you remember what you learned about honoring the living
arts of every people.

## Materials

- Learners' work from the unit (depth artworks, rhythm and melody cards, dance steps,
  characters, prototypes)
- Display space and labels

## Preparation

- Set up four stations — **see** (visual art), **hear** (music and sound), **move**
  (dance and drama), and **make** (making and design) — with learners' work and simple
  labels.
- Prepare a short reflection prompt: "One thing I made, one thing I learned about
  another culture's art, and one respectful question I still have."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is both a celebration and a
**summative** review, so use it to retrieve the unit's key ideas (retrieval practice):
ask learners to name the three depth tricks, read a rhythm aloud, describe one
culture's music or dance, and recall the design cycle. Keep the tone celebratory and
non-competitive — this is a gallery and a sharing, never a contest or ranking
(`docs/philosophy.md` §8). Each learner shares at least one thing; a learner who is
shy may share through a partner or by placing their work with a written label. Honor
the unit's cross-cultural thread explicitly: invite learners to restate one respectful
question they asked, and to say why learning *from* a living community matters more
than borrowing *about* it. Capture the work in the portfolio — photos, recordings, or
written reflections — so the unit's artifacts endure.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (10 min).** In a circle, look at the celebration graphic —
   four arts around one center. Quickly remember together: What were the three ways to
   show depth? What did "ta" and "ti-ti" mean? What dance did you learn, and what did
   it imitate? What are the five design steps?
2. **Visit the four stations (25 min).** Move through the stations in small groups:
   - **See:** show your depth artwork and point to where you used overlap, size, and
     placement.
   - **Hear:** clap or sing one rhythm or melody you read from notation.
   - **Move:** perform your dance step or your character, and tell its meaning.
   - **Make:** show your prototype and say what your user's feedback changed.
   At each station, share and look at others' work with curiosity and kindness.
3. **Honor the living arts (10 min).** Gather again. Say one thing you learned about
   another culture's art, music, dance, or object — and one respectful question you
   still have. Why is it better to learn *from* a living community than to just borrow
   *about* it?
4. **Reflect and close (15 min).** Write or say your reflection: one thing you made,
   one thing you learned about another culture's art, and one respectful question you
   still have. Place your work in your **portfolio**. Remember: you are a maker now —
   and a guest who honors the makers of the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a reflection sentence frame ("I made…, I learned…, I wonder…")
  and let the learner share at one station rather than all four.
- **Extension:** Write a short "artist's note" for one work explaining the choices you
  made, and present it aloud to the group.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Share work by pointing, performing, or placing it with a
  drawn or written label; a partner reads the label aloud.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The unit's artifacts — depth artwork, notation card, dance
  or character performance, design prototype, and reflective note — gathered in the
  portfolio.
- **Self (reflection):** The learner's written or spoken reflection naming one thing
  made, one thing learned about another culture's art, and one respectful question.

## Home connection

At home, hold a small family gallery: show your favorite work from the unit, perform
one piece, and tell your family one respectful question you learned to ask about
another culture's art.
