# Lesson 4 — Reading and Playing a Melody

## Summary

Building on rhythm notation, learners meet the **staff** — five lines where a note's
height on the page tells you its **pitch**, how high or low it sounds. They read a
simple five-note melody from notation, sing or play it, and trace its rising and
falling shape with their hands, bodies, or instruments.

## Objectives

- Read and sing or play a simple melody from basic notation. _(D07.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

A melody is a rhythm with a shape — it climbs and falls the way a path climbs a hill
and comes back down. Written on five lines, that shape becomes visible: a note placed
high on the page sounds high; a note placed low sounds low. A song you love is a
melody someone wrote down so others could sing it far away. Today you learn to read
that climbing and falling path.

## Materials

- Melody notation guide graphic
- A simple pitched instrument (optional): recorder, xylophone, chime bars, or tuned
  glasses

## Preparation

- Print or display the melody notation guide.
- If using instruments, tune or set them to the melody's notes (C–D–E–F–G or a
  five-note scale).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Reading pitch is a **skill**, so use a
worked example and guided practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): point to each
note, sing its name, and trace the shape with your hand before learners try alone. The
five-note melody in the guide (C–D–E–F–G, or "do–re–mi–fa–sol") rises by steps — a
friendly first melody. Learners may sing note names, solfège, or any neutral syllable.
A learner who is deaf or hard of hearing can *see* the rising shape and match it by
moving a hand or a set of colored bars up and down, or feel a pitched bar's vibration
compared to another. A learner who is blind or has low vision can feel a row of chime
bars or tuned bottles from low to high and play the pattern by touch. A learner who
does not sing may play or trace the melody. Every voice and body reads music.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you read rhythm. Today you read **pitch** — how high
   or low a note sounds — and that turns rhythm into **melody**.
2. **Meet the staff (10 min).** Look at the guide graphic. A **staff** has five lines.
   A note placed **higher** on the staff sounds **higher**; a note placed **lower**
   sounds **lower**. The five notes go: **do re mi fa sol** — or **C D E F G** — each
   one a **step** up from the last.
3. **Read and trace (10 min).** Point to each note left to right and sing or say its
   name. Watch your hand climb as the notes rise: do (low), re, mi, fa, sol (high).
   That rising shape is the melody.
4. **Sing or play it (15 min).** Sing the melody on note names or any syllable — or
   play it on your instrument, chime bar, or tuned glasses. Then try it going back
   down: sol, fa, mi, re, do. Check: does your voice or instrument rise and fall the
   way the notes do?
5. **Close (5 min).** You read a melody from the page. Written notes are a map of
   sound — higher up means higher pitch, lower down means lower.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read just three notes (do–re–mi) first; add notes when confident.
- **Extension:** Read the melody in reverse, or write your own five-note melody on a
  blank staff and teach it to a partner.
- **Support (deaf/hard-of-hearing):** Match a row of bars or bottles from low to high
  and follow the printed shape; feel vibrations to compare high and low.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner read the notes left to right and
  sing or play the rising and falling shape accurately?
- **Performance (peer):** A partner checks that the learner's melody matches the
  printed notes in pitch direction.

## Home connection

At home, find five objects that make different sounds (glasses with water, jars,
bottles), line them up low to high, and "read" a melody you write down as up and down
steps for someone to play.
