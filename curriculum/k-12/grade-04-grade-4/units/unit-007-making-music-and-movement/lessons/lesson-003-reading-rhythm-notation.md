# Lesson 3 — Reading Rhythm Notation

## Summary

Learners meet the written symbols of rhythm: a **quarter note** (one beat, spoken
"ta"), two **eighth notes** (one beat split in two, spoken "ti-ti"), and a **quarter
rest** (one silent beat). They read symbols left to right, play rhythms by clapping or
tapping, and write a short rhythm of their own to perform.

## Objectives

- Read and play a simple rhythm from basic notation. _(D07.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Music is a language people can write down so others can play it — the way a recipe
lets someone cook a dish they have never tasted. On paper, a steady beat becomes
symbols: a filled note for "clap," a joined pair for "quick-quick," a squiggle for
"rest — be silent." A drummer in one town can write a rhythm and a drummer far away
can read it and play the very same thing. Today you learn to read that language.

## Materials

- Rhythm notation guide graphic
- Blank rhythm cards or paper
- Voice and body (clapping, tapping) or sticks/stones

## Preparation

- Print or display the rhythm notation guide.
- Have blank rhythm cards or paper ready for learners to write their own rhythm.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Reading notation is a **skill**, so
teach it with a worked example and guided practice before independent work
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model "ta ta ta ta," then "ti-ti ta rest," clapping
each bar while pointing to the symbols, and have the group echo. Keep the beat steady
by tapping a foot or using a drum. A learner who is deaf or hard of hearing can feel
the beat through a drum on the floor or a partner's tap on their hand, and can show a
rest as "no movement." A learner with limited hand use can nod, blink, or tap a finger.
The goal is that the *symbols* match the *sounds* — correctness, not speed.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You already keep a steady beat. Today you learn to *write* a
   beat so anyone can read it. That writing is called **notation**.
2. **Meet the symbols (10 min).** Look at the guide graphic.
   - A **quarter note** is a filled oval with a stem. It is **one beat** — say "ta."
   - Two **eighth notes** are joined by a beam. Together they are **one beat** split in
     two — say "ti-ti."
   - A **quarter rest** is a squiggly mark. It is **one silent beat** — say nothing,
     or whisper "rest."
3. **Read together (10 min).** Point to each symbol and say or clap it. Start with the
   worked example: four quarter notes — "ta, ta, ta, ta." Then try "ti-ti, ta, rest."
   Keep the beat steady the whole way.
4. **Write your own (15 min).** On your card, write a rhythm four beats long using
   quarter notes, eighth notes, and a rest. Practice it, then perform it for a
   partner. Can your partner read it back exactly as you played it?
5. **Close (5 min).** You just read and wrote music. Symbols carry sound — one drummer
   writes it, another plays it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Begin with only quarter notes and rests; add eighth notes when ready.
- **Extension:** Write two measures, or add a "ti-ka-ti-ka" (four sixteenth notes) to
  the mix and teach it to a partner.
- **Support (deaf/hard-of-hearing):** Feel the beat through a drum or a partner's tap,
  and show a rest as no movement.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Read and write rhythm by touch instead of sight —
  use raised note cards or small objects (a round stone for a quarter note, two small
  linked stones for a pair of eighths, an empty space for a rest) and read back the
  pattern by feeling each shape while a partner says "ta," "ti-ti," or "rest" aloud;
  a learner can also echo a rhythm back by clapping it after hearing it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner read a written rhythm and play it
  steadily, matching the symbols?
- **Performance (peer):** A partner reads back the learner's written rhythm; the
  learner checks it matches what they intended.

## Home connection

At home, clap a four-beat rhythm and write it down in symbols, then teach it to
someone and see if they can read it back to you.

## Resources
