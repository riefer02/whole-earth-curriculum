# Lesson 8 — Design for a User

## Summary

Learners design and make something for a **specific user** and a real **need** — not
for themselves, but for someone they can actually ask. They follow a five-step cycle —
**ask, imagine, make, try with the user, improve** — build a simple **prototype**,
gather the user's **feedback**, and revise it. Along the way they discover that good
design includes everyone, especially people whose needs are often left out.

## Objectives

- Design and make something for a specific user and need, and gather feedback to
  improve it. _(D07.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

Someone you know has a small problem every day — a grandparent whose hands get tired
opening a jar, a younger sibling whose pencil keeps rolling away, a friend who uses a
wheelchair and wishes a table were the right height. A designer does not guess; a
designer **asks**. When you make something for a real person and let them try it, they
tell you what works and what does not — and your idea gets better. This is how the
things you use every day, from a cup to a ramp, were made: someone asked, imagined,
made, tried, and improved.

## Materials

- Building materials (cardboard, paper, tape, string, fabric scraps, recycled
  containers)
- Design cycle graphic

## Preparation

- Print or display the design cycle graphic.
- Gather building materials and arrange a space for making and testing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The design cycle is a **skill**, so
walk through it as a worked example before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006): "My user is a grandparent whose hands tire — I'll make a fatter, softer
handle for a pencil; here is my cardboard prototype; now let's have the grandparent
try it and tell me what to change." Help learners choose a user they can actually
interview (a classmate, sibling, or you). Feedback is the heart: schedule a moment
when each learner asks their user to *try* the prototype and say one thing to keep and
one thing to change. Emphasize **accessibility** and **fairness** — a design that
serves someone often serves many (the "curb-cut effect"), and the best designers ask
who is being left out. Keep the build simple; the thinking matters more than the
finish. A learner with limited fine motor may design by directing a partner, or choose
a need they can prototype with large pieces.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have made art, music, and movement. Today you make something
   that **helps a real person** — you become a **designer**.
2. **Meet the design cycle (10 min).** Look at the graphic. There are five steps:
   - **Ask:** who is my **user**, and what do they need?
   - **Imagine:** think of two or three ideas.
   - **Make:** build a first try — a **prototype**.
   - **Try with the user:** let the user test it and say what works and what does not.
   - **Improve:** change one thing and try again.
3. **Ask (10 min).** Choose your user — someone you can talk to. Ask them: "What is
   something small that is hard for you?" Listen, and write their need in one sentence.
4. **Imagine and make (15 min).** Choose one idea and build a quick prototype from the
   materials. It does not have to be perfect — it has to be testable.
5. **Try with the user and improve (15 min).** Hand your prototype to the user. Ask:
   "Try it. What works? What is hard?" Take their **feedback**, change one thing, and
   let them try once more.
6. **Close (5 min).** You did not guess — you asked. A good design serves a real
   person, and their feedback made it better.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a need bank (pencil that rolls, bag hard to open, cup hard to
  grip) and a two-step version: make, then one improvement.
- **Extension:** Interview two different users and design for both, noting what is the
  same and what must change.
- **Support (fine motor):** Direct a partner who builds the prototype, or prototype
  with large pieces that are easy to handle.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner design for a named user and need,
  build a prototype, and revise it after real feedback?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The prototype (or a photo) plus one sentence recording the
  user's feedback and the change made.

## Home connection

At home, ask someone what small thing is hard for them and make a first try to help,
then let them test it and tell you one thing to improve.
