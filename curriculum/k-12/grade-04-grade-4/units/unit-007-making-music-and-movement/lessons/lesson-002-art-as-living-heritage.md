# Lesson 2 — Looking with Respect: Art as Living Heritage

## Summary

Learners learn that another culture's art is not a "thing from long ago" but a
**living heritage** made by real people today. Using a look-closely frame, they
identify an artwork's **subject** (what it shows), **materials** (what it is made of),
and likely **purpose** (what it is for), then name **one respectful question** — a
question that honors the makers instead of judging them.

## Objectives

- Identify the subject, materials, and likely purpose of an artwork from another
  culture and name one respectful question about it. _(D07.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

When you meet a new person, you do not poke them or say "you look strange" — you say
"tell me about yourself." Art from another culture deserves the same welcome. A woven
cloth, a painted rafter, a carved figure is still being made and used by living people
who love it. Looking with respect means asking what it shows, what it is made of, what
it is for — and then wondering, not judging. In any part of the world, the best way to
meet an artwork is the best way to meet a person: with curiosity and care.

## Materials

- Images or examples of artworks from more than one culture
- Look-closely frame (printed or drawn) with boxes for subject, materials, purpose,
  and one respectful question

## Preparation

- Choose a few respectful, real images of living arts — for example, an Akan
  **adinkra** cloth (stamped cloth from Ghana), Māori **kōwhaiwhai** rafter patterns
  (Aotearoa New Zealand), and an Andean woven textile (Peru/Bolivia). Prefer images
  shared by the communities or cultural institutions, and honor any that are private
  or sacred by not using them at all.
- Copy a look-closely frame for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The heart of it is a *stance*: other
people's arts are living heritage, never curiosities. Model the respectful-question
skill with a worked example — a respectful question asks to understand ("Who makes
this, and what does it mean to them?"), while a judgment is different ("Why do they
make it look like that?"). Hold that distinction gently and often. If you can, invite
a living tradition-bearer from the local community; if you cannot, say plainly that
images are only a doorway and the best learning comes from the people themselves. Some
arts are sacred or private, and the most respectful act is sometimes *not* to look —
teach that boundary as part of respect. Distinguish what we can *observe* (subject,
materials, purpose) from what we must *ask about* (meaning, occasion, belief).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned how artists show depth. Today you learn
   how to look at art from another **culture** with respect. Other people's arts are
   **living heritage** — made and used by real people today, not just long ago.
2. **Meet the look-closely frame (10 min).** Look at the frame graphic. It has four
   parts:
   - **Subject:** what does it show?
   - **Materials:** what is it made of?
   - **Purpose:** what is it for?
   - **One respectful question:** something you genuinely want to understand.
3. **Look at a real artwork (15 min).** With the group or on your own, study one
   artwork — an adinkra cloth, a kōwhaiwhai pattern, or a woven textile. Fill the first
   three boxes from what you can *see*. Then write your one respectful question. Here
   is the difference: "What do the symbols mean?" is a respectful question; "Why does
   it look like that?" can sound like a judgment. Choose the kind that honors the
   makers.
4. **Share questions (10 min).** Read your respectful question to a partner. Listen to
   theirs. Which questions would a living artist be glad to answer?
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: an artwork is a doorway to people. Ask to understand,
   not to judge — and know that some things are private, and respecting that is
   respect too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide word cards (subject, materials, purpose) and sentence starters
  ("I notice…", "I wonder…") to fill the frame.
- **Extension:** Compare two artworks from two cultures and write a respectful question
  for each, noticing what you would ask the makers.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to subject, materials, and purpose, and show a
  "wonder" card for the respectful question.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a subject, material, and purpose,
  and offer a question that seeks to understand rather than judge?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed look-closely frame with the learner's
  respectful question.

## Home connection

At home, find one object or artwork that comes from a culture different from yours and
ask, with a grown-up: what does it show, what is it made of, and what is it for? Then
write one respectful question about it.
