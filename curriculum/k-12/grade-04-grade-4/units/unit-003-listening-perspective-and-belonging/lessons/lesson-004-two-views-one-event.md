# Lesson 4 — Two Views, One Event

## Summary

Learners **compare** their own **point of view** with a classmate's on the **same
event**, finding what is *alike* and what is *different*. They learn that two views can
both be true — because each person sees from their own position, role, and experience.

## Objectives

- Compare their own point of view with a classmate's on the same event. _(D02.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

A rainstorm falls on one street. The child who planned a picnic thinks, "What bad
luck!" The neighbor whose garden was dry thinks, "What a gift!" Same storm, two true
views — one saw what was lost, the other saw what was given. In a fishing village, rain
may mean both a day off the boat and a full water tank. Everywhere, the same event looks
different depending on where you stand. Comparing views does not make one person wrong —
it makes the picture fuller, and it is how people begin to understand one another.

## Materials

- A two-views chart
- Same-event cards
- Paper and pencils (optional)

## Preparation

- Copy a two-views chart for each pair.
- Prepare same-event cards with two clear roles.
- Arrange the room for pair work.

## Facilitator note

This deepens Grade 3's "retell a story from another character's point of view" — here
the learner compares *their own* view with a *real classmate's* on a shared event. The
core idea to protect: **two views can both be true, and "different" does not mean one is
wrong.** Teach the compare sentence as explicit instruction — "We both noticed…, but I
thought… while you thought…, because…" — and model it (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). Watch for the learner who wants to rank the views ("mine is right"); gently
reframe to "both are true from their own position." This is an egalitarian and
epistemological moment: reality is one, but our view of it is partial, and comparing
views is how we get closer to the whole. Honor every way of expressing a view — sign,
drawing, a written line, acting — all count fully. Keep the events light and shared so
no learner's private life is exposed. Read the procedure aloud.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember Lesson 3's clue detectives: you can explain *why*
   someone feels a way. Today you discover something bigger — the *same event* can look
   different to two people, and **both can be right**.
2. **Meet the two-views chart (10 min).** Look at the chart: one **event** in the
   middle, and two boxes — "my view" and "your view" — with a compare row beneath. Watch
   a worked example: the same rainstorm. "My view: my picnic is spoiled." "Your view: my
   garden is watered." Compare: *alike* — we both noticed the rain; *different* — I hoped
   it would stop, you hoped it would fall. Neither view is wrong.
3. **Fill the chart together (10 min).** In pairs, take a same-event card. Each of you
   writes or draws *your own view* of that event in your box — from your own position.
4. **Compare (15 min).** Use the compare sentence: "We both…, but I thought… while you
   thought…, because…". Find at least one thing *alike* and one thing *different*. Switch
   cards and try again.
5. **Close (5 min).** Two views can both be true. Comparing them — not arguing over who
   is right — is how you come to understand another person, and how the picture of the
   world gets fuller.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a fill-in frame ("We both… but I… while you…") and a single clear
  event with two pictures; a non-speaking learner points to picture views.
- **Extension:** Add a third view (a bystander's) and explain how each of the three
  positions shapes what each person sees.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner state their own view and a classmate's
  view of the *same* event, and name at least one thing alike and one thing different?
- **Peer (check):** Did each partner's view get written or drawn as *their* view, and did
  the pair avoid declaring one view "right"?

## Home connection

The next time you and someone at home remember the same day differently, try the compare
sentence: "We both… but I thought… while you thought… because…" — and notice how both
can be true.
