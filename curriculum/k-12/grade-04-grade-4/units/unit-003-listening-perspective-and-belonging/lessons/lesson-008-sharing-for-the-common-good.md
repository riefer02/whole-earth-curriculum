# Lesson 8 — Sharing for the Common Good

## Summary

Learners explain the difference between **sharing for the common good** — so *everyone
has enough* — and **taking more than one's share**, which leaves someone out. They sort
real scenarios and practice dividing resources fairly.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between sharing for the common good and taking more than one's
  share. _(D02.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

A bowl of fruit sits on the table for the whole family. If one person takes the biggest
pile, someone at the far end may get nothing. But if each person takes a fair share, the
bowl serves everyone — and the table becomes a place where nobody is hungry and nobody is
left out. In a village, sharing a catch of fish so every family eats is the common good;
one boat taking it all is more than its share. Everywhere, the same choice appears: take
for *me*, or share for *us*. Today you learn to see the difference — and to choose the
common good.

## Materials

- A fair-share balance poster
- Sharing scenario cards
- A tray of real objects to divide (optional)

## Preparation

- Post the fair-share balance poster.
- Prepare sharing scenario cards.
- Set out a tray of small objects for a real fair-share trial.

## Facilitator note

This is the unit's fairness lesson, and it carries the deepest egalitarian weight: the
difference between **enough for all** and **some left with none**. Teach the two ideas as
explicit, contrasted instruction with the side-by-side poster (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). Guard the moral clarity while keeping it non-shaming: "taking more than one's
share" names a *choice*, not a *bad child* — a learner who has grabbed more is invited to
notice the person left out and re-divide, never to be humiliated. The common good is not
"everyone gets exactly equal piles" — it is *everyone has enough*, which may mean a bigger
person needs more food and a smaller one less, fairly. Let learners discover that
fairness is not always identical shares but *enough for each*. Hold the global lens:
sharing for the common good is how communities everywhere survive — a shared catch, a
shared harvest, a shared well. A learner who struggles with the concept revisits it with
concrete objects; "not yet" is information, never a verdict.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have listened, negotiated, taken a role, and done an act of
   care. Today you learn the big idea underneath all of it: **sharing for the common
   good**.
2. **Meet the two choices (10 min).** Look at the poster. Left: cups handed out so every
   child has one — *the common good: everyone has enough*. Right: one child grabs a big
   pile while another has none — *more than my share: someone is left out*. Say the two
   labels together.
3. **A real fair-share trial (10 min).** With the tray of objects, try it twice. First,
   divide so *everyone has enough* — notice that shares may differ in size but nobody is
   empty. Second, let one person grab more — and *feel* what happens to the person left
   with nothing.
4. **Sort the scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, take the scenario cards — a bowl of
   fruit, a tray of cups, one swing, a box of crayons. Sort each: is this *common good*
   or *more than my share*? Say why, using "everyone has enough" or "someone is left
   out."
5. **Close (5 min).** Sharing for the common good is a choice you can see: *does everyone
   have enough, or did someone take more and leave another with none?* You can choose the
   common good — at a table, on a playground, anywhere people share.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only real objects and the two labels ("everyone has enough" / "someone
   is left out"); a non-speaking learner points to the scene that matches.
- **Extension:** Divide a set of resources where "fair" means *enough for each* rather
  than *equal piles*, and explain why the two differ.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a scenario as common good or more than
  one's share, and give the reason ("everyone has enough" or "someone is left out")?
- **Peer (check):** In the fair-share trial, did the pair notice when someone was left
  out and re-divide so everyone had enough?

## Home connection

At your next shared meal or game, notice: does everyone have enough, or did someone take
more than their share? Try a fair re-divide and see how it changes the table.
