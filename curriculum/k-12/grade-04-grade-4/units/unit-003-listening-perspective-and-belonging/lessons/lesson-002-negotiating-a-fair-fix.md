# Lesson 2 — Negotiating a Fair Fix

## Summary

Learners learn to **negotiate** a **fair solution** to a disagreement with a peer, with
almost no adult help, using five steps: **name the problem, say what each side needs,
think of options, choose one both can live with, check that it feels fair**. The goal is
not that one person wins — it is that both people get something they need.

## Objectives

- Negotiate a fair solution to a disagreement with a peer, with minimal adult help.
  _(D02.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

Two children both want the same book; two neighbors both want to use the one shared
hose; two friends want to play different games. In every one of these, a *fair fix* is
possible — and it almost never means one person gives up and the other takes it all. In
a market stall, two traders who both want the same load of fruit might split it; in a
game, two teams might agree to alternate. Everywhere people live together, they work out
solutions both sides can live with. That is negotiating — and you can do it without an
adult stepping in.

## Materials

- A fair-fix steps chart
- Disagreement scenario cards
- Option stones or slips (optional)

## Preparation

- Post the fair-fix steps chart where everyone can see it.
- Prepare small, friendly disagreement scenario cards.
- Arrange the room for pair work.

## Facilitator note

This builds directly on Grade 3's "I" statements and on Lesson 1's active listening —
so begin by having learners *recall* those, then add the new piece: turning needs into
**options** and choosing one *both* can live with. Teach the five steps as explicit,
worked instruction (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006), and model one full negotiation
aloud. Protect the core idea: **fair does not mean both sides give up everything; it
means both sides get something they need, and nobody is left out.** Watch for the
learner who "wins" by making the other give up — name that as *not yet fair* and invite
another option, never as a verdict on the learner. Honor every way of contributing —
sign, gesture, picture card, written word — all count as a full voice at the table. This
is the egalitarian heart of the unit: negotiation is how power is shared fairly between
people. A learner who cannot yet do all five steps alone may still carry two or three;
that is progress, and the adult (or a peer) supplies only the missing piece.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember Lesson 1: *listen, say it back, check*. And remember
   Grade 3's "I" statements. Today you add the next skill: when you disagree, you can
   **negotiate a fair fix** together.
2. **Meet the five steps (10 min).** Learn them: **one, name the problem** ("We both
   want the same book"); **two, say what you need** ("I need it for my homework"; "I
   need it to finish my story"); **three, think of options** ("share it now, one uses it
   first then the other, ask for a second copy"); **four, choose one both can live
   with**; **five, check it feels fair** ("Do we both feel okay about this?").
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** Two volunteers (or the facilitator and a
   learner) model the five steps with a real scenario. Notice how each side *says their
   need* with an "I" statement, and how the chosen option gives *both* something.
4. **Try it (15 min).** In pairs, draw a scenario card. Work the five steps together —
   no adult needed unless you are truly stuck, and even then, ask only for a nudge, not
   an answer. Try two scenarios if you finish one.
5. **Close (5 min).** A fair fix is not one person winning — it is both people getting
   something they need and leaving as friends. You can do this with almost no adult help.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer the two starter sentences ("I need…", "What if we…") and one
  scenario with only two obvious options; a non-speaking learner signs or points to
  picture options.
- **Extension:** Negotiate a fix where a third friend is involved, or where the "fair"
  choice must also be fair to the whole class.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner state their need with an "I" statement,
  propose at least one option, and help choose a solution both sides can accept?
- **Peer (check):** After the pair chooses, ask each partner: "Does this feel fair to
  you? Why or why not?"

## Home connection

The next time you and a sibling or friend disagree about something shared, try the five
steps before an adult steps in — and tell them what fair fix you found.
