# Lesson 1 — Listen and Say It Back

## Summary

Learners meet **active listening**: not just waiting for a turn to speak, but really
hearing a partner and then **paraphrasing** — saying back in their *own words* — before
they respond. They practice a three-step loop: **listen, say it back, check**.

## Objectives

- Practice active listening by paraphrasing what a partner said before responding.
  _(D02.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of a friend telling you about something that happened to them. If you only wait
for your turn to talk, you miss half of what they said. But if you listen and then say,
"So what you're saying is you were left out of the game" — you show them you heard, and
you give them a chance to fix it if you got it wrong. A fisher checking the net, a cook
tasting the soup, a friend saying a worry back to you — everywhere, people *check* that
they understood. That is active listening, and it is how trust is built between people.

## Materials

- A paraphrase starters chart
- Pair talk cards
- A soft talking object (optional)

## Preparation

- Post the paraphrase starters where everyone can see them.
- Prepare small, friendly pair talk cards (one topic per card).
- Arrange the room or outdoor space for partner work.

## Facilitator note

Active listening is a **skill**, so teach it with explicit instruction and a worked
example before learners try it alone (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The one idea to
protect: *paraphrasing is not repeating word-for-word* — it is saying the idea back in
your own words and then checking. Model the difference clearly, and model the "check"
("Did I get it right?") as a genuine question, not a test. CASEL places listening inside
relationship skills. Honor every way of listening and speaking: a learner who signs,
points to a starter, or writes a paraphrase is doing active listening fully, and their
turn is treated the same as any other. This is an egalitarian moment — the quiet voice
and the loud voice each get a turn, and no one is skipped. Keep topics friendly and
small so no learner's life is put on display. Read the procedure aloud and model each
starter with voice and body.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned to take turns and build on what a
   classmate said. Today you go deeper: it is one thing to wait for your turn — it is
   another to *really hear* the person. What does it feel like when someone truly
   listens to you?
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Learn the three steps of active listening: **one,
   listen** — look at the speaker and keep your own reply waiting; **two, say it back**
   — put their idea into your own words using a starter: "So what I hear you saying
   is…"; **three, check** — ask, "Did I get it right?" Watch a worked example: a
   volunteer shares one sentence; the facilitator says it back *in different words* and
   checks. Notice it is not copying — it is understanding.
3. **Say it back, not copy (10 min).** With a partner, try the difference. Your partner
   says, "I was nervous before the test." First, copy it word-for-word (that is *not*
   paraphrasing). Then say it in your own words: "It sounds like you were worried about
   the test." Check: "Did I get it right?"
4. **Partner loop (15 min).** Use a pair talk card. One of you shares for about half a
   minute. The other **listens, says it back, and checks** — then you switch roles. Take
   turns so each of you paraphrases at least twice.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: active listening is *listen, say it back, check*. It
   tells the speaker, "I hear you," and it gives you a chance to be corrected kindly.
   This is a skill you will use your whole life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer the single starter "So what I hear you saying is…" and a very short
  topic; a non-speaking learner points to the starter and signs or writes their
  paraphrase.
- **Extension:** Paraphrase *and* add one follow-up question ("So you were nervous
  before the test — what helped you feel better?").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner paraphrase the partner's idea in their
  own words and then check, rather than repeating word-for-word or skipping to their own
  reply?
- **Peer (check):** In the loop, did the listener's paraphrase match what the speaker
  meant — and did the speaker get to correct it?

## Home connection

The next time someone at home tells you about their day, listen and say it back in your
own words, then check: "Did I get it right?"
