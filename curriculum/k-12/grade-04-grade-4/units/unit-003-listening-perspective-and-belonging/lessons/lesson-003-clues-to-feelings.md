# Lesson 3 — Clues to Feelings

## Summary

Learners become **clue detectives**. They explain *why* a person might feel the way they
do by gathering three kinds of clues — **what happened**, the **face and body**, and the
**voice and words** — and turning them into a single sentence: "They might feel…,
*because*…".

## Objectives

- Explain why a person might feel the way they do, using clues about what happened.
  _(D02.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

A friend walks in and slams the door. You do not know yet *why* they are upset — but you
can read the clues: the slammed door (what happened), the tight jaw and crossed arms
(face and body), the short, sharp words (voice). Put them together and you can guess: "They
might feel angry *because* something just went wrong." A farmer reads the sky and the
wind to guess the weather; you read faces, bodies, and events to guess feelings. The
clues are never a guarantee — feelings are private — so you always leave room to be
wrong. That is why you check with the person, like you learned in Lesson 1.

## Materials

- A clue detective chart
- Feeling-scenario cards
- Paper and pencils (optional)

## Preparation

- Post the clue detective chart where everyone can see it.
- Prepare feeling-scenario cards with an event and a reaction.
- Arrange the room for pair work.

## Facilitator note

This deepens Grade 3's "guess how a classmate feels by reading face, voice, or body" —
here the new piece is *explaining the why* with a "because" that ties the feeling to
**what happened**. Teach it as explicit, worked instruction: model reading one scenario,
naming each clue kind, then speaking the full "they might feel… because…" sentence
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Guard the honest limit: clues let us *guess*, not
*know* — feelings belong to the person, and our first guess can be wrong. Always pair the
guess with a check ("Did I read that right?"), which loops back to Lesson 1's active
listening and Lesson 2's "check it feels fair." Honor every culture's ways of showing
feeling — some faces are quieter, some voices louder, and no one way is the "right" one.
Read the procedure aloud and model with voice and body.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember Lessons 1 and 2: you listen and say it back, and you
   negotiate fair fixes. Today you add a detective's skill: reading the **clues** that
   tell you *why* someone feels the way they do.
2. **Meet the three clues (10 min).** Learn the clue kinds: **what happened** (the
   event), **face and body** (what you see), and **voice and words** (what you hear).
   Watch a worked example: "The team lost the final." Clues: the score (what happened),
   heads down and slow steps (face and body), quiet mumbling (voice). So: "They might
   feel disappointed *because* the team lost the game they practiced so hard for."
3. **Read clues together (10 min).** With a partner, take a scenario card. Find one clue
   of each kind, then say the sentence together: "They might feel…, *because*…". Take
   turns.
4. **Add the check (15 min).** For each scenario, after you guess, add the honest check:
   "Did I read that right?" — because a guess about feelings is a question, not a fact.
   Try two or three scenarios.
5. **Close (5 min).** You can now explain *why* someone might feel the way they do —
   and you know that your guess is a kind guess to check, never a verdict on them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a three-box frame (what happened / face and body / voice and words)
  and a sentence starter "They might feel… because…"; a non-speaking learner points to
  clue cards.
- **Extension:** Read a scenario where the *same* event makes two people feel different
  things, and explain why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner name a feeling and give a "because" that
  connects it to what happened (the event), not just to a face?
- **Peer (check):** Did the partner's guess use at least one clue of each kind, and did
  they check it rather than state it as fact?

## Home connection

Next time someone at home seems happy or upset, quietly gather the clues — what
happened, their face and body, their voice — and try a kind "You might feel… because…?"
Then check.
