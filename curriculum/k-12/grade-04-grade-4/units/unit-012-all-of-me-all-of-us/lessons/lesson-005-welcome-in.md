# Lesson 5 — Welcome In

## Summary

Everyone is a newcomer sometime — new to a school, a neighborhood, a city, a country.
Learners feel their way into what that is like, name the concrete things communities do
to **welcome** a newcomer (a greeting, a seat saved, a name learned and said correctly, a
tour, an invitation to play), and explain **why belonging matters** — for the newcomer,
and for the community that grows stronger when no one is left at the door. They write a
welcome plan they can actually carry out.

## Objectives

- Describe how communities welcome newcomers and why belonging matters.
  _(D02.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

Remember a time you were new — a first day, a new place, a room where everyone else
already knew the rules and each other. That feeling of standing at the edge is what a
**newcomer** feels. And remember what made it better: someone said your name, saved you
a seat, showed you where to put your things, invited you into the game. A welcome is a
small door someone opens for you — and **belonging** is what it feels like on the other
side. Today you learn to open that door for someone else.

## Materials

- A "welcome plan" page (three welcome actions / one reason belonging matters)
- Writing and drawing supplies

## Preparation

- Prepare the page with two sections: "three ways to welcome a newcomer" and "one
  reason belonging matters."
- Have supplies ready.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its commitment: **belonging is a human
need, and welcoming is a skill everyone can learn and practice.** Handle this gently —
some learners in the room are newcomers right now, or have been, and the topic is not
abstract for them. Never single anyone out or demand they tell their arrival story;
invite, and let a pass be a full answer. Frame "why belonging matters" with real, gentle
evidence rather than slogans: humans are social, and when people are included they feel
safer, learn better, and grow healthier, while exclusion hurts (see the evidence in
`docs/contexts.md` on relationships and belonging; for age-appropriate framing, use the
metaphor of warmth and safety, not clinical terms). Distinguish *welcome as value* (we
ought to include everyone) from *welcome as fact* (communities everywhere practice it —
a greeting, a shared meal, a naming). Keep the global lens: welcome looks different
across cultures (a bow, a handshake, a cup of tea, a song) and all are real welcomes.
For a learner who is blind or has low vision, describe the circle aloud and act the
welcome with voice and touch. For a non-speaking learner, accept drawing, signing,
pointing, or a partner voicing. Close on agency: belonging is something we *build*, not
something we wait for.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Everyone is a newcomer sometime. Think quietly: have you ever
   been new somewhere? You don't have to say it aloud — just hold the feeling.
2. **Feel the edge (10 min).** Form a circle. One volunteer stands just outside it as a
   "newcomer." For a moment, the circle stays closed. What might that feel like? Now —
   open a space, reach out a hand, say "welcome," learn and say their name. What changed?
3. **Name the welcome (10 min).** Together, list concrete things communities do to
   welcome a newcomer: say hello and use their name correctly, save a seat, show them
   around, invite them to join a game or meal, ask what they like, be patient with what
   they don't know yet. Welcomes look different in different places — a bow, a
   handshake, a cup of tea — but all say the same thing: *you belong here.*
4. **Write your welcome plan (10 min).** On your page, write or draw **three ways** you
   could welcome a newcomer in your class or neighborhood. Make them real and doable.
5. **Say why belonging matters (5 min).** Write or tell **one reason** belonging
   matters. Think: how does it feel to be included? What does a community gain when no
   one is left at the door? Belonging is warmth and safety — and a community grows
   stronger when everyone is inside.
6. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your three welcomes and your reason — and listen
   to theirs. Pick one welcome you will *actually do*.
7. **Close (5 min).** A welcome is a door you open for someone else. Add your page to
   your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a menu of welcome actions to choose from; scribe the "reason
  belonging matters" line.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Act out a welcome or point to a picture; a partner voices
  the plan.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Act the welcome with voice and touch; the plan lives
  in words.
- **Extension:** Compare welcome practices across two cultures and explain one more
  reason belonging matters — for learning, safety, or health.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio + observation):** Can the learner describe at least two
  concrete ways a community welcomes a newcomer and give one reason belonging matters —
  by word, drawing, sign, acting, or a partner voicing?

## Home connection

This week, notice one person who might be at the edge — new, quiet, or left out — and do
one thing from your welcome plan. Tell your class what you did.
