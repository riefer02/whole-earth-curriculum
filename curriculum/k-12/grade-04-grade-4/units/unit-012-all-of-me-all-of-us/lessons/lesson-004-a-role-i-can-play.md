# Lesson 4 — A Role I Can Play

## Summary

A community runs on the small roles people take up and carry out. Learners identify a
real role they can play in their class or neighborhood — watering the plants, being a
reading buddy, greeting visitors, helping a neighbor carry things — name what the role
involves, and then **actually do it**. Each role is equally valuable; a job is not more
important because it is louder or more visible. The role card joins the portfolio as a
record of contribution.

## Objectives

- Identify a role they can play in their class or neighborhood community and carry it
  out. _(D02.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of any place where people are together — a classroom, a market, a bus, a
neighborhood street. It works because lots of people quietly do their **roles**: someone
waters the plants, someone greets you at the door, someone keeps the path clear, someone
watches out for the smallest ones. A role is a way of belonging: it says "this place is
mine too, and I help make it work." Today you choose your role and carry it out — not
just talk about it, but do it.

## Materials

- A "role I can play" card (role / what it does / when I'll do it)
- Writing and drawing supplies

## Preparation

- Prepare the role card with three lines: "my role," "what it does," and "when I'll do
  it."
- Have supplies ready, and be ready for learners to actually perform their roles.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It moves the year's care work from
**helping when asked** to **holding a role** — a small, ongoing responsibility. The
central commitment is **egalitarian**: every role is equal in worth. Greeting is not less
than leading; watering a plant is not less than writing a notice. Model this by listing
roles without ranking them, and by noticing the quiet roles too. Choose roles that are
real and free — no role should require money or privilege. Some learners live with
instability or may be new themselves; a role that makes them feel settled (arranging the
reading corner, feeding a class pet) is as valid as a public one. For a learner with a
mobility, sensory, or communication difference, find a role that fits *their* body and
voice — and say so plainly: the point is contribution, not a standard shape of it. Carry
out the role *in this lesson* (or begin it) so "carry it out" is performed, not
promised. For a self-directed learner, the role may be at home or in the neighborhood;
the card records what they actually did. Keep the tone practical and warm: a role is a
way to belong.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** A community is not made by a few big heroes — it is made by
   everyone doing small, real roles. What roles can you already see around you?
2. **See the roles (5 min).** Together, list roles your class or neighborhood needs:
   water helper, reading buddy, greeter, recycler, path keeper, neighbor helper, plant
   checker, quiet signaller. Notice: none is "better" than another. All help.
3. **Choose your role (10 min).** Pick one role that fits *you* — what you can do, where
   you are. On your card, write "my role" and "what it does." Be concrete: a water helper
   checks the plant each day and waters it when the soil feels dry.
4. **Carry it out (15 min).** Do your role now, or do its very first action. Greet
   someone with a warm hello. Water the plant. Sort one bin of recycling. Help a neighbor
   carry something. Feel what it is to *hold* a role, not just wish it.
5. **Check it with a partner (5 min).** Tell a partner what you did, and listen to
   theirs. Ask each other: *is this role helpful to the whole community, or only to me?*
   A good role serves others too.
6. **Commit (5 min).** On your card, write "when I'll do it" — a real time you will do
   this role again this week. That turns a one-time help into a role.
7. **Close (5 min).** You belong here. Your role proves it. Add your card to your
   portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a picture menu of roles to choose from; scribe the "what it does"
  line; pair with a peer to do the role together.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Act out the role or point to it; a partner voices the
  card.
- **Support (mobility/sensory):** Choose a role that fits the learner's own body and
  pace; name plainly that contribution has many shapes.
- **Extension:** Explain *why* your role matters to the whole community — who benefits,
  and what would happen if no one did it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance + observation + peer):** Can the learner name a concrete role,
  describe what it does, and actually carry it out (or its first action) — by doing,
  showing, signing, or a partner voicing?
- **Observation:** Does the learner describe the role as serving the community, not only
  themselves?

## Home connection

Choose one small role at home or in your neighborhood for the week — laying the table,
feeding a pet, greeting a neighbor — and do it every day. Tell your class what you held.
