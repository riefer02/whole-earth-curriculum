# Lesson 2 — My Tradition, My Community

## Summary

Every family and culture keeps things alive by passing them on — a food, a song, a
holiday, a way of greeting, a way of caring for someone. Learners name one tradition
that belongs to their family or culture, describe what it means to them, and then
trace how it connects them to a **larger community** — the many people, near and far,
who share or honor that same thread. The page joins the portfolio.

## Objectives

- Describe a family or cultural tradition and how it connects them to a larger
  community. _(D01.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Think of a special meal, song, holiday, or way of showing respect that happens again
and again in your family or culture. That is a **tradition** — something kept alive by
being **passed on**. When you keep a tradition, you are never doing it alone: you are
touching hands with grandparents, neighbors, and people in other towns or countries who
keep the same thread. A single lantern, drumbeat, or shared plate can connect one home
to a whole world. Today you trace your own thread and see how far it reaches.

## Materials

- A "tradition and its threads" page (a center circle and a larger outer circle)
- Drawing and writing supplies

## Preparation

- Prepare the two-circle page: a small center circle labeled "my tradition" and a
  larger surrounding circle labeled "the larger community," with radiating lines.
- Have drawing and writing supplies ready.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its commitment: **every family shape and
every culture is honored, and no tradition is "better" or "more real" than another.** A
learner's family may be parents, one parent, grandparents, extended kin, guardians,
foster or group care, a chosen circle, or a blended family — and "tradition" may be
anything *kept and passed on*, from a holiday to a weekly meal to a way of remembering
someone who has died. Handle adoption, loss, and mobility gently: a child who has moved
or lost someone may hold a tradition that feels far away or painful — invite, never
demand, and let "the tradition I carry in my heart" be a full answer. Some learners may
have no family tradition they can name; a *cultural* tradition (a language, a greeting,
a food of a place) is equally valid. Model the abstraction aloud: "a tradition connects
me to a larger community when other people, beyond my own home, share or honor it." For
a learner who is blind or has low vision, describe the two circles aloud and trace the
threads in words or on a raised line. For a non-speaking learner, accept a drawing, an
object, signing, or a partner voicing. The goal is connection and belonging, not
disclosure.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Traditions are the things people keep alive by passing them on.
   What do you already remember doing again and again with people you love?
2. **Meet the web (5 min).** Look at your page. The center is **your tradition**; the
   outer ring is **the larger community**. The lines between them are the **threads**
   that connect one home to many people.
3. **Name your tradition (10 min).** In the center, write or draw one tradition that is
   yours — a food, a song, a holiday, a greeting, a way of caring. What does it mean to
   you? Write or tell that, too.
4. **Trace the threads (10 min).** Along the lines to the outer ring, write or draw the
   people who share or honor this tradition beyond your own home — relatives in another
   town, neighbors, a place of worship, people in another country, friends, a shop that
   sells its ingredients. How does it connect you to them?
5. **Share and listen (10 min).** With a partner, tell your tradition and one thread it
   connects you to. Then listen to theirs. Notice: every home has a thread, and none is
   more important than another.
6. **Close (5 min).** A tradition is a gift passed from hand to hand — and it reaches
   farther than you can see. Add your page to your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a menu of traditions (holiday, food, song, greeting, remembrance)
  to choose from; scribe the "what it means" line.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Show a drawing or object and point to its threads; a
  partner voices the connection.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Describe the tradition and its threads in words;
  trace the two circles tactually.
- **Support (experienced loss or mobility):** Honor "the tradition I carry in my
  heart"; a remembered tradition from a former home is a full answer.
- **Extension:** Compare two traditions — yours and a partner's — and name one thing
  they share and one thing that is beautifully different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio + observation):** Can the learner name a family or cultural
  tradition, say what it means, and name at least one person or group beyond their home
  it connects them to — by word, drawing, sign, or a partner voicing?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about the tradition you chose: who taught it to them, and who did
they pass it on to? Bring one new thread back to share.
