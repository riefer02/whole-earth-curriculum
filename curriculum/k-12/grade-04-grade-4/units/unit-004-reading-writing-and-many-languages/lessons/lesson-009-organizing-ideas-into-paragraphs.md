# Lesson 9 — Organizing Ideas into Paragraphs

## Summary

Learners learn the clear structure of a **paragraph**: a **topic sentence** that states
the idea, two or three **details** that support it, and a **closing sentence** that
wraps it up. They practice building a paragraph so a single idea is easy to follow.

## Objectives

- Write a narrative, informative, or opinion piece organized into paragraphs with clear
  ideas. _(D04.S3.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of a paragraph as a small house for one idea. The topic sentence is the front
door — it tells you what is inside. The details are the rooms — they hold the idea up.
The closing sentence is the back step — it lets you leave knowing what you saw. When
each paragraph holds just one idea, in order, your reader walks through your thinking
without getting lost. That is what makes writing clear.

## Materials

- A paragraph model
- A paragraph-building chart

## Preparation

- Choose or write a clear model paragraph.
- Prepare the paragraph-building chart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Paragraph structure is a foundational writing skill, so use
explicit instruction and a worked example: name the three parts, show a model, then
build one together before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the
three jobs clear — topic sentence (states the idea), details (support it), closing
sentence (wraps it). This structure transfers across languages and writing systems,
though the conventions may differ; honor the learner's home writing system. The goal is
clarity for a reader, not a rigid formula — a learner who can explain why each part is
there has the skill. Celebrate every attempt; writing is thinking made visible.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one thing you could explain to a friend in three
   sentences. That is a paragraph waiting to be built.
2. **Meet the three parts (5 min).** Learn a paragraph's three jobs: the **topic
   sentence** (states the idea), the **details** (support it), and the **closing
   sentence** (wraps it up).
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** Read a model paragraph. Name its topic sentence,
   its details, and its closing sentence, and see how each does its job in order.
4. **Build a paragraph (15 min).** Choose an idea and write a paragraph with all three
   parts. Read it back and check: is the idea clear? do the details support it? does the
   closing wrap it?
5. **Share (5 min).** Read your paragraph to a partner and ask: can you find my topic
   sentence, my details, and my closing sentence?
6. **Close (5 min).** A clear paragraph is a clear idea, in order. Build it well, and
   your reader will follow.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Order a prepared set of sentence strips into a paragraph, then name each
  part.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Build a paragraph by arranging picture-plus-sentence cards
  in order, or sign the parts.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Compose the paragraph orally or in braille or large
  print, naming the three parts as you go.
- **Extension:** Write two paragraphs on one topic, each with its own topic sentence and
  details, and explain where one idea ends and the next begins.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner write a paragraph with a topic sentence,
  supporting details, and a closing sentence, so one idea is clear?

## Home connection

At home, tell someone one thing in a "mini-paragraph": a topic sentence, two details,
and a closing sentence — and see if they can name the three parts.
