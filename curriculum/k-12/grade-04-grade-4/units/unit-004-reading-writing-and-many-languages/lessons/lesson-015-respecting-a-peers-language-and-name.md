# Lesson 15 — Respecting a Peer's Language and Name

## Summary

Learners show **respect** for a peer's language by learning and correctly **pronouncing**
their **name** and a few words. They ask "how do you say it?", practice until it is
right, and check kindly — treating every name and every language as worthy of care. This
lesson closes the unit by bringing its whole journey together.

## Objectives

- Show respect for a peer's language by learning and correctly pronouncing their name and
  a few words. _(D04.S4.04.02)_

## Connection

A person's name is one of the first gifts they are given — it is tied to who they are,
their family, and their story. Saying a name wrong, or not trying to say it right, can
feel like saying "you are not worth the effort." Saying it right, and learning even two
words of a person's language, says "I see you, and I honor where you come from." Respect
begins with a name.

## Materials

- A "my name" card
- A "words to learn" sheet

## Preparation

- Prepare the name cards and words-to-learn sheet.
- Think about how to model asking "how do you say your name?" with warmth and patience.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. This is the unit's capstone of respect: model asking "how do you
say your name?" and echo it carefully, and let a learner correct you until you get it
right — the facilitator is the first learner here. Names carry identity; mispronouncing a
name repeatedly can feel like erasure, and the research on name pronunciation and
belonging supports this (Kohli & Solórzano, 2012, on "racial microaggressions" including
name mispronunciation). Hold this gently: no one should be forced to teach their language
or be made into a display; a peer offers their name and words as a gift, and the gift can
be declined. Practice is cooperative, not graded — "did I say it right?" is an invitation
to be corrected, never a test with a score. Honor signed languages exactly as high as
spoken ones — a signed name is a name. Treat accents as natural variation, never as
error. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing learns names and words in sign or through
written or visual forms. The measure is not perfection but care — a learner who keeps
trying until it is right has shown the respect that matters.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think about your own name. How do you feel when someone says it
   right? How do you feel when they do not try?
2. **Meet the question (5 min).** Learn the respectful question: **"How do you say your
   name?"** — and the two-word gift: learn a peer's **greeting** and a **courtesy word**
   (such as "thank you") in their language.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator asks a volunteer "how do you say
   your name?", listens, echoes it, and asks "did I say it right?" — repeating until
   correct. Watch how asking and echoing shows care.
4. **Learn a name and words (15 min).** Pair up. Ask your partner how to say their name,
   and practice it until it is right, asking "did I say it right?" as you go. Then learn
   their greeting and a courtesy word. Switch roles.
5. **Share the circle (5 min).** In the group, each person shares the name and one word
   they learned, saying it with care, and the person whose language it is confirms.
6. **Close (5 min).** Every name and every language is a door into a person's world.
   Learning them is respect in action. You have done that today.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Learn just the name first, then one word, checking "did I say it right?"
  after each.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Learn the name and words in sign or written/visual form, and
  confirm understanding with a nod, a sign, or a written note.
- **Support (Deaf/hard of hearing or speech difference):** Learn and produce names and
  words through the learner's preferred mode — sign, written form, or a communication
  device — and practice at a comfortable pace.
- **Extension:** Learn a short phrase beyond a single word — "my name is…" or "please" —
  in a peer's language, and use it correctly later in the week.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Can the learner correctly pronounce (or produce, in
  the preferred mode) a peer's name and at least two words, checking and adjusting until
  the peer confirms it is right?

## Home connection

At home, ask everyone to teach you how to say their name just as they like it, and learn
one new word in a language spoken by someone you know.
