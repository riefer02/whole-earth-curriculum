# Lesson 12 — Revising for Clear Ideas

## Summary

Learners **revise** their draft to make ideas clearer, using three moves — **add** what
is missing, **remove** what is extra, and **reorder** what is out of place — guided by a
checklist and a partner's kind feedback. Revising is about *ideas* (not spelling, which
comes next).

## Objectives

- Use a simple writing process — plan, draft, revise, edit, and publish — on their own.
  _(D04.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

Revision is where writing gets good. A first draft is like a garden just planted — the
plants are there, but some need thinning, some need moving into the sun, and some need a
little more soil. Revising is tending: you add what helps the idea grow, remove what
crowds it, and move what is in the wrong place. Every great writer revisits and reworks.

## Materials

- The learner's draft
- A revision checklist

## Preparation

- Have each learner's draft ready.
- Prepare the revision checklist.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Revising is a taught step: model it on a shared paragraph with a
worked example — add, remove, reorder — thinking aloud about *why* each change clarifies
the idea (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the distinction sharp for learners:
**revising** works on ideas; **editing** (the next lesson) works on spelling and
punctuation. Frame peer feedback as help, not judgment — one "this is clear" and one
"this could be clearer" — so the writer stays in charge of their own piece (a cooperative,
not competitive, act; see `docs/facilitation.md`). Honor revisions in the learner's home
language and writing system, and honor revising by dictation, braille, or assistive
technology. Mastery means the learner can name the change they made and why it helps.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Read your draft as a reader, not a writer. Where might a reader
   stumble or wonder? Those are the places to revise.
2. **Meet the three moves (5 min).** Learn them: **add** what is missing, **remove** what
   is extra, **reorder** what is out of place. Revising is about ideas, not spelling.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator revises a shared paragraph,
   thinking aloud: "This idea is unclear — I will add a detail… this part is extra — I
   will remove it… this sentence belongs earlier — I will move it." Watch the idea grow
   clearer.
4. **Revise your draft (20 min).** Use the checklist. Read your draft and ask: is each
   idea clear? Mark places to add, remove, or reorder, then make the changes.
5. **Partner check (5 min).** Trade drafts. Tell your partner one place that is clear and
   one place that could be clearer. Then decide which suggestion to keep — it is your
   piece.
6. **Close (5 min).** Revision is care for the reader. Your ideas are clearer now — next,
   you will polish the words.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Revise with one move at a time, focusing on "add" first, using a prepared
  list of possible details.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Revise by dictation, sign, or assistive technology, naming
  each move.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Revise from a draft in braille or large print or by
  dictation, with the checklist read aloud.
- **Extension:** Revise for word choice too — replace two ordinary words with more exact
  ones — and explain how each change sharpens the idea.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Can the learner make and explain revisions (add,
  remove, reorder) that make their ideas clearer?
- **Portfolio:** Keep the marked-up draft as the third artifact of the writing process.

## Home connection

At home, read your revised draft to someone and ask them to name one part that is clear
and one part they would make clearer — then decide what you will change.
