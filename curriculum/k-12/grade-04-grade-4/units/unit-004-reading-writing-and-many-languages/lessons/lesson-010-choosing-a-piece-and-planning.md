# Lesson 10 — Choosing a Piece and Planning

## Summary

Learners choose the kind of piece they will write — **narrative** (a story),
**informative** (facts), or **opinion** (what I think and why) — and **plan** it with a
graphic organizer before drafting. This is the first step of the writing process, and
the plan they make will carry through the lessons that follow.

## Objectives

- Write a narrative, informative, or opinion piece organized into paragraphs with clear
  ideas. _(D04.S3.04.01)_
- Use a simple writing process — plan, draft, revise, edit, and publish — on their own.
  _(D04.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

Builders do not stack bricks and hope. They draw a plan first. Writers are builders of
ideas, and planning is the drawing. Before you write a story, a report, or your opinion,
you choose what kind of piece it is and gather your ideas around one clear center. A few
minutes of planning now saves confusion later — and makes the drafting that follows flow.

## Materials

- A planning web or outline sheet
- A genre menu card (narrative, informative, opinion)

## Preparation

- Prepare the planning organizers and genre menu card.
- Think of example topics for each kind to share.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Planning is a taught skill, so model it with a worked example:
choose a kind and topic aloud, fill a planning web, and think aloud about which ideas
belong (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Teach the three kinds plainly — narrative
tells a story, informative gives facts, opinion says what you think and why — and let the
learner choose the one that fits their purpose. The plan belongs to the learner and will
grow across the coming lessons into a published piece, so keep it as a portfolio
artifact. Honor plans in any form — web, outline, list, drawing — and in the learner's
home language. Planning is thinking made visible; there is no single "correct" plan.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of something you could write about. Is it a story, a set of
   facts, or your opinion? That choice comes first.
2. **Meet the three kinds (5 min).** Learn them: a **narrative** tells a story; an
   **informative** piece gives facts; an **opinion** says what you think and why. Choose
   the one that fits your idea.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator chooses a kind and a topic, then
   builds a planning web, thinking aloud: "This idea belongs in the center; these details
   branch from it; that one does not fit." Watch the plan take shape.
4. **Plan your piece (15 min).** Choose your kind and topic. Fill your planner: the
   topic in the center, your ideas and details branching out. Check that every branch
   supports the center.
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your kind, your topic, and two planned ideas. Does
   the plan make sense to them?
6. **Close (5 min).** A clear plan is a clear path. Keep your plan — you will draft it in
   the next lesson.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Choose from two prepared topics and use a simple three-box planner (idea,
   detail, detail).
- **Support (non-speaking):** Plan with drawings or picture cards around a center idea.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Plan orally — name the kind, topic, and details — and
  record them by dictation, braille, or large print.
- **Extension:** Plan two pieces of different kinds on the same topic (a story and a
  report) and explain how the plan differs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner choose a kind (narrative, informative, or
  opinion) and produce a plan with a clear topic and supporting ideas?
- **Portfolio:** Keep the plan as the first artifact of the writing process.

## Home connection

At home, plan what you would write about your day — choose the kind (story, facts, or
opinion) and list your main idea and two details, then show someone your plan.
