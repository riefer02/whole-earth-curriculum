# Lesson 4 — Using Context Clues

## Summary

Learners learn to use **context clues** — the words and ideas *around* an unfamiliar
word — to figure out what it means. They hunt for four kinds of clues: a **definition**
right in the sentence, a **synonym**, an **example**, or a **contrast**. They guess,
then check.

## Objectives

- Use context clues and word parts, such as roots, prefixes, and suffixes, to figure
  out unfamiliar words. _(D04.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever met a word you did not know — on a sign, in a message, in a story — and
figured it out anyway? You already used context clues. The words around an unfamiliar
word are like the people around a stranger at a gathering: they tell you who the
stranger is. Readers do not stop at every unknown word; they read around it, gather
clues, and guess — then keep reading to check.

## Materials

- A short passage with one or two unfamiliar words
- A "clue hunt" highlighter or underline strip

## Preparation

- Choose a short sentence or paragraph where surrounding words clearly reveal the
  unfamiliar word's meaning.
- Prepare the highlighter or underline strip.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Using context clues is a taught skill, so model it with a worked
example: read a clue-rich sentence, circle the unfamiliar word, underline the clue
words, and think aloud about the guess (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Teach the
four clue kinds explicitly — definition ("a zephyr, or a soft wind"), synonym ("a zephyr,
a gentle breeze"), example ("zephyrs, such as the morning breeze"), and contrast ("not a
gale, but a gentle zephyr"). The point is a reasonable, checked guess, not a perfect
dictionary answer — meaning-making over memorization. This works in any language and
writing system: the clues are in the surrounding ideas, whatever the script. Where a
dictionary or digital tool is used, treat it as a *check* after guessing, not a
replacement for thinking — and notice that a tool can define a word but cannot gather
clues for you. Honor guesses made in the learner's own language.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you met an unknown word and guessed its meaning
   from the words around it. How did you know?
2. **Meet the clue kinds (5 min).** Learn the four context clues: a **definition**
   nearby, a **synonym**, an **example**, or a **contrast**. Each one whispers the
   unfamiliar word's meaning.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads a sentence, circles the
   unfamiliar word, and hunts the clue words, thinking aloud: "What kind of clue is
   this? What do I guess the word means?" Watch the guess form, then check it.
4. **Hunt clues yourself (15 min).** Read your passage. Circle the unfamiliar word,
   underline the clue words, and name the clue kind. Guess the meaning in your own
   words, then check by re-reading.
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your word, your clue words, and your guess. Did
   the clues point the same way?
6. **Close (5 min).** Readers are detectives. The words around an unknown word are
   clues — gather them, guess, and check.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give two possible meanings for the unfamiliar word; the learner uses the
  clues to choose.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the clue words and the meaning in a picture or
  sign.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Hear the sentence read aloud, or read it in braille or
  large print, then name the clue words and guess orally.
- **Extension:** Find an unfamiliar word in your own reading, identify the clue kind,
  guess its meaning, and check it in a dictionary.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify the clue words around an
  unfamiliar word and use them to make and check a reasonable guess at its meaning?

## Home connection

Tonight, find one unfamiliar word in something you read or hear, and try to figure it
out from the words around it before asking anyone or checking.
