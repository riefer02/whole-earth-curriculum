# Lesson 11 — Drafting into Paragraphs

## Summary

Learners turn their **plan** into a **draft**, writing one **paragraph** per idea. The
draft is a "sloppy copy" — the goal is to get the ideas down clearly and in order, with
spelling and polish saved for revising and editing.

## Objectives

- Use a simple writing process — plan, draft, revise, edit, and publish — on their own.
  _(D04.S3.04.02)_

## Connection

A draft is a first try, not a final product. Just as a builder first raises the frame of
a house and hangs the doors later, a writer first gets the ideas standing in order and
polishes afterward. Giving yourself permission to draft freely — mistakes and all — is
what lets the ideas come out. You can always fix it later; first, get it down.

## Materials

- The plan from the previous lesson
- Draft paper

## Preparation

- Have each learner's plan ready.
- Provide draft paper or a notebook.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Drafting is a taught step in the writing process: model turning
one plan branch into a paragraph, thinking aloud about how one idea becomes one
paragraph (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Emphasize that the draft is a "sloppy
copy" — do not correct spelling now; that belongs to editing. This keeps the focus on
ideas and protects fluency. One idea per paragraph is the organizing rule. Honor drafts
in the learner's home language and writing system, and honor drafting by dictation,
braille, or assistive technology. Every draft is a step, never a verdict — the goal is
ideas on the page, not perfection.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at your plan. Today you will grow it into a draft. A draft
   is a first try — free and full of ideas, not yet polished.
2. **Meet the drafting rule (5 min).** Learn it: **one paragraph per idea**. Each branch
   of your plan becomes a paragraph, in order, so a reader can follow your thinking.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator takes one branch of a plan and
   writes a paragraph from it, thinking aloud: "This idea becomes my topic sentence;
   these details support it; this is my closing." Watch the plan become prose.
4. **Draft your piece (20 min).** Write your draft from your plan, one paragraph per
   idea. Do not stop to fix spelling — just get the ideas down, clearly and in order.
5. **Read it back (5 min).** Read your draft aloud (or sign it) to yourself or a
   partner. Does it follow your plan? Are the ideas in order?
6. **Close (5 min).** A draft is a doorway, not a finished room. Keep it — next you will
   revise it to make your ideas even clearer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Draft one paragraph at a time, pausing after each to check it matches the
  plan.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Draft by dictation, sign, or assistive technology, one
  paragraph per idea.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Draft in braille or large print, or by dictation, from
  the plan held in memory or a raised organizer.
- **Extension:** Draft a longer piece with a clear paragraph for each planned idea and a
  one-sentence lead-in that tells the reader what is coming.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner produce a draft that follows their plan,
  organized into paragraphs with one idea each?
- **Portfolio:** Keep the draft as the second artifact of the writing process.

## Home connection

At home, take your plan and read your draft aloud to someone, then ask: "Can you follow
my ideas from beginning to end?"
