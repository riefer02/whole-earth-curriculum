# Lesson 2 — Key Details of a Read-Aloud

## Summary

Learners listen to a **read-aloud** and practice choosing the **key details** — the
few facts, steps, or events that are needed to understand the whole — while setting
aside the **extra** details. They close by retelling the passage with the main idea and
its key details.

## Objectives

- Summarize the main idea and key details of an oral presentation or read-aloud.
  _(D04.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

When someone reads a story or a report aloud, not every word is equally important.
Some details are the load-bearing walls of the meaning; others are just decoration. If
you tell a friend about a trip, you keep the storm that changed the plan — and leave
out the color of every single shirt. Choosing key details is how listening becomes
understanding.

## Materials

- A read-aloud passage (short, grade-level, with clear key details)
- A "key details" note sheet

## Preparation

- Choose a short passage with a few clear, important details.
- Prepare the note sheet.
- Plan to read the passage at a clear, unhurried pace.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Picking key details is a taught skill: model it with a worked
example — read a short passage, then think aloud about which details are *needed* to
understand it and which are extra (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The test question
is always "if I took this detail away, would the meaning change?" — not "is this detail
interesting?" Accept retells in the learner's own words and language, and honor a
faithful retell even when the wording differs. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing
retells from a signed read-aloud or written text; a learner who is blind or has low
vision retells from what they heard. Reading aloud well — with clear pace and volume —
is itself a model of the speaking skills this unit builds.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a story you love. If you had to tell it in one minute,
   which parts would you keep? Those are its key details.
2. **Meet the question (5 min).** Learn the listener's question for every read-aloud:
   **which details are needed to understand the whole, and which are extra?** Key
   details matter; extras can be set aside.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads a short passage, then
   thinks aloud: "This detail — is it needed? Yes, because… That detail — is it needed?
   No, it is extra." Watch how they keep the few and set aside the rest.
4. **Listen and choose (15 min).** Listen to the read-aloud. Jot or sketch the details
   you hear, then decide which are **key** and which are **extra**. Keep the key ones.
5. **Retell (5 min).** Retell the passage to a partner: the main idea plus the key
   details, in your own words. Did your partner leave out the extras?
6. **Close (5 min).** A good listener is not a tape recorder. They choose what matters
   and carry that forward.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a very short passage and give a prepared list of details; the learner
  circles the key ones.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to pictures or sign the key details in order.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Note key details by memory or braille as the passage is
  read, then retell orally.
- **Extension:** After retelling, explain *why* each kept detail was key and why one
  extra was set aside.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner retell a read-aloud with the main idea
  and its key details, leaving out extras, in their own words and language?

## Home connection

At home, have someone read you a short piece from a book or a message. Retell it with
only the key details, and ask whether you kept what mattered.
