# Lesson 3 — Presenting with Clear Volume and Pace

## Summary

Learners prepare a short talk and practice the two things that make a speaker easy to
hear and follow: **volume** (loud enough for the whole room, but not shouting) and
**pace** (not too fast, not too slow, with small **pauses**). They give and receive
simple listener feedback and adjust.

## Objectives

- Present a short talk to a group, speaking clearly with appropriate volume and pace.
  _(D04.S1.04.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever missed what someone said because they whispered, or lost the thread
because they raced through it? Speaking is not only about words — it is about being
*heard* and *followed*. Whether you are telling a story, explaining a rule, or asking
for help, your voice can build a bridge to the listener or let the meaning fall into a
gap. Volume and pace are two of the tools that build that bridge.

## Materials

- A short prepared talk (two to three sentences)
- A "listener check" card ("I could hear you" / "I could follow you")

## Preparation

- Have each learner think of a short talk they can give (a favorite animal, a game, a
  how-to).
- Prepare the listener check card.
- Arrange the room so a speaker can be seen and heard from the back.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Presenting is a skill, so model it: give a short talk yourself at
the right volume and pace, then once too quietly and too fast, and let learners name the
difference (worked example before practice; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Frame
feedback as help, not judgment — "I could hear you" and "I could follow you" are
observations, not scores. Honor many ways of presenting: a learner who is Deaf or hard
of hearing presents in sign or with a communication device, and clarity of signing pace
and size is just as real as spoken volume; a learner who stutters or speaks with a
speech difference presents at their own pace, and the room listens with patience. The
measure of a clear talk is whether the audience understands, never whether a voice
sounds "standard." Keep the atmosphere safe and encouraging — speaking in front of a
group is brave, and every attempt is a step.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you could not hear or follow a speaker. What
   would have helped? That is what you will practice today.
2. **Meet the two tools (5 min).** Learn the two checks for a clear talk: **volume**
   (loud enough for the farthest listener, not shouting) and **pace** (not too fast, not
   too slow, with small pauses between ideas).
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator gives a short talk three ways:
   too quiet, too fast, then just right. After each, ask: could you hear it? could you
   follow it? Notice what changed.
4. **Prepare your talk (5 min).** Choose your two or three sentences and say them to
   yourself once, slowly.
5. **Present and check (15 min).** Present your talk to a partner or group. Listeners
   use the card: "I could hear you" and "I could follow you," plus one helpful
   suggestion. Try it once more, adjusting volume or pace.
6. **Close (5 min).** A clear voice is a gift to your listeners. Volume and pace are
   tools you already own — use them so your meaning arrives.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Present one sentence at a time, pausing between; a partner gives one
  specific, kind suggestion at a time.
- **Support (anxiety):** Present to one trusted partner first, then a small group, before
  the whole group.
- **Support (Deaf/hard of hearing or speech difference):** Present in sign or with a
  device; clarity of pace and sign size is the "volume and pace" of that mode.
- **Extension:** Present a longer talk, varying pace on purpose — slow for an important
  idea, quick for an exciting one — and explain the choice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner present a short talk that the
  audience reports hearing clearly and following at a comfortable pace?

## Home connection

At home, tell someone one thing you learned today, checking your volume and pace, and
ask: "Could you hear me and follow me?"
