# Lesson 1 — Summarizing an Oral Presentation

## Summary

Learners listen to a short **oral presentation** and learn to **summarize** it: state
the **main idea** in one sentence, then name the **key details** that matter. A
summary is shorter than the original, in the listener's own words, and keeps only what
is most important.

## Objectives

- Summarize the main idea and key details of an oral presentation or read-aloud.
  _(D04.S1.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of someone explaining a new game, giving directions to a place, or telling you
what happened at the market or in the field. You do not walk away holding every single
word — you keep the big point and the few details that matter. That is summarizing. It
is how you carry a speaker's meaning with you, in your own words, long after they
finish.

## Materials

- A short oral presentation (live or recorded, one to two minutes)
- A summary frame card ("The main idea is…" / "Key details: 1, 2, 3")

## Preparation

- Choose or record a short, clear presentation about one thing.
- Prepare the summary frame card.
- Arrange a listening circle where everyone can hear the speaker.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Summarizing is a taught skill: model it first with a worked
example (a short talk you give, then summarize aloud), then guide learners to try it
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the parts clear — the **main idea** is the one
big thing the talk is about; the **key details** are the few facts or steps that hold it
up. Accept a summary in the learner's own words and own language; there is no single
"correct" wording. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing summarizes a signed
presentation; a learner who is blind or has low vision summarizes what they hear. This
is sense-making, not a memory test — honor a faithful summary even when it is worded
differently. If a recording or translation tool is used, notice what it adds (many
voices) and what it cannot carry (the living person); people remain the real source.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember the last time someone explained something to you. What
   did you keep from it? That is a summary already.
2. **Meet the summary (5 min).** Learn what a summary is: the **main idea** in one
   sentence, plus the **key details** — the few things that matter most. It is shorter
   than the original, and it is in your own words.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator gives a short talk, then thinks
   aloud: "Mostly, this talk was about…" (the main idea), "and the key details were…"
   (the important parts, not every word). Watch how one big idea is held up by a few
   details.
4. **Listen and summarize (15 min).** Listen to a short presentation. Ask yourself: what
   was it *mostly* about? What few details really mattered? Say or write the main idea
   in one sentence, then list three key details.
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade summaries. Did your partner catch the main
   idea? Did they keep the key details and leave out the small extras?
6. **Close (5 min).** A summary is the gift of understanding: you keep the big idea and
   the details that matter, and you can pass them on in your own words.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a very short, single-topic talk and offer a choice of two possible
  main ideas; the learner picks the one the talk was mostly about.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Summarize by pointing to pictures, signing, or writing.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Summarize from a talk heard aloud, stating the main
  idea and details orally or in braille or large print.
- **Extension:** Summarize a longer presentation in two sentences and note which
  details the speaker themselves emphasized most.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the main idea of a heard
  presentation in one sentence and name the key details, in their own words and
  language?

## Home connection

Tonight, ask someone at home to tell you how their day went or how to do a task, then
say it back: "So mostly, it was about…, and the important parts were…" Ask if you got
the summary right.
