# Lesson 8 — Compare and Contrast Across Two Texts

## Summary

Learners hold **two texts** side by side and compare and contrast their **themes**,
**characters**, or **ideas** — finding what is alike and what differs, and supporting
each finding with **evidence** from the texts. They work with paired stories or
informational texts.

## Objectives

- Compare and contrast themes, characters, or ideas within and across two texts.
  _(D04.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Two storytellers can tell about the same thing — kindness, courage, water, the moon —
and each tells it their own way. Reading two texts about a related idea is like hearing
two voices on one subject: you notice where they agree and where they go their own way.
That is how a reader gathers more than any single book can give, and how the same idea
grows richer in many tellings.

## Materials

- Two short texts on a related theme
- A two-text compare chart

## Preparation

- Choose two short texts that can be compared (two folktales about kindness, two
  informational texts about the same animal).
- Prepare the chart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Comparing across texts is a taught skill: model it with a worked
example — read two short passages, then think aloud as you sort "same" and "different"
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Anchor every claim in evidence: "I say their themes
are alike because in this text… and in that text…" The goal is supported thinking, not a
single right answer. This is a global moment too: paired texts can come from two
cultures or two languages, and comparing them is how a reader hears more than one voice.
Honor comparisons in the learner's own language and honor differing interpretations that
are faithful to the texts. A learner who is blind or has low vision compares from texts
heard aloud or read in braille or large print.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of two stories you know that share an idea — courage, or
   kindness. What do they do alike, and what do they do differently?
2. **Meet the two-text comparison (5 min).** Learn to hold two texts side by side:
   **compare** their themes, characters, or ideas (how alike?) and **contrast** them (how
   different?), always pointing to the texts.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads two short passages, then
   thinks aloud: "Both are about… (alike), but in the first… while in the second…
   (different)." Watch each claim carry its evidence.
4. **Compare across texts yourself (20 min).** Read your two texts. Fill the chart:
   what is the same, and what is different, about their themes, characters, or ideas?
   Write one sentence for a similarity and one for a difference, each with evidence.
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner your similarity and difference, and your evidence.
   Did their evidence come from both texts?
6. **Close (5 min).** Two voices, one idea, many tellings. Reading across texts is
   hearing the whole conversation.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read two very short texts and give a prepared list of points; the learner
  sorts each as "same" or "different."
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort picture cards or sign the similarities and
  differences.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Compare from texts heard aloud, stating similarities
  and differences orally or in braille or large print.
- **Extension:** Compare three texts on one theme and write a short paragraph explaining
  the idea the three share and how each differs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a similarity and a difference between
  two texts and support each with evidence from the texts?

## Home connection

At home, read or hear two things on the same subject — two songs, two stories, two news
items — and name one thing they share and one thing that differs.
