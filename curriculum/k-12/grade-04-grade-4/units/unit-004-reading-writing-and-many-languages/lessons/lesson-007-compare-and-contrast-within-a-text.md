# Lesson 7 — Compare and Contrast Within a Text

## Summary

Learners learn to **compare** (find what is the **same**) and **contrast** (find what is
**different**) between two characters, events, or ideas *within one text*. They use a
simple chart or Venn diagram to sort their thinking.

## Objectives

- Compare and contrast themes, characters, or ideas within and across two texts.
  _(D04.S2.04.02)_

## Connection

Think of two friends. They are alike in some ways and different in others, and noticing
both is how you understand them — and how you understand a story. When two characters
meet the same problem, or two events echo each other, asking "how are they the same, and
how are they different?" opens the story's meaning. Comparing is a reader's way of
seeing clearly.

## Materials

- A short text with two comparable things
- A compare-and-contrast chart ("same" / "different", or a Venn diagram)

## Preparation

- Choose a short text where two characters, events, or ideas can be compared.
- Prepare the chart or Venn diagram.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Comparing and contrasting is a taught skill: model it with a
worked example — read a passage, then think aloud as you sort "same" and "different"
details (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the two moves distinct: **compare** =
how they are alike; **contrast** = how they differ. Anchor every claim in the text: "I
say they are alike because…" The goal is supported thinking, not right answers. This
works in any language and writing system — the comparison is about meaning, not script.
Honor comparisons made in the learner's own language, and invite comparisons that are
faithful to the text even when worded differently. A learner who is blind or has low
vision compares from a text heard aloud or read in braille or large print.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of two people you know. Name one way they are the same and
   one way they are different. That is comparing and contrasting.
2. **Meet the two moves (5 min).** Learn them: **compare** = how they are alike;
   **contrast** = how they differ. A reader asks both questions of the things a text
   holds.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator reads a short passage with two
   characters facing the same problem, then thinks aloud: "They are alike because…
   they are different because…" Watch each detail land in the "same" or "different"
   column, with a reason from the text.
4. **Compare and contrast yourself (15 min).** Read your text. Choose two things to
   compare — two characters, two events, or two ideas. Sort what is the same and what is
   different on your chart, and be ready to say *why*, pointing to the text.
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner one "same" and one "different," each with a reason.
   Did your partner's reasons come from the text?
6. **Close (5 min).** Reading deeply is noticing both likeness and difference. Both open
   the meaning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare two things with a prepared list of traits; the learner sorts each
  into "same" or "different."
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort picture cards into "same" and "different" columns, or
  sign the comparison.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Compare from a text heard aloud, stating similarities
  and differences orally or in braille or large print.
- **Extension:** Compare three things in a text, and write one sentence for each pairing
  explaining how they are alike and different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one similarity and one
  difference between two things in a text, each supported by a detail from the text?

## Home connection

At home, compare two things you notice — two foods, two songs, two days — and name one
thing that is the same and one that is different.
