# Lesson 5 — Finding Roots in Words

## Summary

Learners learn that many words are built on a **root** — a small part that carries the
word's core meaning. They explore how one root grows into a whole family of related
words (for example, the root **port**, meaning "carry," grows into *portable*,
*transport*, *import*, and *export*), and use the root to figure out unfamiliar words.

## Objectives

- Use context clues and word parts, such as roots, prefixes, and suffixes, to figure
  out unfamiliar words. _(D04.S2.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of a family: many people, one shared name, one shared story. Words have families
too. A **root** is the shared part that carries the core meaning, and the words built on
it are its children. If you know what the root means, you can often guess a whole word
you have never met — like recognizing a family member by the family name. Roots turn a
strange word into a familiar one.

## Materials

- A word-root chart
- A set of root cards

## Preparation

- Choose one root to model (for example, **port** = "carry").
- Prepare the root chart and root cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Roots are a taught skill, so model the word tree with a worked
example, thinking aloud about how the root's meaning lives inside each word (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006). Use roots that clearly carry meaning, such as **port** ("carry"),
**struct** ("build"), or **spect** ("look"). This is script- and language-agnostic: every
language builds words from meaningful parts, though the parts differ; invite learners to
share roots from their own languages too. The goal is meaning-making, not memorizing a
list — a learner who can explain *why* a word means what it does has the skill. Honor
guesses and explanations in the learner's own language, and be generous: a reasonable
connection between root and word is success, not a perfect etymology. Where a digital
word-part tool is used, treat it as a helper to confirm, not a substitute for the
thinking.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a word you know that is long and built from smaller
   parts. What does each part do?
2. **Meet the root (5 min).** Learn what a **root** is: the small part of a word that
   carries its core meaning. Many words share one root, and the root tells you what they
   all have in common.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator draws a word tree: the trunk is
   the root **port** ("carry"); the branches are *portable* (easy to carry), *transport*
   (carry across), *import* (carry in), *export* (carry out). Watch how the root's
   meaning lives in every branch.
4. **Grow your own word tree (15 min).** Choose a root. List words that share it, and
   tell how the root gives each word its meaning. Draw or write your tree.
5. **Figure out a new word (5 min).** Meet an unfamiliar word that shares your root.
   Use the root to guess what it means, then check by explaining it to a partner.
6. **Close (5 min).** Know the root, and a whole family of words opens to you. Word
   parts are keys.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one root and match pre-made words to it; the learner explains how the
  root lives in each.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the root and to the part of each word that
  carries it, or sign the shared meaning.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Hear the root and words read aloud, or read them in
  braille or large print, and explain the shared meaning orally.
- **Extension:** Find the roots of three words in your own reading and explain each
  word's meaning from its root, checking in a dictionary.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify a root in unfamiliar words and
  use its meaning to figure out or explain the words?

## Home connection

At home, pick one word you use often and try to find its root — ask someone, or look it
up — then name another word that shares it.
