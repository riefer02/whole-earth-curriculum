# Lesson 14 — Language Families and Shared Roots

## Summary

Learners discover that languages belong to **families**, and that many languages
**share roots**. They use a living example — the word for "mother" in several related
languages — to see how one older word grew into a family of cousin languages, and they
recognize that shared roots are a record of shared human history.

## Objectives

- Explain that languages belong to families and that many languages share roots, giving
  an example. _(D04.S4.04.01)_

## Connection

Think of a family reunion: cousins from far places, who have never met, still share a
name and a grandparent. Languages are like that. The word for "mother" in English
(*mother*), Spanish (*madre*), French (*mère*), German (*Mutter*), and Hindi (*mātā*) all
descend from one very old word, spoken by people long ago. Languages that share a root
are cousins, and their shared words are the family stories carried down the ages.

## Materials

- A language-family chart
- A "shared root" word card set (for example, "mother" in several related languages)

## Preparation

- Prepare the language-family chart and word cards.
- Gather the languages spoken or signed in the room, and any other sources available.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Teaching language families is an act of noticing, not ranking:
guide learners to *see and hear* shared roots, and to understand that every language —
related or not — is equal in worth (UNESCO holds that every language is a bearer of
dignity). The example of "mother" is a real one: linguists reconstruct a common ancestor
for English *mother*, Spanish *madre*, French *mère*, German *Mutter*, and Hindi *mātā*
(the Proto-Indo-European root **méh₂tēr*; see Fortson, *Indo-European Language and
Culture*). Keep it honest about evidence: this ancient word is a *reconstruction* that
linguists infer by comparing the descendant words — a scientific story, offered as such,
not a revealed truth. Other families have their own shared roots (for example, in the
Bantu family, the word for "person" is *muntu* in some languages and *motho* in others —
a shared root across a wide family). Invite every learner's home language into the
circle, each entry equal. A language that has no "living relatives" is not less — it is
simply its own branch, and it deserves the same honor. Treat translation tools as helpers
that can show words, never as a replacement for the people who live the languages.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of your home language and the word you use for "mother."
   Others in the world use words that sound surprisingly close. Why might that be?
2. **Meet the language family (5 min).** Learn what a **language family** is: a group of
   languages that grew from one older language, like branches from one root. **Cousin
   languages** share words because they share that root.
3. **Watch a worked example (10 min).** The facilitator shows the word for "mother" in
   several related languages — *mother, madre, mère, Mutter, mātā* — and points out the
   shared sounds. They explain: linguists compare these words to reconstruct one old
   root they all grew from. Watch how one root becomes a family.
4. **Find a family yourself (15 min).** With the word cards or voices in the room, compare
   one word across several languages. Which ones sound like cousins? Say or sign each, and
   notice the shared root.
5. **Share (5 min).** Tell a partner which languages you compared and what they shared.
   Did you find a family?
6. **Close (5 min).** Languages are families, and their shared roots are the memory of our
   common past. No language is better than another — every branch is part of the great
   human tree.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare just two related words and name the one sound they share.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Compare signed or written forms of the word and point to
  the shared parts.
- **Support (blind/low vision):** Hear each word spoken and say which sound the same, or
  read the words in braille or large print.
- **Extension:** Find a second shared-root example (such as "night" — *night, Nacht,
  nuit*) and explain what makes it a family.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain, with an example, that languages
  belong to families and share roots?

## Home connection

At home, ask everyone for the word "mother" (or "water" or "three") in every language
they know, and look for the ones that sound like cousins.
