# Lesson 7 — Partners in Life: Pollination and Symbiosis

## Summary

Learners describe a **symbiotic relationship — pollination** — and explain how **both organisms
benefit**. They work the bee-and-flower pair as a worked example, then trace a second partnership
(coral and algae) the same way.

## Objectives

- Describe a symbiotic relationship, such as pollination, and explain how both organisms benefit or
  are affected. _(D10.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Watch a bee at a flower. The bee is not doing the flower a favor out of kindness — it is there for
the sweet nectar, its food. Yet as it moves from flower to flower, it carries pollen on its body
and, without meaning to, helps the flower make seeds. Each side gets something it needs. Life is
full of these partnerships, called **symbiosis**, where two different living things depend on each
other. Today you learn how they work.

## Materials

- Partnership sheet
- Partnership cards

## Preparation

- Copy the partnership sheet and partnership cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall that matter cycles through an ecosystem. Today we look at the
  *partnerships* that keep that cycle moving — like pollination, which lets plants make the next
  generation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) symbiosis is a close
relationship between two different species; in mutualism, both benefit; (2) pollination is a
mutualism — the pollinator gets food (nectar, pollen) and the plant gets its pollen moved so it can
make seeds; (3) "benefit" means each side gets something it needs to live or reproduce, not that
they intend to help each other.** Model the bee-and-flower pair once (worked example, S-011). The
environment lens: symbiosis shows that living systems are webs of interdependence, not solitary
competition. The global lens: pollination happens worldwide, with many different partners — bees,
butterflies, moths, bats, birds — differing by region. The technology & ethics lens: humans depend
on pollination for much of our food, so caring for pollinators is also caring for ourselves and for
farmers everywhere. Keep the science precise: pollination is a real mutualism; coral gets food from
its symbiotic algae, and the algae get shelter (S-215, S-330); distinguish these facts from the
value that interdependence is worth honoring.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What cycles through an ecosystem? Today we meet the partnerships that keep
   the cycle going.
2. **Meet the idea (8 min).** **Symbiosis** is a close relationship between two different species.
   When **both benefit**, it is called **mutualism**. **Pollination** is one famous example.
3. **Watch it modeled — bee and flower (15 min).** The **bee** visits a **flower** for **nectar and
   pollen — its food**. As it feeds, pollen sticks to its body. When it flies to the next flower,
   some pollen rubs off — and the **flower gets its pollen moved, so it can make seeds**. Neither
   is being kind; both are getting something they need. Fill in your two arrows: bee gets food;
   flower gets seeds made.
4. **Trace a second partnership (15 min).** With a group, take the **coral and algae** card. Coral
   gives the tiny algae a **safe, sunny home**; the algae make **food** the coral can use. Name
   what each side gets, and draw the two arrows.
5. **Close (7 min).** Share one partnership you traced. Remember: **in mutualism, both sides
   benefit — even if neither one planned it.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two benefits pre-listed and ask learners to match each to the right
  partner.
- **Extension:** Contrast mutualism with a relationship where one side benefits and the other is
  harmed (parasitism), using a named example, and say what makes them different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name both benefits in a symbiotic pair and explain
  why each is a benefit?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed partnership sheet for coral and algae, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Find a partnership near home — a pollinator at a flower, lichen on a rock, or a bird that eats
insects off a large animal. Name what each side gets.
