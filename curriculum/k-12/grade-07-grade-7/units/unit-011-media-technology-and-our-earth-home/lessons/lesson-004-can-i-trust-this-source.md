# Lesson 4 — Can I Trust This Source?

## Summary

Learners learn **five questions** that make a source trustworthy and use them to **compare two
sources on the same topic for reliability**. They work one pair of sources as a modeled example,
then compare a second pair with a partner.

## Objectives

- Explain what makes a source trustworthy, and compare two sources on the same topic for
  reliability. _(D10.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

Every day you meet claims — a headline, a post, a label, a friend's message. Some of them are
solid; some are not. The difference is not how loudly a claim is repeated or how confident it
sounds, but *where it comes from*. A trustworthy source has a name behind it, a reason for
existing you can see, and evidence you can check. Today you learn five questions that separate a
source you can lean on from one you should hold lightly.

## Materials

- Five-questions checklist
- Source pairs

## Preparation

- Copy the five-questions checklist and source pairs.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall what a persuasive technique is (D10.S2.06.02). Today we move
  from "what is this trying to do to me?" to "can I trust what it says?"

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) trustworthiness is checked,
not felt — five questions do the checking: who made it, why, what evidence, when, and whether a
second source agrees; (2) comparing two sources means asking all five questions of each and then
placing them side by side; (3) reliability is about the source, not whether we like its answer.**
Model the first pair once (worked example, S-011). The critical-thinking lens: this is the unit's
core "question the answer" habit, made into a checklist. The egalitarian lens: the ability to check
a source is power — people who are lied to cannot act fairly; teaching everyone to check is a
leveling act. The global lens: the five questions work on any source in any language and any
place. The technology lens: digital tools make it easier than ever to publish — which makes
checking *more* necessary, not less. Use a low-stakes, non-scary example pair (e.g., a claim about
whether a plant is safe, or whether recycling works) so the skill is practiced without fear. The
checklist is an age-appropriate version of source analysis (S-217; S-063).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What is a persuasive technique? Today we ask a harder question: is this
   source telling me something I can trust?
2. **Meet the five questions (8 min).** To check a source, ask: **Who made it? Why was it made?
   What evidence does it give? When was it made? Does another source agree?** Look at your
   checklist — you ask all five, not one.
3. **Watch it modeled (15 min).** Here is a claim: *"Drinking water with a little salt cures all
   illness."* Source A is a signed page from a health organization, with the author named, the
   purpose (to inform) stated, evidence cited, a recent date, and agreement from other health
   bodies. Source B is an anonymous post with no author, no evidence, no date, and the clear
   purpose of selling a salt product. Run all five questions on both. Source A is far more
   reliable — not because it is polite, but because it survives the questions.
4. **Compare a second pair (15 min).** With a partner, take a source pair on one topic. Run the
   five questions on each, then say which is more reliable and why.
5. **Close (7 min).** Share one difference you found. Remember: **trust is earned by evidence, not
   by confidence.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the five questions with two possible answers each ("author named / no
  author named") and ask learners to circle the more reliable one.
- **Extension:** Find a third source on the same topic and rank all three for reliability, with
  reasons.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the five questions and apply them to two
  sources to say which is more reliable, with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed comparison of one source pair, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Pick one claim you see this week — on a screen, a package, or a sign — and ask the five questions
about where it came from. Tell someone what you found.
