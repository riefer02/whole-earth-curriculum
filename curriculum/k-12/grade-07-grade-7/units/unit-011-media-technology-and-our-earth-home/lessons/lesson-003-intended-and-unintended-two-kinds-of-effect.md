# Lesson 3 — Intended and Unintended: Two Kinds of Effect

## Summary

Learners distinguish an **intended effect** of a technology from an **unintended effect**. They
work the **plastic bag** as a worked example — designed to carry things cheaply, it also ended up
in rivers and oceans, harming wildlife — then sort a second technology the same way.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between an intended effect and an unintended effect of a technology, with
  an example of each. _(D10.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Think of a plastic bag. Somewhere, someone designed it to do one job: hold your shopping, cheaply
and lightly. That part worked exactly as planned. But the same bag, dropped or blown away, can
wash into a river and then the sea, where a turtle or a fish might swallow it. Nobody sat down and
*planned* that second part — it happened anyway. Every technology has these two kinds of effect,
and learning to see both is part of using tools wisely.

## Materials

- Two-kinds-of-effect sheet
- Technology cards

## Preparation

- Copy the two-kinds-of-effect sheet and technology cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the four questions for tracing a technology (begin, travel,
  adopt, change). Today we zoom in on the last part — what changed — and split it into two kinds.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) an intended effect is the
result a designer planned for; an unintended effect is a consequence that happened anyway, planned
or not; (2) "unintended" is not the same as "unimportant" — an unplanned harm is still a real harm;
(3) seeing unintended effects is a skill, and it helps us design and choose better.** Model the
plastic bag once (worked example, S-011). The technology lens: design always aims at some effects
and misses others — that gap is exactly where unintended effects live. The environment lens: many
unintended effects land on the living Earth (plastic in the ocean, S-216; animals mistaking
plastic for food, S-066). The egalitarian lens: unintended harm often falls hardest on people with
the least power to avoid it — ask "who is harmed?" The ethics lens: noticing an unintended effect
raises a question — now that we know, what do we owe? Keep the distinction descriptive first
(what happened) and normative second (what we should do about it). Distinguish evidence (plastic
enters rivers and oceans and can harm wildlife — S-216) from value (we *should* reduce it).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What four questions trace a technology's journey? Today we split "what
   changed" into two kinds.
2. **Meet the two kinds (5 min).** An **intended effect** is the result someone planned for. An
   **unintended effect** is a consequence that happened anyway, whether or not anyone planned it.
3. **Watch it modeled — the plastic bag (15 min).** The bag was designed to **carry things cheaply
   and lightly** — that is its intended effect. But bags also blow into gutters, wash into rivers,
   and reach the ocean, where animals can swallow them or get tangled — that is an **unintended
   effect**. Sort these onto your two columns. Notice: the harm is real even though no one planned
   it.
4. **Sort a second technology (15 min).** With a partner, take a technology card — the mobile
   phone or the car. List its intended effect, then list at least one unintended effect. Say which
   kind each is.
5. **Close (10 min).** Share one unintended effect you found. Remember: **unintended does not mean
   unimportant.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the bag's effects pre-listed and ask learners to sort them into the two
  columns, then add one effect of their own.
- **Extension:** For an unintended effect, ask "who is harmed most?" and propose one change to the
  design or its use that might reduce that harm.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner give an intended and an unintended effect of a
  technology and say which is which?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed two-column sheet for one technology, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Choose one everyday object at home (a bottle, a phone, a food wrapper). With someone, name what it
was made to do and one thing it does that probably was not planned.
