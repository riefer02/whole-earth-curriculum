# Lesson 11 — Peer Feedback That Helps: Give and Use It

## Summary

Learners give and use **peer feedback** to improve a draft, using sentence frames that
make feedback **specific, kind, and useful**. They revise one part of their own writing
and **explain the choice** they made — so feedback becomes authorship, not correction.

## Objectives

- Give and use peer feedback to improve a piece of writing, and explain the choices made.
  _(D04.S3.07.02)_
- Apply precise-language revision while responding to feedback. _(D04.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

A writer alone sees what they *meant* to write; a reader sees what is *actually* on the
page. That is why good writers trade drafts — in a newsroom in Lagos, a poets' circle in
Kyiv, or a classroom anywhere. The trick is that feedback works only when it is
*specific* ("the detail about the tin roof made me feel the heat"), *kind* (it respects
the writer's aim), and *useful* (it suggests a next step). And the writer keeps the final
word: you decide which advice to take, and you can say why.

## Materials

- Feedback sentence frames
- Feedback loop page
- Draft from the genre lesson

## Preparation

- Copy the feedback frames and the loop page for each learner.
- Have learners bring their genre-page drafts from Lesson 10.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **feedback is a
gift with three parts — specific, kind, useful — and the writer stays the author**. Model
the three stems on a sample draft before learners practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006; Hattie's feedback finding, S-012). Protect writers: feedback comments on the *text*,
never the person, and every writer chooses what to accept and explains why. The four
lenses: *ethics* (honest but kind; no fake praise, no cruelty); *egalitarianism* (every
draft deserves a careful reader, and every reader's view counts — mastery, not ranking);
*social-emotional* (receiving feedback can sting; name that and keep the focus on the
work); *critical-thinking* (choosing which advice to take is judgment). See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time someone told you how to make something better.
   What made that advice easy to hear — or hard?
2. **Meet the frames (10 min).** Read the sentence frames. Helpful feedback is
   **specific** (points to a place), **kind** (respects the aim), and **useful** (suggests
   a next step). Worked example on a sample draft: *"I noticed the detail about the roof
   made me feel the heat — it works because it is specific."* and *"What if you added one
   more sensory detail to the storm?"*
3. **Give feedback (15 min).** Trade drafts with a partner. Read carefully. On the loop
   page, write one **"I noticed…"** (what works and why) and one **"What if you…"** (a
   next step), using the frames. Then switch roles.
4. **Use feedback (10 min).** Read what your partner wrote. Choose one suggestion to use —
   or choose not to, and say why. Revise one part of your draft. Under your revision,
   write one sentence: *"I changed ___ because ___."*
5. **Close (5 min).** You have given a gift and used one. Feedback made your writing
   yours in a deeper way. Next lesson the unit turns to the many languages of the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the frames with blanks ("I noticed ___ because ___") and accept one
  sentence of feedback and one small revision.
- **Extension:** Explain two choices — one you accepted and one you declined — with a
  reason for each.

## Assessment

- **Peer-check:** The feedback given is checked against the three parts (specific, kind,
  useful).
- **Portfolio:** The loop page with the revision and the "I changed ___ because ___"
  sentence, kept as evidence of using feedback.
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their revision and confirms they can explain the
  choice.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one piece of feedback on something you made or wrote, then say
back what you will change and why.
