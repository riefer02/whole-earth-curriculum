# Lesson 6 — Setting and Plot: The World and What Happens

## Summary

Learners trace how a story's **setting** (where and when it happens) and its **plot**
(what happens, in order) develop together. They map a short original story onto a
**story-mountain** — rising action, climax, falling action, resolution — and explain how
the setting causes the conflict.

## Objectives

- Analyze how an author develops setting and plot across a text, and how the two work
  together. _(D04.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

Every story happens *somewhere*, and that somewhere usually causes the trouble. A story
set beside a flooding river is about the flood; a story set on a dry steppe is about
water; a story set in a crowded city is about people. The **setting** is not wallpaper —
it is the engine of the **plot**. Whether the story comes from a fishing village in
Senegal, a mountain pass in Nepal, or a port city in Chile, the reader's job is the
same: find the world, then watch what it makes happen.

## Materials

- Story-mountain page
- "The River Crossing (original short story)"
- Highlighter or pencil

## Preparation

- Copy the story-mountain page and the story for each learner.
- Read the story once; mark where the climax occurs.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **setting and
plot develop together — the setting causes the conflict, and the plot is the chain of
what happens because of it**. Model one worked example: read the story aloud, then place
the first two beats on the mountain and say *why* the setting caused them (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006). The story is an original teaching text, not a sacred or cultural
narrative; treat it as one invented example. The four lenses: *environment* (the natural
world is a character — flood, steppe, city); *global* (compare this river story with
water stories learners know); *egalitarianism* (in the story, everyone must work together
to cross — no hero alone); *critical-thinking* (plot beats need text evidence). See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a story you know. Where does it happen? Would the story
   still work if you moved it somewhere else? Why or why not?
2. **Read the story (10 min).** Read "The River Crossing" once, then once more. As you
   read, underline words that tell you the *setting* (place, weather, time).
3. **Map the mountain (15 min).** On your story-mountain, write the setting in the base
   box. Then fill the beats: the **rising action** (what builds), the **climax** (the
   turning point), the **falling action**, and the **resolution** (how it ends).
4. **Link setting to plot (10 min).** With a partner, say it aloud: "Because the river
   was flooding, …" — trace how the setting *caused* the conflict. What would change if
   the setting were a dry plain?
5. **Close (5 min).** Setting and plot are one machine. Tomorrow you will trace the
   third strand: the **central idea** an author develops across a whole text.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the mountain with the setting and climax pre-filled; learners add
  the resolution only.
- **Extension:** Rewrite the story's ending in a *different* setting and explain how the
  plot changes.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place the climax correctly and name one
  way the setting causes the conflict?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads the mountain and confirms each beat is backed by a
  sentence from the story.

## Home connection

Retell the plot of a film or story you watched to someone at home in four beats —
including the climax — and ask them to guess the setting from your retelling.

## The River Crossing (original short story)

It was the season of rains, and the river behind the village had risen in the night. By
morning it was no longer a river but a wide, brown road of rushing water, carrying
branches and a drowned cooking pot. The only bridge — three planks and a rope — was
half under the flood.

An old woman stood on the near bank with a sack of seed rice. A boy stood beside her with
a goat on a rope. "We must cross before the water rises more," the woman said. "The seed
must reach the far fields, or the planting will be late."

The planks were slick. The boy went first, leading the goat. The goat bleated and slipped,
and the boy grabbed the rope with both hands. The current pulled. "Help me," he called.

The old woman set down the rice, took the rope, and pulled with all her strength. The boy
dug his heels into the mud. The goat scrambled onto the bank. Then the boy held the rope
for the old woman as she crossed, the sack of seed balanced on her head.

On the far bank, the two of them stood and watched the river swallow the bridge. "We
crossed just in time," the boy said. The old woman smiled. "No," she said. "We crossed
because we pulled together."
