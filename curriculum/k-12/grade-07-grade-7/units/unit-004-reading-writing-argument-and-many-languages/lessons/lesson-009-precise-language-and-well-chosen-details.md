# Lesson 9 — Precise Language and Well-Chosen Details

## Summary

Learners develop ideas with **relevant, well-chosen details** and **precise language**. They
compare vague and precise sentence pairs, revise three vague sentences of their own, and
explain why each new detail belongs to the idea — the writer's version of the evidence
standard they applied as readers and listeners.

## Objectives

- Develop ideas with relevant, well-chosen details and precise language. _(D04.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

"Somewhere there was a thing that did something." That sentence is true of almost
everything and tells you almost nothing. Writing comes alive when the words get specific:
not "a bird" but "a wet-eyed heron," not "it was hot" but "the tin roof burned your
hand." The same idea — a hard journey, a good meal, a storm — can be told vaguely or
precisely, in any language. Precise writing is not fancy writing; it is *honest* writing,
and it is how you make a reader see what you see.

## Materials

- Vague-to-precise pairs
- Revision page
- Highlighters or two colors of pencil

## Preparation

- Copy the vague-to-precise pairs and the revision page for each learner.
- Prepare one vague sentence to revise together on the board.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **precise writing
chooses details that belong to the idea — relevant, concrete, sensory — and cuts details
that wander off**. Model one worked example: take a vague sentence, revise it step by
step, and say *why* each added detail belongs (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006; Graham
& Perin, 2007). Emphasize "relevant" as much as "precise": a precise detail that does not
fit the idea is clutter, not craft. The four lenses: *ethics* (precision is honesty — the
writer refuses to blur); *egalitarianism* (every learner's language has precise words;
none is "more precise" by nature); *global* (sensory details differ by place — a roof of
tin, of thatch, of snow — and all can be precise); *critical-thinking* (choosing is
judging: which detail earns its place?). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Say this sentence: "The weather was bad." Now say it so someone can
   *see* it. What did you add?
2. **Compare pairs (10 min).** Read the vague-to-precise pairs. For each, find the words
   that changed: vague nouns become concrete, weak verbs become exact, general weather
   becomes sensory detail. Underline the precise words.
3. **Revise three sentences (15 min).** On your revision page, rewrite each vague
   sentence with at least two relevant, precise details. Use words a reader can see,
   hear, smell, or feel.
4. **Check relevance (10 min).** With a partner, read your revisions. For each added
   detail, ask: "Does this detail belong to the idea?" Cross out any detail that wanders
   off, and keep the ones that earn their place.
5. **Close (5 min).** Precise writing is a choice you make word by word. Tomorrow you
   will shape one idea into many different *genres*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank and revise one sentence at a time; allow one added
  detail instead of two.
- **Extension:** Revise a paragraph of your own from another subject, adding precise
  details and cutting one irrelevant one.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner replace vague words with precise ones and
  name why a detail is relevant?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads each revision and confirms every added detail can
  be seen, heard, or felt — and belongs to the idea.

## Home connection

Describe one ordinary thing at home to someone — first vaguely, then precisely — and ask
them which version they could see.
