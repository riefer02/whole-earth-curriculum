# Lesson 10 — One Idea, Many Genres

## Summary

Learners write the same idea in **three genres** — narrative, argument, and
informational — keeping the language precise in each and adapting to each genre's
audience and purpose. They learn that genre is a choice of shape, not a change to the
truth of the idea.

## Objectives

- Develop ideas with relevant details and precise language across a range of genres.
  _(D04.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

The same true thing can be told many ways. That the river floods every rainy season can
be a *story* ("the year the water reached our door"), an *argument* ("we should move the
seed store to higher ground, because…"), or *information* ("in this region, heavy rains
arrive between June and September"). Each shape fits a different audience and purpose —
a story moves the heart, an argument moves the mind, information builds knowledge — and
each needs precise language to do its job. Genre is not a cage; it is a tool you choose.

## Materials

- Genre-shapes poster
- Genre page
- Example set

## Preparation

- Copy the genre page and the example set for each learner.
- Prepare the genre-shapes poster with one central idea and three branches.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **genre is a
choice of shape — narrative tells, argument persuades, information explains — and precise
language serves each differently**. Model all three genres for one idea as a worked
example before learners write their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006; Graham &
Perin, 2007). Let the idea be small and safe; learners may choose their own. The four
lenses: *ethics* (the truth of the idea stays the same across genres — no exaggerating to
persuade); *egalitarianism* (every culture has these three ways of speaking — story,
plea, account — in its own language); *global* (example ideas from many regions);
*critical-thinking* (choosing a genre is matching shape to audience and purpose). See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one idea you believe: "we should save water," "everyone
   deserves rest," "the market opens early." How many different ways could you tell it?
2. **Meet the three shapes (10 min).** Look at the poster and the example set — one idea,
   three versions. **Narrative** tells a story with characters and events. **Argument**
   makes a claim and gives reasons and evidence. **Informational** gives accurate facts.
   Read each example and name its audience and purpose.
3. **Write your three (20 min).** On your genre page, choose one idea and write it three
   ways: a short story (a few sentences), a short argument (claim + two reasons), and a
   short informational note (two facts). Keep the language precise in each.
4. **Check the shape fits (10 min).** With a partner, read your three versions. Does the
   story actually tell? Does the argument actually persuade? Does the information stay
   factual? Revise one version that does not yet fit its shape.
5. **Close (5 min).** You can now bend one idea into many shapes without breaking its
   truth. Next lesson you will trade writing with a partner and make each other's work
   better.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the idea and the three starter frames; allow two genres instead of
  three.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth shape — a poem — and explain how poetry's audience and
  purpose differ from the other three.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner write one idea in three genres, each
  matching its shape and staying precise?
- **Portfolio:** The genre page, kept as a draft for revision in the next lesson.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one idea three ways — as a story, as a reason to agree, and as a
fact — and ask which way convinced or moved them most.
