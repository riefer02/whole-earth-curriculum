# Lesson 7 — Tracking a Central Idea Across a Text

## Summary

Learners trace a **central idea** — the big point an author develops across a whole text —
using the image of a thread running through beads. They read one short nonfiction passage
and one short story, state each central idea in a sentence, and name the moments that
carry it from beginning to end.

## Objectives

- Analyze how an author develops a central idea across a text, tracing it from beginning
  to end with evidence. _(D04.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

A text is not just events on a string — it is a string with a thought running through it.
A news story about a new well in a dry region, a tale about a girl who plants one tree,
a report about migrating birds: each has a **central idea** the author keeps returning to,
the thread that holds the beads together. Pull the beads off and the thread remains.
Tracing that thread — in an essay from Cairo, a story from Manila, a report from Lima —
is how you understand what the author really wants you to carry away.

## Materials

- Thread-and-beads page
- Two short texts
- Highlighter or pencil

## Preparation

- Copy the thread-and-beads page and the two texts for each learner.
- Read the texts once; mark the sentences that carry the central idea.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a central idea
is developed, not announced — through repetition, examples, and contrast — and the reader
traces it with evidence**. Model one worked example: read the first text aloud, state its
central idea in one sentence, and point to two places the author develops it (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006; Duke & Pearson, 2002). The texts are original teaching passages;
treat them as examples, and invite learners to bring their own. The four lenses: *ethics*
(what the author believes matters); *egalitarianism* (whose story is centered, whose is
left out); *global* (central ideas appear in every tradition); *critical-thinking* (a
central idea needs evidence, not a feeling). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a song, a film, or a story you know well. If you had to
   say its one big idea in a sentence, what would it be?
2. **Read the two texts (12 min).** Read the nonfiction passage once, then the story.
   As you read, underline any sentence that seems to carry the "big point."
3. **Trace the thread (15 min).** On your page, write each text's **central idea** on the
   thread in one sentence. On the beads, write two or three moments from the text that
   carry that idea — the repetition, the example, the contrast.
4. **Name the craft (10 min).** With a partner, say *how* the author develops the idea:
   by repeating it, by giving examples, or by contrasting it with its opposite?
5. **Close (5 min).** Character, setting, plot, and now the central idea — you can read a
   text the way an author builds it. Next lesson you turn from *reading* to *judging*:
   can you trust the source?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the central idea pre-written; learners find and copy one sentence
  that carries it for each bead.
- **Extension:** Compare the two texts: do they share a central idea? Write one sentence
  comparing how each develops it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a central idea in one sentence and
  point to two moments that carry it?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads the thread and checks that each bead names a real
  moment from the text, not a guess.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home the central idea of a film or story you both know in one sentence,
then name two moments that carried it.

## Text A — One Well for Many (original nonfiction passage)

In a dry region, a single well can change a whole community. Before the well, the women
and children walked for hours each day to fetch water from a distant river, and the
water was often not clean. After the well was dug, the walk became minutes, and the
water ran clear. Children who once spent the morning carrying water now spent it in
school. Gardens that had withered now grew vegetables. The well did not bring water to
one family — it brought it to all, and everyone helped keep it clean and shared the
work of drawing. One well, shared fairly, is worth more than a hundred private ones.

## Text B — The Girl Who Planted One Tree (original short story)

Mina lived where the hills had been cut bare for firewood. One day she planted a single
seedling beside the path and watered it every morning. Her brother laughed: "One tree
will not bring back the forest." Mina kept watering. The seedling grew. It held the soil
when the rains came, and birds nested in it, and their droppings carried new seeds. Year
after year, one tree became a patch of green, and the patch became a wood. When the
children of the village asked Mina how she had planted a forest, she said, "I did not
plant a forest. I planted one tree, and I did not stop."
