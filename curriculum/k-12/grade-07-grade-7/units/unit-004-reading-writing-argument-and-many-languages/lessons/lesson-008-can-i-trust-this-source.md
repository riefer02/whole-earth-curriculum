# Lesson 8 — Can I Trust This Source?

## Summary

Learners judge whether a source is **credible** by running a three-part check — **author,
purpose, evidence** — and reaching a verdict: trust, question, or reject. They practice on
four short sources that make the *same* claim but differ in who wrote them, why they wrote,
and what proof they offer.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the credibility of a source by checking its author, purpose, and evidence.
  _(D04.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

Not everything you read or hear is equally trustworthy. A claim that a certain tea cures
colds, that a certain phone is the best, that a certain event happened — the same claim
can come from a doctor or a stranger, from a newsroom or a seller, with proof or with
none. Before you believe, you check three things: **who** wrote it, **why** they wrote it,
and **what evidence** they give. This habit protects you and the people you care about,
whether the claim arrives by paper in Bamako, by radio in Manila, or by phone anywhere.

## Materials

- Three-part trust checker
- Source cards
- Highlighter or pencil

## Preparation

- Copy the trust checker and the source cards for each learner.
- Prepare four short sources making one shared claim. Adaptable example claim: "This herb
  tea cures colds." Sources: (1) a doctor's note with a study, (2) a seller's ad with no
  evidence, (3) an anonymous post with a strong feeling, (4) a health-organization page
  with named evidence.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **credibility is
checked, not felt — through author, purpose, and evidence — and the result is a verdict,
not a ranking of people**. Model one worked example: run all three checks on one source
aloud, reaching "trust" and saying *why* (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Distinguish
a source's *purpose* (to inform, to persuade, to sell) from its *bias*. The four lenses:
*ethics* (honest sources show their evidence); *egalitarianism* (a false health claim
hurts those with least access to doctors most); *global* (credibility checks work in every
language and media system); *technology* (the internet spreads both good and bad sources
at the same speed — the check matters more, not less). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you believed something and later found it was not
   true. What would you check now, before believing again?
2. **Meet the three-part check (10 min).** Read the trust checker. **Author:** who wrote
   this, and do they know the subject? **Purpose:** why — to inform, to persuade, or to
   sell? **Evidence:** what proof is given, and can it be checked? Worked example: run the
   three checks on source 1 aloud and reach a verdict.
3. **Check four sources (15 min).** With a partner, read the source cards. For each, fill
   the three boxes and write a verdict: **trust**, **question**, or **reject** — with one
   reason.
4. **Compare verdicts (10 min).** Two pairs share. Did you agree? Where you differed, what
   check changed the verdict? Notice: the *same claim* can be trustworthy or not depending
   on the source.
5. **Close (5 min).** You now own a shield and a tool: before you believe, check who, why,
   and what proof. Tomorrow you begin to write — with the same standards you now expect of
   others.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the verdicts pre-suggested and a sentence starter ("I question this
  because the author…"); learners check one source at a time.
- **Extension:** Look up the author of one source online and add what you found to the
  author box, revising your verdict if needed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the author, purpose, and evidence of a
  source and give a verdict with a reason?
- **Peer-check:** Partners exchange trust checkers and confirm each verdict is backed by
  at least one named check.

## Home connection

Pick one claim you hear this week — an ad, a message, a rumor — and run the three checks
aloud with someone at home: who, why, and what proof?
