# Lesson 13 — Interpretation: Meaning in the Moment

## Summary

Learners distinguish **interpretation** from **translation** — interpretation is spoken
and *live*, translation is written and can take time — and sort everyday situations into
which one is needed. They practice a short live interpretation and explain the choice,
completing their picture of how people bridge languages.

## Objectives

- Explain what interpretation is and describe when it — rather than translation — is
  needed in everyday life. _(D04.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

A medicine label can be translated once, carefully, and printed on a thousand bottles.
But when a grandmother describes her pain to a doctor in a language the doctor does not
speak, no printed page will do — someone must carry the meaning *right then*, in the
room, as it is spoken. That live, spoken bridge is **interpretation**. One is for the
page, the other for the moment; both are skills real people use every day, in a hospital
in Nairobi, a courtroom in São Paulo, or a parent–teacher meeting anywhere.

## Materials

- Translation vs interpretation poster
- Situation cards
- Situation sort page

## Preparation

- Make or print the two-column poster.
- Copy the situation cards and sort page for each learner.
- Prepare the eight situations (four written, four spoken). Adaptable set: a medicine
  label, a doctor's visit, a news broadcast, a family letter, a school meeting, a train
  announcement, a poem in a book, a market bargain.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **translation is
written and can take time; interpretation is spoken and live — and the situation decides
which is needed**. Model the sort with one written and one spoken situation as worked
examples before practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the distinction clean
but honest: a real interpreter may also translate, and some jobs need both. The four
lenses: *ethics* (an interpreter must be faithful *and* honest about uncertainty in the
moment); *egalitarianism* (interpretation lets people who do not share a language meet as
equals); *global* (live interpretation happens wherever languages meet — markets,
hospitals, ports); *social-emotional* (interpreting for a family member is a real
responsibility — honor the person, not just the words). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall translation — carrying written meaning across languages.
   What if the meaning must travel *right now*, as someone speaks? What do we call that?
2. **Meet the two columns (10 min).** Read the poster. **Translation:** written, can take
   time, polished. **Interpretation:** spoken, live, in the moment. Worked example: a
   medicine label → translation; a doctor's visit → interpretation. Say why for each.
3. **Sort the situations (15 min).** With a partner, take the situation cards and place
   each in the right column on your page. Under each, write one short reason.
4. **Practice a live bridge (10 min).** Choose one spoken situation and role-play it in
   pairs of three: a speaker, a listener who does not share the language, and an
   interpreter who carries the meaning live. Keep it short and kind.
5. **Close (5 min).** You now hold both tools: the pen for the page and the voice for the
   moment. Tomorrow the unit closes by honoring the *many ways* one language is spoken.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the situations pre-sorted; learners write the reason for each.
- **Extension:** Describe one real situation in your life that needed translation, one
  that needed interpretation, and one that needed both.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a situation correctly and give a
  reason that names written-vs-live?
- **Peer-check:** Partners compare sorts and defend any two different choices with
  reasons.

## Home connection

Think of a moment in your family or neighborhood where someone bridged two languages —
was it written or spoken? Tell someone at home what you decided.
