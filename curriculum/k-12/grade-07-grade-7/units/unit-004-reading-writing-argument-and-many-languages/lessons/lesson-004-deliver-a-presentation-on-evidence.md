# Lesson 4 — Deliver a Presentation on Evidence

## Summary

Learners turn the listening skills of the first three lessons around: they now *build*
an argument for others to evaluate. Each plans a short talk adapted to a chosen
**audience** and **purpose**, supports every point with **evidence**, delivers it, and
revises one point after feedback.

## Objectives

- Deliver a short presentation adapted to an audience and purpose, supporting points
  with evidence. _(D04.S1.07.02)_
- Apply the evaluation skills from earlier lessons to one's own and peers' presentations.
  _(D04.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Speaking to be understood is the mirror of listening carefully. The same chain you have
been checking in others — claim, reason, evidence — is now yours to build. You already
adapt without thinking: you explain a rule one way to a small child and another way to a
grandparent. A presentation just makes that adaptation deliberate: *who* am I speaking
to, *why* am I speaking, and *what evidence* will convince them?

## Materials

- Audience/purpose/evidence planner
- Presentation checklist
- Timer

## Preparation

- Copy the planner and checklist for each learner.
- Arrange small circles of 4–5 for delivery.
- Prepare an example topic list (a small, safe claim learners care about) for those who
  need a starting point.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a good
presentation is built, not performed: audience, purpose, and evidence come first**. Model
a 60-second talk once — stating the audience and purpose aloud, then giving two points
with evidence — before learners plan their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).
Keep topics small and safe; offer a topic list so no one is stuck. The four lenses:
*ethics* (support claims honestly, no invention); *egalitarianism* (every voice gets a
turn and a respectful hearing — no ranking); *global* (topic list spans regions and
languages); *technology* (if recording, use it for self-assessment, not to shame).
**Expression modes:** "deliver" means express the argument however works best — spoken
aloud, signed, written or typed and shown, drawn as a poster, or produced with a
communication device (AAC). No mode is more correct than another; assess the argument,
not the voice. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall the chain: claim → reason → evidence. Today you build a
   chain of your own and speak it to others. What is a topic you could talk about for one
   minute?
2. **Plan the talk (15 min).** On your planner, fill three boxes. **Audience:** who will
   hear this? (A class, a younger sibling, a neighbor, a committee.) **Purpose:** what do
   you want them to do or believe? **Evidence:** two pieces of proof for each point.
   Write two points, each with evidence.
3. **Model and rehearse (10 min).** Watch or hear the 60-second model once. Then
   rehearse yours with a partner: state your audience and purpose (spoken, signed, or
   written), give your two points, and end with your claim. Use the timer — about
   60–90 seconds.
4. **Deliver (15 min).** In your circle of 4–5, take turns. "Deliver" can be spoken,
   signed, shown as a poster, or shared with a communication device — you choose the
   mode that lets you say your argument best. Listeners use the checklist: claim clear?
   points supported? adapted to the audience? clear and paced?
5. **Revise one point (10 min).** Use one piece of feedback to change one point — add
   evidence, or reword for the audience. Say what you changed and why.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Allow a one-point, 45-second talk; provide sentence frames ("My claim is…,
  my evidence is…") and a pre-chosen audience. Offer a non-spoken delivery path — a
  written, drawn, signed, or device-assisted (AAC) presentation — as a first-class
  option, never a fallback.
- **Extension:** Deliver a two-minute version to a *different* audience and explain how
  the adaptation changed the evidence chosen.

## Assessment

- **Performance:** The delivered talk — spoken, signed, written/poster, or
  device-assisted — checked against the presentation checklist. Assess the argument and
  its adaptation to audience/purpose, not the mode of expression.
- **Peer-check:** The checklist, completed by listeners, naming one strength and one
  suggested change.
- **Self-check:** The learner names the one revision they made and why.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one thing you learned today, first the way you would tell a friend,
then the way you would tell a much younger child — and notice how you adapted it.
