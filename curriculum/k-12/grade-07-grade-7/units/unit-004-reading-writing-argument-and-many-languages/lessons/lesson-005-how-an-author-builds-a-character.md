# Lesson 5 — How an Author Builds a Character

## Summary

Learners trace how an author **develops a character across a text** — through actions,
words, and what the character learns — and back each step with **text evidence**. They
close-read a retelling of the Japanese folktale "The Stonecutter," map the change, and
apply the same map to a character of their own choosing.

## Objectives

- Analyze how an author develops a character across a text, using evidence from the text
  to support the reading. _(D04.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

People in stories are not the same at the end as they were at the beginning — and neither
are the people around you. The stonecutter who wishes for more, the friend who learns to
listen, the character who stops being afraid: an author shows these changes through small
clues — what a person *does*, *says*, and *learns*. Finding those clues is the reader's
detective work, and it works the same in a Japanese folktale, a novel from Lagos, or a
telenovela from Mexico City.

## Materials

- Character-change map
- "The Stonecutter (retold)"
- Highlighter or pencil

## Preparation

- Copy the character-change map and the tale for each learner.
- Read the tale once yourself; mark the sentences that show the change.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **character is
shown, not stated — through actions, words, and learning — and the reader supports the
reading with evidence from the text**. Model one worked example: read the tale aloud,
then mark the sentence where the character changes and say *why* it shows change
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006; Duke & Pearson, 2002). The tale is a folktale in the
public tradition; teach it as one culture's story among many, never as "the" way. The
four lenses: *ethics* (what the character values, and whether it changes); *egalitarianism*
(every culture's stories carry wisdom — none is the default); *global* (this is a Japanese
tale; compare with tales learners know); *critical-thinking* (claims about a character
need text evidence). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a character you know from a story, film, or series. How
   were they at the start? How were they at the end? What changed?
2. **Read the tale (10 min).** Read "The Stonecutter" once, then once more. As you read,
   underline any sentence that shows what the character is like or how he changes.
3. **Map the change (15 min).** On your map, write the character at the *beginning*
   (proud, wishing to be greater) and at the *end* (content, glad to be himself). On the
   line, write the **evidence** — the exact words that show the change. Example: at the
   end he wishes to be a stonecutter again and is happy.
4. **Name the craft (10 min).** How does the author show the change — through *actions*
   (what he does), *words* (what he asks for), or *learning* (what he understands)? Put
   a check beside each one you find.
5. **Close (5 min).** You can now trace a character's arc. Next lesson you will trace
   how the *world* of a story — its setting — and its plot develop too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the map with the beginning and end traits pre-filled; learners
  find and copy one sentence of evidence for each.
- **Extension:** Choose a character from your own reading and fill a second change map,
  naming the cause of the change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state how the character changed and point
  to a specific sentence as evidence?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their map and confirms each trait is backed by at
  least one piece of text evidence.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a character in a story or show you both know: how did they
change, and what made them change?

## The Stonecutter (retold)

Once there was a stonecutter who cut stone at the foot of a mountain. One hot day, as he
worked, a rich man passed in a fine carriage with servants. "How great it must be to be
rich," the stonecutter sighed. A spirit of the mountain heard him and made him rich.

But then the sun beat down on his fine house, and he wished to be the sun. The spirit
made him the sun. Then a cloud covered the sun, and he wished to be the cloud. The spirit
made him the cloud. Then the wind blew the cloud away, and he wished to be the wind. The
spirit made him the wind. Then the wind blew against the mountain and could not move it,
and he wished to be the mountain. The spirit made him the mountain.

Then, from far below, he felt a small tapping — a stonecutter, cutting stone at his foot.
"Ah," said the mountain, "it is good to be a stonecutter." And the spirit made him a
stonecutter again, and he was content.
