# Lesson 1 — What Did the Speaker Actually Claim?

## Summary

Learners separate a speaker's **claim** (the main point they want you to accept) from
their **support** (the reasons and evidence they offer). They practice on short,
everyday examples from several world regions, and begin to notice when support is thin
or missing — the first step toward evaluating reasoning.

## Objectives

- Separate a speaker's main claim from its support, telling reasons from evidence, and
  notice where support is thin or missing. _(D04.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

All day you hear people asking you to accept things: "This fruit is the freshest at
the market," "The bus comes at seven," "That medicine works," "My team is better."
Behind every one of these is a **claim** — a point someone wants you to believe — and
**support** — the reasons and evidence they give for it. Whether you are in a market in
Accra, a bus stop in Jakarta, or a clinic in La Paz, the first move of a careful
listener is the same: name the claim, then look for its support.

## Materials

- Claim/support poster
- Claim/support page
- Three short spoken examples

## Preparation

- Print or draw the claim/support poster: one claim bubble on top, three empty support
  boxes below.
- Copy a claim/support page for each learner.
- Prepare three short spoken claims (about 2–3 sentences each). Suggested, adaptable
  set: a market vendor's pitch, a radio weather report, a friend's opinion about a game
  or a place.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **every argument
has a claim and support, and the listener's first job is to separate them** — not yet to
judge whether the support is good (that comes next lesson). Use worked examples: model
naming a claim and its support aloud on the poster before learners try their own
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Choose examples from many places and languages so no
region or accent is the "default"; translate each into the learners' home language. Keep
the examples small and safe — market, weather, games — not loaded disputes. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall: in earlier grades you learned to find a speaker's *main
   argument*. Today you sharpen it. Think of the last time someone tried to convince you
   of something. What were they claiming?
2. **Meet claim and support (10 min).** Look at the poster. A **claim** is the point a
   speaker wants you to accept. **Support** is what they give for it — **reasons** (why
   they think so) and **evidence** (facts, examples, or proof). Worked example, said
   aloud: *"You should buy these tomatoes — **claim**. They were picked this morning —
   **evidence**. They will taste better than the old ones — **reason**."* Say each part
   with me.
3. **Sort three examples (15 min).** Listen to each example twice. For each, write the
   claim in the bubble and any support in the boxes below. Label each support as a
   *reason* or *evidence* if you can. Compare with a partner.
4. **Notice the gap (10 min).** Look at your pages. Which claim had the thinnest support?
   Which had none? A claim with little or no support is **unsupported** — it may still be
   true, but the speaker has not shown it yet.
5. **Close (5 min).** You can now see the skeleton of any argument: claim on top, support
   below. Tomorrow you will check whether the support actually holds the claim up.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the claim bubble pre-filled; learners only find and sort the
  support, and label "reason" vs "evidence" with a word bank.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to find a claim with two different kinds of support and a
  claim with a reason but no evidence, and write each down.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the claim in their own words and
  point to at least one piece of support, separating reason from evidence?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their page and checks that the claim is a *point to
  accept* (not the support) and that each support box holds a reason or evidence.

## Home connection

Listen to one claim at home this week — a family member's opinion, a radio or phone
message — and tell someone at home what the claim was and what support came with it.
