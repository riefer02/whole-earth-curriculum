# Lesson 12 — Translation: Carrying Words Across Languages

## Summary

Learners define **translation** — carrying meaning from a **source language** into a
**target language** — and discover that something is always gained and something lost.
They trace the real journeys of everyday words like "tea/chai" and "mother" across the
world, and practice translating one idea word-for-word and then naturally.

## Objectives

- Explain what translation is and what is gained or lost in it, and describe when it is
  needed in everyday life. _(D04.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

The word for the drink in your cup has traveled the world twice. It left China as *cha*
and walked overland through Central and South Asia, becoming *chai* in many languages;
it also left by sea from a southern port as *te*, becoming *tea*, *thé*, and *Tee* in
others. The same drink, two journeys, many words. **Translation** is that journey in
reverse: taking a meaning from one language and carrying it into another — and every
carrier knows that something is always gained, and something always left behind.

## Materials

- Word-journey poster
- Translation trios page
- Example set (tea, mother, thank you)

## Preparation

- Make or print the word-journey poster showing the two routes of "tea."
- Copy the trios page and example set for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **translation
carries meaning across languages, and it is a craft of choices because exact equivalence
is rare — something is always gained or lost** (Everett, 2012; WALS "tea," S-169). Model
one trio as a worked example before practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Always
begin in the learners' home language, and hold multilingualism as wealth, never deficit
(UNESCO, S-019). The four lenses: *ethics* (a translator must be faithful to the
speaker's meaning, not twist it); *egalitarianism* (no language is "untranslatable in a
lesser way" — every language has riches another lacks); *global* (words travel with
trade, migration, and empire — trace the routes); *critical-thinking* (word-for-word is
not the same as faithful). See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a word in your home language that has no exact match in
   another language you know. What would you have to *explain* to translate it?
2. **Trace a word's journey (10 min).** Look at the poster. One word, two routes: *cha*
   by land became *chai*; *te* by sea became *tea*, *thé*, *Tee*. What stayed? What
   changed? This is what translation works with — words that never match exactly.
3. **Meet the trio (10 min).** On your page, take one idea (a greeting, a proverb, a
   feeling). Box 1: the idea in your home language. Box 2: a **word-for-word gloss** —
   each word translated literally. Box 3: a **natural translation** — how a speaker would
   really say it. Worked example first, together.
4. **Find what is gained and lost (10 min).** With a partner, compare boxes 2 and 3.
   What does the gloss miss? What does the natural version add? Write one sentence: *"In
   translating ___, we gain ___, but we lose ___."*
5. **Close (5 min).** Translation is a bridge we build every day — for a sign, a
   medicine label, a story. Tomorrow you will meet its live, spoken partner:
   **interpretation**.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the idea and the word-for-word gloss; learners write only the
  natural translation.
- **Extension:** Translate the same idea into a third language you or a partner knows,
  and note a new thing lost or gained.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define translation and name one thing
  gained and one thing lost?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their "we gain / we lose" sentence and confirms it
  names a real difference between the gloss and the natural version.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a word in your language that has no exact match in another, and
try to translate it together — what would you have to explain?
