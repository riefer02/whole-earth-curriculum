# Lesson 3 — Unsupported Claims and Probing Questions

## Summary

Learners move from *finding* weak links to *asking about* them. They name an
**unsupported claim**, then ask a **probing question** — a question that presses for
missing support or tests a hidden assumption — in a way that is curious rather than
hostile. They practice on everyday claims from many regions.

## Objectives

- Identify an unsupported claim in everyday speech and ask a probing question that
  presses for missing support or tests an assumption. _(D04.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

When a claim has no support, you have two choices: accept it silently, or ask. The
question "How do you know?" is one of the most powerful tools a person owns. It is how
you protect yourself and others from a false cure, a bad deal, or a rumor — and it works
the same in a pharmacy in Nairobi, a repair shop in Hanoi, or a family kitchen anywhere.
A probing question is not an attack; it is a door you open together.

## Materials

- Question-stem cards
- Claim cards
- Probing page

## Preparation

- Make question-stem cards: "How do you know…?", "What is the evidence…?", "Could it
  also be…?", "Who says so, and why should we trust them…?", "What would change your
  mind…?"
- Prepare six short claims, some unsupported. Adaptable set: a health cure with no
  evidence, a "this brand is best" claim, a school rumor, a weather prediction, a money
  tip, a claim about a device.
- Copy a probing page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a probing
question asks for the missing support or tests an assumption — and it can be asked
kindly**. Model the tone as much as the words: "I wonder how we know that," not "prove
it!" Use worked examples before practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Where
questioning adults is culturally risky, frame questions as *wondering together*, and
offer an anonymous "wonder box" for written questions (see `docs/contexts.md`). The four
lenses: *ethics* (questioning to protect, not to wound); *egalitarianism* (anyone can ask;
questioning power, gently, is a shared right); *global* (question-stems travel across
languages); *social-emotional* (curiosity feels different from attack — practice the warm
tone). Keep claims small and safe.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall last lesson's broken chains. Today you do something about
   them: you ask. What question would you ask when a chain has no evidence?
2. **Meet the probing question (10 min).** A **probing question** asks for missing
   support or tests an assumption. Read the stems on the cards. Worked example: someone
   says *"This phone is the best."* Probing questions: *"How do you know?"* *"What
   evidence do you have?"* *"Could another phone also be good for a different reason?"*
   Say them with a warm voice.
3. **Probe six claims (15 min).** With a partner, take the claim cards. For each, first
   say whether the claim is supported or unsupported, then ask two probing questions
   using the stems. Write your best two on your page.
4. **Wonder together (10 min).** Choose one claim and role-play: one partner states the
   claim, the other asks a probing question, then they switch. Notice the *tone* — how
   does a question feel when it is curious rather than accusing?
5. **Close (5 min).** You now have a habit, not just a skill: when a claim has no
   support, you ask — kindly, and you keep asking until you see the reasoning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the stem cards plus a sentence starter ("I wonder how we know…")
  and let learners ask just one question per claim.
- **Extension:** For one claim, ask a question that tests a hidden assumption ("You
  assume… — is that always true?") and write it down.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a claim as unsupported and ask a
  question that genuinely probes for support or tests an assumption?
- **Peer-check:** Partners rate each other's questions with three checks: does it ask for
  support? is it kind? is it on the real claim?

## Home connection

Practice one "How do you know?" this week — asked gently to a friend, a sibling, or
yourself — and notice what the answer reveals about the support behind a claim.
