# Lesson 2 — The Reasoning Chain: Claim, Reason, Evidence

## Summary

Learners model reasoning as a **chain of three links** — claim, reason, evidence — and
learn to find the exact place a chain is weak. They test spoken arguments from several
world regions, naming a gap when a claim has no reason, a reason has no evidence, or the
evidence does not fit the claim.

## Objectives

- Trace the link from claim to reason to evidence in a spoken argument, and name where
  the chain is weak or broken. _(D04.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

A strong argument holds together like a chain. The **claim** is the first link, the
**reason** the second, the **evidence** the third. Pull on a chain and it breaks at its
weakest link — so the careful listener asks not just "what is the claim?" but "does each
link actually connect to the next?" A neighbor's advice about which seed to plant, a
radio story about a storm, a friend's case for which road to take: each one is a chain
you can check.

## Materials

- Chain poster
- Chain page
- Four short spoken arguments

## Preparation

- Make the chain poster: three paper links labeled claim, reason, evidence, plus one
  spare broken link.
- Copy a chain page for each learner.
- Prepare four short arguments (two strong, two with a broken link). Adaptable set: a
  market pitch (strong), a school proposal (strong), a health claim with no evidence
  (broken), a weather claim with off-topic evidence (broken).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **reasoning is a
chain, and evaluating it means finding the weak link — not rejecting the person**.
Model one full chain and one broken chain as worked examples before independent practice
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep arguments small and safe; translate everything
into the home language; vary accents and regions so no voice is "the correct one." The
four lenses: *ethics* (is the reasoning honest?); *egalitarianism* (does a weak chain
hurt some people more — e.g., a false health claim spread to those with less access to
doctors?); *global* (reasoning patterns appear everywhere); *technology* (radio, phone,
and internet carry claims faster, so checking chains matters more). See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall last lesson: a claim sits on top and support sits below.
   Today you check whether the support *holds*. What is the difference between a reason
   and evidence?
2. **Meet the chain (10 min).** Look at the chain poster. A full chain: **claim → reason
   → evidence.** Worked example, said aloud: *"We should leave early for the market —
   claim. The bridge floods in heavy rain — reason. It flooded twice last month — 
   evidence."* Now a broken one: *"This drink cures colds — claim. My uncle says so —
   reason. ... and then nothing."* Where is the break? (No evidence.)
3. **Test four arguments (15 min).** Listen to each argument twice. On your chain page,
   fill the three links. If a link is missing or the evidence does not fit the claim,
   draw the link broken and write one word for the gap (no reason / no evidence /
   off-topic).
4. **Name the gap (10 min).** With a partner, say each gap aloud: "The claim has a
   reason, but no evidence," or "The evidence is about something else." Which gap was
   hardest to spot?
5. **Close (5 min).** You now check the *connections*, not just the pieces. Tomorrow you
   will turn the gaps you find into **probing questions**.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the chain page with the claim pre-filled; learners add one reason
  or mark "missing" and "off-topic" from a checklist.
- **Extension:** Write one new argument with a *deliberately* off-topic evidence link and
  ask a partner to find it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner fill all three links for a strong argument
  and correctly name the broken link for a weak one?
- **Peer-check:** Partners exchange chain pages and check that each gap is named with the
  right word (no reason / no evidence / off-topic).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to give you one reason for something they believe, then gently ask,
"What is the evidence?" — and notice whether the chain holds or breaks.
