# Lesson 2 — Looking Back on My Decisions to Choose Better

## Summary

Learners **retrieve** the four-step decision loop from Unit 1 and **deepen** it: instead of reflecting
on one choice, they look back across the whole year at several small decisions, **find a pattern**
among them, and turn what they learned into one concrete **better next choice** to carry into Grade 8.
The result becomes the first half of the year-end decision-and-routine note.

## Objectives

- Reflect on a past decision and its consequences to make a better choice next time.
  _(D01.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

One choice tells you a little; a hundred choices tell you a lot. You lent a tool you needed, spoke
sharply and watched a face close, stayed up too late before a big day. Alone, each is just a moment.
Together they form a pattern — a habit of choosing — and the pattern is what you can actually change.
Today you do not just read one ripple; you read the whole year's current, and steer.

## Materials

- Decision-loop poster (revisited)
- Year-of-decisions page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the revisited decision-loop poster (four steps with the "across the year" ring).
- Copy a year-of-decisions page for each learner.
- Retrieval: from Unit 1, recall the four steps — decision, consequence, reflection, better next
  choice. This lesson runs the loop over a *year*, not a single choice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **reflection is a learnable loop —
decision → consequence → reflection → better next choice — and it is most powerful when it runs over
a *pattern* of choices, not one isolated mistake.** This deepens Unit 1, Lesson 9, which taught the
loop on a single decision; here learners scan the year for a *habit of choosing* and convert it into a
next step they will carry forward. The goal is never to shame the past but to extract its lesson; a
past decision is information, never a verdict. Teach the loop as a worked example before independent
practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006, S-011). The ethics lens asks what a pattern of choices
did to others; the egalitarian lens asks whose needs it counted and whose it left out — and notices
that some learners carry heavier constraints on their choices (work, care, travel) that are not
personal failures. The global lens: reflecting on choices and adjusting course is a human practice
everywhere, not one culture's habit. Let learners choose **small, safe, recent** decisions and offer a
made-up example as an option; never require disclosure of painful material. Feedback is built in as a
peer check that the "next time" step actually follows from the pattern found. The year-spanning
retrieval is deliberate spacing (Dunlosky et al., 2013, S-039). Distinguish **evidence from values**:
that reflection improves later judgment is supported by learning science; that a learner's past is
never a verdict is a value this curriculum holds — name it as one.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (8 min).** You learned this loop in Unit 1. Quick retrieval: name the four
   steps — **decision**, **consequence**, **reflection**, **better next choice**. Now you will run the
   same loop over a whole year.
2. **Meet the revisited loop (10 min).** Look at the poster: the same four steps, wrapped in a ring
   that says **"across the year, one choice at a time."** Worked example: *This year I rushed into
   group work without listening — twice it caused a clash, once I did all the work. Reflection: my
   pattern is acting before hearing others. Better next choice: I will ask one question before I
   answer, starting next term.* Notice the loop found a **pattern**, not just one event.
3. **List your year of decisions (12 min).** On your page, list three or four small decisions from the
   year — not your heaviest ones, just ordinary ones (how you spent time, how you answered someone,
   when you prepared or did not). For each, one line: what I chose, and what happened.
4. **Find the pattern (10 min).** Look across your list. Do the choices rhyme? (Do I rush? Do I avoid?
   Do I choose comfort now and pay later?) Write the pattern in one sentence, then write one concrete
   **better next choice** — "I will ___," not "I should try to ___."
5. **Peer check and close (10 min).** Swap with a partner. Does your "next choice" actually follow from
   the pattern you named? Is it concrete? Offer one improvement and take one. Close: **you are not stuck
   with your pattern — noticing it is the first step to choosing differently.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each step ("I chose ___, what happened was ___, the
  pattern I notice is ___, next time I will ___") and a partially filled list to copy first.
- **Extension:** Add a fifth question to one decision: whose needs did my choice count, and whose did it
  leave out? Then note whether the pattern would change if you counted those needs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a pattern across several decisions and produce a
  concrete "better next choice" that follows from it?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The year-of-decisions page, kept as the first half of the unit's
  decision-and-routine note.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a choice they used to make repeatedly and how they changed it — and share the
pattern you noticed and the next choice you named.
