# Lesson 4 — When Our Community Changes — Hard and Good, and What We Carry

## Summary

Learners **retrieve** "when a community changes" from Unit 3 and **deepen** it by linking the
community's change to their own: they name what a community lost and gained, reflect on **how they
belong to it now**, and name what they hope the community carries forward. The result becomes the
opening piece of the year-end belonging-and-common-good reflection.

## Objectives

- Explain how communities change over time and why change can be both hard and good.
  _(D02.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

No community stands still — a village gets a road and the market grows, but the quiet field where
children played is gone; new families arrive with new languages and foods, and the neighborhood is
richer, but the people who were there first may feel crowded or unseen. And you change with it: the
self you wrote about on day one is not separate from the place you live in. A coast, a mountain town,
a city block, a school — everywhere, the "me" and the "we" change together, hard *and* good, in the
same breath.

## Materials

- Me-and-we change poster
- Belonging reflection page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the me-and-we change poster (a "me" circle inside a "we" circle, a change arrow
  through both).
- Copy a belonging reflection page for each learner.
- Retrieval: from Unit 3, recall the three-panel story of a community changing and the "both/and"
  idea — change brings loss and gain at once, and lands differently on different people.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **communities change over time, and
change is both hard and good; the self changes *inside* that community change, so belonging is not a
fixed seat but something we keep re-finding.** This deepens Unit 3, Lesson 9 (which named loss and
gain in a community change) by connecting it to the "who I am becoming" work of this unit and adding
the question "what do we carry forward?" Keep the examples global and ordinary (a road, a market,
migration, a new building), never one region's "default" and never a story that ranks one culture above
another. Guard the egalitarian lens: the same change often helps some and burdens others, and naming
who carries the cost is part of caring — without making anyone a villain; also notice how belonging
can include some and exclude others at once. The environment lens: many community changes are changes
to the land and water people share. The technology lens: new tools and roads and phones change a
community's shape and pace. Invite learners to name a change from their own community only if they
wish; no one is required to share. Model one loss-and-gain pair as a worked example (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006, S-011). Note that grief for what is lost is real and legitimate — it is not
"resisting progress." The retrieval here is spaced recall (Dunlosky et al., 2013, S-039); the
both/and stance follows RULER's mixed-feelings framing (S-015).

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (8 min).** In Unit 3 you asked how a community changes. Quick retrieval: name
   one thing a community can lose when it changes, and one thing it can gain — and hold both. Today
   you add your own place inside that change.
2. **Meet the me-and-we poster (10 min).** Look at the poster: a small **"me"** circle inside a large
   **"we"** circle, with a change arrow through both. Worked example: *Our town got a new market road.
   Lost: the quiet field where we played. Gained: the clinic is reachable and new foods arrived. I
   changed too: I walk a different way to school and I know new neighbors — and I miss the field.* The
   self and the community change together.
3. **Name the loss and the gain (12 min).** On your page, choose one community change (real or
   invented) and fill two columns: **what changed in our community** and **how I belong to it now**.
   Name at least one loss and one gain in the change.
4. **Who feels it most — and what we carry (10 min).** Share with a partner. Then ask: does this change
   help everyone equally? Often it helps some and burdens others. Finally, write one line: **what I hope
   our community carries forward** — one thing worth keeping through the change.
5. **Close (10 min).** Change is not merely to accept or merely to resist; it is to understand — what is
   lost, what is gained, who carries each, and what we keep. Tomorrow you turn to how people care for one
   another through it, and you celebrate all of it together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-chosen change with one loss and one gain already filled; ask the learner
  to add how they belong to it now.
- **Extension:** Write one paragraph arguing *for* the change and one arguing *for what was lost*, then
  one sentence holding both together — and one naming how you belong through it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a loss and a gain from the same community change,
  say how they belong to it now, and name one thing worth carrying forward?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The belonging reflection page, kept as the opening piece of the unit's
  belonging-and-common-good reflection.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what the neighborhood or village was like when they were young — listen for one
thing they miss, one thing they are glad changed, and one thing they hope is never lost.
