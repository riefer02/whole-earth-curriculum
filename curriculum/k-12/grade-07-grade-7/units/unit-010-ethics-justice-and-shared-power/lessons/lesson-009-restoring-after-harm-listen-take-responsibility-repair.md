# Lesson 9 — Restoring After Harm: Listen, Take Responsibility, Repair

## Summary

Learners learn three **restorative steps — listen, take responsibility, repair** — and
apply them to an **invented, role-played** conflict. They practice with a **talking piece**
in a circle and use three guiding questions: *what happened, who was harmed, how do we make
it right*. No learner is asked to disclose real harm.

## Objectives

- Apply restorative steps — listening, taking responsibility, and repairing harm — to a
  real or role-played conflict. _(D09.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

When something is broken — a plate, a promise, a friendship — the question is what to do
next. One answer is to blame and punish. Another is to **restore**: to hear everyone, to
have the person who caused harm take responsibility, and to repair what was damaged. The
second way does not ignore harm; it faces it and works to make things as right as they can
be. Today you learn its steps — so that when harm happens around you, you have a path that
is not just punishment.

## Materials

- Restorative steps card
- Role-play scenarios

## Preparation

- Copy the steps card and scenario cards.
- Retrieval: recall the three questions of the moral framework (helped, harmed, fair).
  Restorative practice is the three questions turned into *action* after harm.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) restorative practice
responds to harm by repairing it and restoring relationships, not only by punishing; (2) the
three steps are: listen (everyone affected is heard), take responsibility (the person who
caused harm owns it), repair (together, make things as right as possible); (3) three guiding
questions: what happened? who was harmed, and how? how do we make it right? — these are the
well-known restorative questions (International Institute for Restorative Practices, S-022).**
Model the steps once (worked example, S-011), then let groups role-play. **Emotional safety
is non-negotiable: use only invented, low-stakes conflicts; never ask a learner to disclose
real harm or act out something that happened to them. If a learner shares real hurt, honor it
calmly and gently redirect to the invented scenario, and follow your setting's safeguarding
practice.** The ethics lens: restoration takes harm seriously without reducing a person to
their worst act. The egalitarian lens: a circle gives everyone — the harmed, the one who
harmed, and the community — a voice, rather than letting the loudest or most powerful decide.
The social-emotional lens: listening and taking responsibility are emotional skills, and the
circle holds them safely. The community lens: repair restores the group, not just the two
people. The multiple-traditions note (philosophy §11): the circle and the talking piece draw
on long-standing Indigenous and community traditions of restoring justice — among them Māori
restorative practice in Aotearoa New Zealand, First Nations peacemaking and sentencing circles
in North America, and Ubuntu-rooted reconciliation in southern Africa; the IIRP questions are
one modern expression of these older ways, held side by side rather than ranked. Emphasize
that repair is often imperfect — "as right as possible," not "like it never happened."

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the three questions of the moral framework? Today we turn
   them into a way to respond *after* harm.
2. **Meet the three steps (10 min).** Look at the steps card. **Listen** — everyone affected
   gets to speak and be heard. **Take responsibility** — the person who caused harm says what
   they did and owns it, without excuses. **Repair** — together, decide how to make things as
   right as possible.
3. **Meet the three questions (5 min).** The steps are guided by three questions: **What
   happened? Who was harmed, and how? How do we make it right?**
4. **Model it (10 min).** Watch a scenario be worked: *One person breaks a shared item and
   hides it.* Listen: both speak. Take responsibility: "I broke it, and I hid it — that was
   wrong." Repair: together they agree how to fix or replace it and how to rebuild trust.
   Notice: no one is shamed, and the harm is not ignored.
5. **Role-play (15 min).** In a small group, choose an *invented* scenario. Pass the talking
   piece so each person speaks in turn. Run all three questions. The goal is practice, not a
   perfect ending.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one thing the steps made possible. Restoration does not undo
   harm; it makes a way forward.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only the listening turn with the talking piece before attempting all
  three steps; give a sentence starter for responsibility ("I did ___, and I am sorry for the
  harm it caused").
- **Extension:** Identify a step that was skipped in a scenario and write what including that
  step would have changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three steps and the three questions,
  and use them in a role-play?
- **Performance:** The role-played restorative conversation, observed for the presence of
  listening, taking responsibility, and repairing.

## Home connection

Ask at home how your family handles it when something gets broken or feelings get hurt —
do people talk it through, and does someone make it right? Compare with the three steps.

## Resources

- The three restorative questions (what happened? who was harmed and how? how do we make it
  right?) follow the restorative-practices framework of the International Institute for
  Restorative Practices: <https://www.iirp.edu/> (S-022).
- The circle and the talking piece have deep roots in Indigenous and other long-standing
  traditions of restoring justice — among them Māori restorative practice (Aotearoa New
  Zealand), First Nations peacemaking and sentencing circles (North America), and
  Ubuntu-rooted reconciliation (southern Africa). The IIRP questions are one modern expression
  of these older ways, offered side by side rather than ranked (philosophy §11).
- On explicit instruction and worked examples: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
