# Lesson 1 — Three Questions: Helped, Harmed, Fair

## Summary

Learners meet a simple **framework** for moral questions — three questions to ask: **who
is helped, who is harmed, and what is fair**. They watch it modeled step by step on one
situation, then apply it with a partner to a second situation.

## Objectives

- Apply a simple framework — who is helped, who is harmed, and what is fair — to a moral
  question. _(D09.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Every day you face small choices that are also questions about right and wrong: who gets
the last of the water, whether to tell a friend something hard, whether to keep a promise
that has become costly. None of these comes with a label saying "right" or "wrong." A
framework is just a set of questions you can ask yourself to think more clearly. Today you
learn three of them.

## Materials

- Three-questions framework card
- Scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the framework card and scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall the difference between a **fact** (what is), a **belief**
  (what someone holds to be true), and a **value** (what someone thinks is right or good).
  Today's questions are about values — but we reason about them with the same care we use
  for facts.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a moral question is
a question about right and wrong, and a framework is a set of questions that helps you think;
(2) the three questions are: who is helped, who is harmed, and what is fair; (3) reasoning
means asking all three, not just one.** Model the framework once, out loud and step by step
(a worked example — see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011), before learners try it
themselves. The ethics lens: these questions make consequences and fairness visible instead
of leaving them as a gut feeling. The egalitarian lens: asking "who is helped and who is
harmed" pushes us to notice people we might otherwise forget — the ones with less power or
less voice. The global lens: the framework is deliberately simple and portable; it works on
a village water question, a classroom dispute, or a market decision anywhere. The
critical-thinking lens: the questions are held as questions — the framework does not hand
out answers, it disciplines the asking. Do not present any single answer to "what is fair"
as the correct one; the point is the reasoning, not the conclusion.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Think back to Grade 6: what is the difference between a
   fact, a belief, and a value? Say it to a partner. Today we work with values — questions
   of right and wrong — and we learn a tool for them.
2. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** Look at the framework card. A **moral question**
   is a question about right and wrong. When you meet one, ask three questions:
   **Who is helped? Who is harmed? What is fair?** Notice you ask all three, not just the
   one you like.
3. **Watch it modeled (10 min).** Here is a situation: *A group has one ball for a break
   time, and one person keeps it the whole time.* Run it through the three questions out
   loud: who is helped? (The one person, for now.) Who is harmed? (Everyone else, who
   cannot play; and maybe the friendship.) What is fair? (A turn system, sharing, or
   deciding together — there is more than one fair answer.) Notice the questions made the
   situation thinkable.
4. **Try it with a partner (15 min).** Choose a scenario card. Read it, then together
   answer the three questions — one sentence each. If you disagree about "fair," that is
   good: say *why* you think so.
5. **Close (10 min).** Share one finding with the group. Remember: the framework is not a
   machine that gives one answer. It is a way to *see* the choice more fully.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a two-question starter (helped, harmed) before adding "fair"; give
  sentence starters ("This helps ___ because ___").
- **Extension:** Take one scenario and compare two different answers to "what is fair,"
  then say which you find stronger and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three questions and apply each to
  a scenario with a concrete answer (who, who, what)?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed framework card for one scenario, kept as the first
  piece of the unit's moral-reasoning portfolio.

## Home connection

Bring one small question home: ask someone in your household what *they* think is fair in a
shared-resource situation (who does the dishes, how a ride is shared) — and whether they
ask who is helped and who is harmed.
