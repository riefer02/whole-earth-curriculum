# Lesson 10 — Power, Hurt, and Conflict Without Violence

## Summary

Learners analyze how **power and hurt** can make conflict worse, and how to address them
**without violence** — naming feelings, balancing power, meeting needs, and repairing harm.
The unit closes by running one **capstone scenario** through the whole arc: the moral
framework, justice, influence, and restorative practice.

## Objectives

- Explain how power and hurt can make conflict worse, and how to address them without
  violence. _(D09.S4.07.02)_
- Apply restorative steps — listening, taking responsibility, and repairing harm — to a
  role-played conflict. _(D09.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

Most conflicts do not start violent; they *become* worse when two things are present:
**power** and **hurt**. When one side has much more power — bigger, older, richer, more
numerous, in authority — the conflict stops being fair. And when people are **hurt** — from
the past, from fear, from being unheard — small sparks can grow. Naming these two things is
the first step to answering conflict without violence. Today you learn how, and you bring
the whole unit together.

## Materials

- Escalation-and-response chart
- Capstone scenario

## Preparation

- Copy the chart and capstone scenario.
- Retrieval: recall the three questions (helped, harmed, fair), the restorative steps
  (listen, take responsibility, repair), and the four methods of influence. Today all of them
  meet in one place: conflict.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) two things make
conflict worse: power — when one side holds much more, the conflict stops being fair — and
hurt — past injury, fear, and feeling unheard, which fuel escalation; (2) conflict without
violence means naming feelings, balancing power (bringing in a fair third party or a circle
so everyone is heard), meeting needs, and repairing harm; (3) these are skills, not magic —
they take practice.** Model the chart once (worked example, S-011), then run the capstone.
**Emotional safety: the capstone scenario is invented; do not ask learners to disclose or
re-enact real conflict, and never stage a fight — the "conflict" is described and discussed,
not acted out physically.** The ethics lens: nonviolence is not weakness; it is a demanding
way of facing conflict that respects the dignity of everyone, including the one who caused
harm. The egalitarian lens: balancing power is the heart of the matter — fairness in conflict
requires that the less powerful be heard. The social-emotional lens: hurt is real and must be
named before it can be addressed; the "I" statement (Rosenberg, S-148) is one tool. The
multiple-traditions note (philosophy §11): nonviolence has deep roots in many world traditions
— the principle of ahimsa (non-harming) in Jain, Buddhist, and Hindu thought, and Gandhi's
satyagraha in India's freedom movement, alongside many others — and the "I" statement is one
modern tool within that wider stream, with no single tradition as the default. The
critical-thinking lens: ask *whose* power and *whose* hurt is in the room — and whether a
proposed response makes it worse or better. End on hope and agency: nonviolence is something
learners can practice today, not a distant ideal.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Name, from memory: the three questions; the three restorative steps;
   the four methods of influence. Today they meet in conflict.
2. **Meet power and hurt (10 min).** Two things make conflict worse. **Power**: when one
   side holds much more, the conflict stops being fair. **Hurt**: past injury, fear, and
   feeling unheard, which turn small sparks into flames. When both are present, conflict
   escalates.
3. **Meet the nonviolent response (10 min).** Look at the chart. To answer conflict without
   violence: **name the feelings** ("I feel ___ when ___", an "I" statement); **balance the
   power** (bring in a fair third party, or sit in a circle where everyone is heard); **meet
   the needs** behind the anger; and **repair the harm** (the three restorative steps).
4. **Run the capstone (20 min).** Read the invented scenario: *A group project is due. One
   member, the oldest, takes over every task and speaks over the others, saying their ideas
   are best. A quieter member, who was left out before, stops speaking entirely and grows
   resentful.* With your group: (a) name who has power and who is hurt; (b) say what would
   make it worse; (c) role-play a nonviolent path — name feelings, balance power, meet needs,
   repair. Use the talking piece.
5. **Close (5 min).** One line each: what did this unit teach you that you will actually
   use? Nonviolence is not the absence of conflict — it is a better way through it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two escalators (power, hurt) and the four responses pre-listed;
   learners match them to the scenario.
- **Extension:** Write a short plan that *balances power* in the scenario (a fair third
   party, a circle, a turn rule) and explain how it changes the outcome.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name power and hurt as escalators and give at
  least two nonviolent responses?
- **Summative (performance):** The capstone run-through, showing the unit's skills — the
  framework, justice analysis, and restorative steps — in one performance.
- **Self-assessment:** Learners self-place on the mastery checklist for the unit's
  objectives, marking "not yet / with help / on my own / can teach."

## Home connection

Ask at home: "When our family has a disagreement, does one person usually have more power?
How do we make sure everyone is heard?" Try one nonviolent response this week.
