# Lesson 2 — Using the Three Questions

## Summary

Learners apply the **three questions — who is helped, who is harmed, what is fair** — to
everyday dilemmas, and write a short **moral-question analysis** with a reason. They
practice holding more than one fair answer at once, which is what real reasoning about
values requires.

## Objectives

- Apply a simple framework — who is helped, who is harmed, and what is fair — to a moral
  question. _(D09.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Thinking clearly about right and wrong is a skill, and like any skill it grows with
practice. Yesterday you met the three questions; today you actually use them — the way a
person uses a map by walking with it, not by staring at it. The goal is not to find the one
"correct" answer, but to be able to say, calmly and with reasons, *here is what I think is
right and here is why* — a skill you will use in a family argument, a job, and a vote.

## Materials

- Moral-question worksheet
- Scenario set

## Preparation

- Copy the worksheet and scenario set.
- Retrieval: recall the three questions from Lesson 1 (helped, harmed, fair) without
  looking — then check against the card.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **applying the framework
means answering all three questions in writing, then giving a reason for your answer — and
it is normal, not failure, to find more than one fair answer.** Model the full worksheet
once on the board before independent work (worked example, S-011), then use guided
practice: do one scenario together, let groups do the rest, and give feedback in the moment.
The ethics lens: a reason matters as much as an answer; "because it feels right" is a start,
not a finish. The egalitarian lens: some dilemmas have a voice that is easy to forget (the
person who owns the borrowed item, the quiet member of the group) — check whether you heard
them. The global lens: the scenarios are deliberately set in everyday, low-cost settings
(water, borrowing, group work, a secret) so they travel across city and village, coast and
mountain. The critical-thinking lens: when learners disagree on "fair," do not arbitrate to
one right answer; ask each to give their reason, and let the disagreement stand as evidence
that fairness is reasoned, not obvious. Keep the written work; it is a portfolio artifact.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Without looking: what are the three questions? Say them, then check.
2. **Model it (10 min).** Watch the worksheet be completed for one scenario: *A neighbor
   borrowed a tool and returned it broken without saying anything.* Who is helped? (No one,
   in the long run — the borrower avoided an awkward moment.) Who is harmed? (The owner, who
   must fix or replace it, and the trust between them.) What is fair? (The borrower says
   what happened and helps repair or replace it.) "My answer and why": the borrower should
   own it and help fix it, because trust is worth more than avoiding embarrassment.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a group, choose a scenario. Answer all three
   questions in writing. If your group has two answers to "fair," write both and a reason
   for each.
4. **Independent work (15 min).** Complete a second worksheet on your own, or with one
   partner. Self-check: did I answer all three questions? Did I give a *reason*, not just an
   answer?
5. **Close (5 min).** Trade worksheets with a partner and read each other's reasons. Ask one
   question about theirs. Remember: more than one answer can be fair — what matters is the
   reasoning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("This is fair because ___"; "This helps ___ and
  harms ___") and a two-scenario limit.
- **Extension:** Name the **trade-off** in the dilemma (what you give up to be fair) and say
  which value you weighed heavier and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the written analysis answer all three questions and
  include a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed moral-question worksheet, kept in the portfolio.
- **Self-assessment:** Learners self-check against the mastery checklist — "Could I apply
  the three questions alone, and explain them to someone else?"

## Home connection

Take the framework home and apply it aloud to one small family decision this week. Ask
someone else their three answers, and compare reasons.
