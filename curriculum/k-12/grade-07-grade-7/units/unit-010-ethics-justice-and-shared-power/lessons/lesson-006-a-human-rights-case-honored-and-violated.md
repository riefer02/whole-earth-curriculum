# Lesson 6 — A Human-Rights Case: Honored and Violated

## Summary

Learners analyze the **right to vote** as a human-rights case: where it was **violated**
for most people through most of history, where and when it was **honored**, and what made
the difference. They see that the same right can be honored in one place and still denied in
another, and produce the unit's human-rights case study.

## Objectives

- Explain what human rights are and analyze a case where they were honored or violated.
  _(D09.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

A right can exist on paper and still be denied in life. For most of history, most adults —
women everywhere, and many men without property — could not vote, even where "the people"
were said to rule. The right to vote was won country by country, at very different times:
New Zealand led in 1893, Switzerland followed only in 1971, and in Saudi Arabia women first
voted in 2015. The same right, honored in one place and one time, violated in another. Today
you analyze how that looks up close.

## Materials

- Case study sheet
- Timeline strip

## Preparation

- Copy the case study sheet and timeline strip.
- Retrieval: recall from Lesson 5 what a human right is (a claim every person has by being
  human) and the difference between a right (should) and a fact (is). Today we watch one
  right move from "should" toward "is" — unevenly.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) the right to vote is
a human right — the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 21) says everyone has the
right to take part in their country's government, directly or through freely chosen
representatives, with equal voting; (2) for most of history this right was violated for most
people — women were excluded nearly everywhere, and many men too; (3) the right was honored
at different times in different places — New Zealand 1893 (the first self-governing country
to grant women the national vote), Switzerland 1971, Saudi Arabia 2015 — so "honored" and
"violated" can coexist in the world at the same moment; (4) analyzing the case means naming
the right, the violation, the honoring, and what made the difference (movements, changing
ideas, and law).** Cite the UDHR (S-149) for the right itself; the historical dates are
documented in Encyclopaedia Britannica ("woman suffrage") and are stated with a "for example"
hedge. The ethics lens: exclusion from voting is a moral wrong because it treats some people
as less than full members of the community. The egalitarian lens: this is a story about who
was counted as "the people" and who was left out — the heart of egalitarianism. The global
lens: the case spans many world regions and shows no single country as the default or the
hero; the vote arrived at different times everywhere. The critical-thinking lens: the right
being honored in law does not by itself mean every person can exercise it freely — ask what
still stands in the way. Handle with care and end on agency: these rights were won by
ordinary people organizing.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What is a human right? What is the difference between a right and a
   fact? Say both to a partner.
2. **Meet the right (5 min).** The **right to vote** is a human right: every person should
   be able to take part in deciding who governs them. Its being *declared* a right is not
   the same as its being *enjoyed*.
3. **Analyze the case (15 min).** On the case sheet, name: **the right** (to vote, and to
   take part in government); **where it was violated** (for most of history, women were
   excluded nearly everywhere, and many men without property too); **where it was honored**
   (New Zealand, 1893 — the first self-governing country to give women the national vote;
   then others, slowly: Switzerland in 1971, Saudi Arabia in 2015); and **what made the
   difference** (people organized, argued, and protested; ideas changed; and finally law
   changed).
4. **Read the timeline (10 min).** With a group, place the dates on the timeline strip.
   Notice: the same right arrived decades apart in different places — honored here, still
   violated there, at the very same moment.
5. **Close (10 min).** Answer in writing: *what does this case show about human rights?*
   One honest answer: rights are won, not automatic, and the winning is uneven — which is
   why people keep working for them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the right, the violation, and the honoring pre-stated; learners match
  them to the analyzer and add one "what made the difference."
- **Extension:** Compare the right to vote with another right (education or safety), naming
  one place or time each was honored and one where it is still denied.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio):** Does the case study name the right, a violation, an honoring,
  and at least one thing that made the difference?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed human-rights case study, kept in the portfolio.
- **Self-assessment:** Learners check against the question "Can I explain what a human right
  is and use a case to show it honored and violated?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home when people in your country or community first got the right to vote —
and whether everyone actually could. Compare with the timeline you studied.
