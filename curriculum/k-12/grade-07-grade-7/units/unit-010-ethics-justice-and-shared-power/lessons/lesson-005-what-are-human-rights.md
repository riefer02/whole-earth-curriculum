# Lesson 5 — What Are Human Rights?

## Summary

Learners learn what **human rights** are — claims every person has simply by being human,
grounded in equal **dignity** — through the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)**
and the **Convention on the Rights of the Child**. They practice distinguishing a right (what
*should* be) from a fact (whether it is honored).

## Objectives

- Explain what human rights are and analyze a case where they were honored or violated.
  _(D09.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

Some things are not privileges you earn or gifts you are given — they are claims that
belong to you simply because you are human: enough food, clean water, safety, a chance to
learn, a voice that is heard. When you are too hot, tired, or hungry, you can feel that
these are not extras but needs. Human rights are the world's agreed way of saying: these
needs are not favors; every person is owed them. Today you learn what that means and where
the idea comes from.

## Materials

- Human-rights wheel
- UDHR excerpt cards

## Preparation

- Copy the wheel and excerpt cards.
- Retrieval: recall the three columns from Lesson 4 (affected, benefiting, more just).
  Human rights are one answer to "what would be more just" — a shared standard for everyone.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a human right is a
claim that belongs to every person simply because they are human — it does not have to be
earned and cannot be taken away in principle; (2) rights rest on equal dignity: every person
is born with worth; (3) the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the United
Nations in 1948 after a devastating war, as a shared promise to the world; (4) a right is a
"should" — saying everyone *has* a right is not the same as saying everyone already *enjoys*
it.** Distinguish "should" (value/aspiration) from "is" (fact) explicitly and repeatedly —
this is the unit's evidence-vs-values spine. The ethics lens: rights are a moral standard we
hold ourselves and others to. The egalitarian lens: rights are *universal* — they do not
depend on wealth, birthplace, or power. The global lens: the UDHR was written by people from
many countries (and has been translated into hundreds of languages), and it is one shared
agreement among many traditions, not the invention of one region. The critical-thinking
lens: the Declaration is a document humans wrote — question it, but also know what it says.
Cite the UDHR (S-149) and the Convention on the Rights of the Child (S-060). Do not quiz
learners on memorizing article numbers; the goal is the concept of universality and dignity.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what did "what would make it more just" mean? Today we
   meet a shared, worldwide standard for that question.
2. **Meet a right (10 min).** A **human right** is a claim that belongs to every person
   simply because they are human. It rests on **dignity** — the worth every person is born
   with. A right is a "should": it says what every person is owed, whether or not it is
   honored everywhere yet.
3. **Meet the documents (10 min).** In 1948, after a terrible war, countries came together
   under the United Nations and wrote the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** — a shared
   promise that every person has rights like life, dignity, food, water, health, education,
   a voice, and safety. For children there is also the **Convention on the Rights of the
   Child**, which adds rights to play, rest, and be heard.
4. **Read the excerpts (15 min).** With a group, read the UDHR excerpt cards. For each,
   answer: what need does this right protect? Is it a "should" (a claim about what is owed)
   or an "is" (a fact about what already happens everywhere)? Notice the difference.
5. **Close (10 min).** Add one right you care about to the wheel, in your own words. Rights
   are promises the world has made — and promises must still be kept.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each right to a picture of a need (water, a book, a bed, a speaking
  voice) before reading the words.
- **Extension:** Explain in writing the difference between a *right*, a *law*, and a
  *courtesy*, with one example of each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define a human right (a claim every person
  has by being human) and separate a right (should) from a fact (is)?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed human-rights wheel, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: "If you could make one promise that every person on Earth is owed, what would
it be?" Compare it with the rights on the wheel.
