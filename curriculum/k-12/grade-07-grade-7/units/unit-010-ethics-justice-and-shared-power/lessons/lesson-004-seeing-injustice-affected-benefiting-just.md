# Lesson 4 — Seeing Injustice: Affected, Benefiting, Just

## Summary

Learners analyze a case of **inequality — unequal access to clean water** — using three
columns: **who is affected, who benefits, and what would make it more just**. They then
apply the same analysis to a second case. This connects the moral framework of Lesson 1–2
to questions of justice in society.

## Objectives

- Analyze a case of inequality or injustice, identifying who is affected, who benefits, and
  what would make it more just. _(D09.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

Fairness is not only about one person's choice; it is also about how whole systems treat
groups of people. When one part of a town has clean water piped to the tap while another
part drinks from a stream that carries waste, that is not just a series of individual
choices — it is a pattern. Learning to see a pattern like that, and to name who is affected
and who benefits, is the first step toward changing it. Today you practice that kind of
seeing.

## Materials

- Case study sheet
- Case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the case study sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: recall the three questions (helped, harmed, fair). Today we extend "helped" and
  "harmed" to whole groups — who is *affected* and who *benefits* — and ask what would make
  the situation *just*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) inequality is a
pattern where some people get more of something important (or carry more of a burden) than
others; injustice is inequality that is also unfair; (2) to analyze a case you name who is
affected, who benefits, and what would make it more just; (3) "more just" can mean a one-time
fix or a change to a rule or system.** Model the water case once on the board (worked
example, S-011), then let groups work a second case. The ethics lens: analysis here is
descriptive first (who is affected, who benefits — these are observable) and normative
second (what would be more just — this is a value claim, and learners may differ). The
egalitarian lens: injustice usually falls hardest on those with the least power; notice whose
voice is missing. The environment lens: clean water is an environmental and a justice issue
at once. The critical-thinking lens: the fact (about 1 in 4 people globally still lack safely
managed drinking water — WHO/UNICEF JMP, S-065) is evidence; the claim that this *should*
change is a value — keep them distinct. The water case is a composite illustration, not a
named real community, so no real place is misrepresented. Handle with care and hope: end on
agency (what could be done), never despair.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the three questions? Now we ask them of a whole *situation*
   affecting many people, not just one choice.
2. **Meet the three columns (5 min).** To analyze inequality, ask: **Who is affected? Who
   benefits? What would make it more just?**
3. **Analyze the water case (15 min).** Read this case: *In a town, one neighborhood has
   clean water piped to every home. A poorer neighborhood down the hill has no pipes; people
   fetch water from a stream that also carries factory waste upstream. More people in the
   poor neighborhood get sick.* Fill the three columns: Who is affected? (The people of the
   poorer neighborhood — especially the sick, the old, and those who spend hours carrying
   water.) Who benefits? (The factory, which disposes of waste cheaply; and the piped
   neighborhood, which stays clean.) What would make it more just? (Stop the waste, bring
   clean water to everyone, make the factory pay for the harm, and include the affected
   people in the decision.)
4. **Apply it (15 min).** With a group, take a second case card. Complete the three columns.
   If you propose a rule or system change as well as a fix, say which and why.
5. **Close (10 min).** Share one "more just" idea. Notice: analysis does not by itself fix
   anything — but you cannot fix what you cannot see.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the affected/benefiting groups pre-listed and ask learners to match
  them to the columns, then add one "more just" idea.
- **Extension:** Propose a **structural** change (a rule, a tax, a new way of deciding) and
  compare its likely effect with a one-time fix.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name who is affected, who benefits, and at
  least one "more just" change, with a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed case analysis, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Look around your own neighborhood or town: name one thing that is not shared equally (water,
shade, transport, a place to play) and ask who is affected and who benefits.
