# Lesson 8 — Civil Society: Acting for the Common Good

## Summary

Learners learn what **civil society** is — the space of voluntary groups between individual
people and government — and how groups and movements act for the **common good** when
government does not. They study real movements from several world regions, including the
**Green Belt Movement** in Kenya and the **Chipko movement** in India.

## Objectives

- Explain what civil society is and how groups and movements act for the common good when
  government does not. _(D09.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Between you alone and the government, there is a whole world of people acting *together*:
a neighborhood that clears a shared path, a women's group that runs a savings circle, a
cooperative of farmers, a movement that plants trees. None of these is the government, and
none is a single person acting alone — they are **civil society**. Often they do what
government is slow, unwilling, or unable to do, and sometimes their work pushes government
to act. Today you meet this middle layer of shared life.

## Materials

- Civil-society diagram
- Movement cards

## Preparation

- Copy the diagram and movement cards.
- Retrieval: recall the four methods of influence from Lesson 7. Civil society is one of
  the deepest forms of **organizing** — people acting together, over time, without waiting
  for government.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) civil society is the
space of voluntary groups between the individual and government — community groups,
cooperatives, faith communities, unions, and social movements; (2) groups act for the common
good when government does not — they fill gaps, meet needs, and often press government to
act; (3) examples: the Green Belt Movement, founded in Kenya in 1977 by Wangari Maathai,
organized people to plant trees, and grew to plant over thirty million trees — work for
which Maathai received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004 (S-059); the Chipko movement in India,
beginning in the 1970s, saw villagers — especially women — embrace trees to stop them being
cut, protecting forests their communities depended on; and the Red Cross/Red Crescent
movement, which gives neutral humanitarian aid in crises.** Cite Maathai/Green Belt Movement
to S-059; Chipko and the Red Cross/Red Crescent are documented historical facts (Chipko is
well documented — S-328; the Red Cross/Red Crescent — S-329 — both stated with a "for example"
hedge). The ethics lens: the common good is the good we share — clean air, water, safety,
belonging — and acting for it is an ethical act. The egalitarian lens: civil society is often
how people with less power get a voice — it is the "voice of the many," not a favor from
above. The environment lens: both movements named are environmental — caring for land and
forests is a form of caring for people. The community lens: civil society is the curriculum's
own vision of cooperation over competition, made real in the world. Do not romanticize —
civil society can also be imperfect — but hold the examples with dignity and hope.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the four methods of influence? Which one is "building a
   lasting group"? Today we go deep into that one.
2. **Meet civil society (10 min).** Look at the diagram: at the bottom are **people**; at
   the top is **government**; in the middle is **civil society** — the voluntary groups
   people form for a shared purpose. They act for the **common good**: the good we all
   share, like clean air, water, safety, and belonging.
3. **Meet two movements (15 min).** Read the movement cards. The **Green Belt Movement**
   (Kenya, founded 1977 by Wangari Maathai) organized people to plant trees — over thirty
   million of them — repairing land and livelihoods; Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize in
   2004. The **Chipko movement** (India, 1970s) saw villagers embrace trees to stop them
   being cut, protecting the forests their communities depended on. In both, ordinary people
   acted together when government did not.
4. **Analyze (10 min).** With a group, answer for one movement: what was its *goal*? What
   *common good* did it serve? How did it act *without waiting for government*?
5. **Close (10 min).** Name one voluntary group you know of or can imagine, and the common
   good it serves. Civil society is not a distant idea — it is people, together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Match each movement to a single phrase (trees/forests, planting/embracing)
  before writing about it.
- **Extension:** Compare the two movements' methods and write why each method fit its
  situation (planting trees vs. physically protecting trees).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define civil society and explain how one
  movement acted for the common good without government?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed civil-society diagram and one movement analysis,
  kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: "What group in our community helps everyone, without being the government?"
Find out what common good it serves and how people join it.
