# Lesson 7 — Influencing Decisions: Voting, Protest, Petitions, Organizing

## Summary

Learners analyze four ways people influence decisions — **voting, protest, petitions, and
organizing** — and the **limits** of each. They see that no single method is complete and
that people often combine them, and they produce the unit's people-influence-decisions
analysis.

## Objectives

- Analyze how people influence decisions in their society — voting, protest, petitions,
  organizing — and the limits of each. _(D09.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

You influence decisions every day — which game the group plays, how a chore is divided —
but whole societies make decisions too, and ordinary people have ways to shape them. Each
way has real power, and each has limits. A vote gives many people a say, but it happens
only now and then and can leave a minority out. A protest is loud and visible, but it can be
ignored or met with force. A petition is easy to join, but easy to ignore. Organizing —
building a group that works together over time — is strong but slow. Knowing both the power
and the limit of each is how you choose well.

## Materials

- Methods-and-limits chart
- Example cards

## Preparation

- Copy the chart and example cards.
- Retrieval: recall the difference between "who benefits" and "who is affected" from Lesson
  4. Today: how people try to *change* who benefits.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) four common ways
people influence decisions are voting, protest, petitions, and organizing; (2) each has real
power and real limits; (3) limits are not reasons to give up — they are reasons to combine
methods and to think clearly.** Model one method's strength-and-limit on the board (worked
example, S-011), then let groups work the rest. The ethics lens: influencing decisions is
itself a moral act — how we influence matters as much as what we want. The egalitarian lens:
notice who *can* vote, who *can* safely protest, whose petition *gets read* — power shapes
access to every method. The global lens: these methods exist in many forms across many
countries, and no one country is the model; keep examples region-varied (a ballot in one
country, a protest in another, a petition in a third, a union or neighborhood group in a
fourth). The technology lens: these methods now also travel through digital tools — online
petitions, messaging that gathers a crowd, organizing across distance — which can widen who
gets heard and also let loud or false voices drown out the quiet; technology changes a
method's *reach*, not its *rightness*. The critical-thinking lens: for every method, ask
*who is left out* and *what it
cannot do* — that is the discipline of the "limits" column. Present these as descriptions of
how influence works, not as a ranking of political systems; do not teach any one method as
always right or always enough.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: who was affected and who benefited in the water case?
   Today: how people try to change such situations.
2. **Meet four methods (10 min).** **Voting** — choosing a representative or a decision by
   casting a vote. **Protest** — gathering and speaking out publicly. **Petition** — many
   people signing their names to demand something. **Organizing** — building a lasting group
   that works together over time.
3. **Model one (5 min).** Take **voting**: its *strength* is that many people at once get a
   say, and it can change who governs. Its *limit* is that it happens only sometimes, and a
   majority can leave a minority out; and not everyone is allowed to vote everywhere.
4. **Fill the chart (20 min).** With a group, complete the strength and the limit for each
   of the other three methods, using the example cards. For each, ask: *who is included, and
   who is left out?*
5. **Close (10 min).** Share one method and its limit. Notice that people rarely use just
   one — a movement may protest *and* petition *and* organize. The limits are not failures;
   they are the reason for combining.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the strengths and limits mixed up and have learners sort them into
  the right method's row.
- **Extension:** Explain how two methods combined (for example, protest + organizing) cover
  each other's limits, with one example.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the four methods and give one strength
  and one limit for each?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed methods-and-limits chart, kept as the unit's
  people-influence-decisions analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they tried to change a decision (at work, in a building, in
a community) — what method did they use, and what was its limit?

## Resources

- The four methods and their limits are a standard analytical framing in civic education,
  not a single factual finding; the "limits" column is a thinking tool, and the examples are
  illustrative. Specific historical cases of protest and organizing appear across the
  curriculum (e.g., the Salt March, the Green Belt Movement — see S-059) and are treated as
  documented facts where named.
