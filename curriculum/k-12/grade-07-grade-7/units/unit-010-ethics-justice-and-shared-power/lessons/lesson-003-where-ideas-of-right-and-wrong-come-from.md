# Lesson 3 — Where Our Ideas of Right and Wrong Come From

## Summary

Learners explore how **background, culture, and position** shape what people think is
right. Through paired cases where two people see the same act differently, they practice
the crucial distinction between **understanding** a view and **agreeing** with it, and they
map where their own ideas come from.

## Objectives

- Explain how someone's background, culture, or position can shape what they think is
  right. _(D09.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Two people can watch the same thing and reach different answers about what is right — and
each can be sincere. The neighbor who keeps a goat and the neighbor who grows vegetables
may see a shared fence very differently; the older and younger members of a family may
disagree about how loudly to celebrate; a guest and a host may each think they are being
polite while doing opposite things. Before you can reason *with* someone, it helps to see
*where their view is coming from*. That is today's work.

## Materials

- Perspective cases
- "Where my ideas come from" web

## Preparation

- Copy the perspective cases and the web.
- Retrieval: recall the three questions (helped, harmed, fair) — today we add a new layer:
  *why* people answer them differently.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) what a person thinks
is right is shaped by their background (how and where they grew up), their culture (the
shared ways of a people), and their position (their role — parent or child, owner or worker,
host or guest, powerful or powerless); (2) this shaping is not a flaw — it is simply true of
everyone, including us; (3) understanding a view is not the same as agreeing with it.** Hold
the difference between "understand" and "agree" carefully: learners may understand why
someone holds a view and still disagree with it, and that is legitimate. The ethics lens: we
reason about values more honestly when we know where they come from. The egalitarian lens:
position matters — people with more power and people with less often see "fair" differently,
and neither view is automatically right. The global lens: use examples from many places, and
invite learners' own practices without ranking any tradition above another (see philosophy
§11 — no one culture is the default). The critical-thinking lens: ask "where does that idea
come from?" about every view, including our own. Do not require any learner to disclose
personal or family information; the web may be completed with invented or general examples.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Name the three questions from the last two lessons. Now a new
   question: why might two people answer "what is fair?" differently?
2. **Meet the shapers (10 min).** Three things shape what a person thinks is right:
   **background** (how and where they grew up), **culture** (the shared ways of their
   people), and **position** (their role and how much power they have). Everyone's ideas
   come from somewhere — including yours.
3. **Read a case together (10 min).** *A family keeps a shop. The parent thinks the child
   should work in the shop after school to help the family; the child thinks evenings should
   be for study.* Whose view is shaped by what? (The parent's, by position and by the
   family's need; the child's, by position and by valuing education.) Understand both — you
   do not have to agree with either.
4. **Pairs (15 min).** Take a perspective case. For each person, answer: what is their
   **background, culture, or position**, and how does it shape what they think is right? Say
   aloud whether you *understand* their view and whether you *agree* with it — and keep the
   two separate.
5. **Map your own (10 min).** On the web, put "me" in the center and add spokes: family,
   culture, position, experience, belief. Write one idea of right and wrong you hold, and
   one source it may have come from. Share only what you wish.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a single case and two sentence starters ("___ thinks ___ because their
  ___ is ___"; "I understand this view when ___").
- **Extension:** Take one case and write two sentences: one that explains the view you
  disagree with, and one that explains why you still disagree — showing you can hold both.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name background, culture, or position as a
  source of a character's view, and distinguish "understand" from "agree"?
- **Self-assessment:** The completed web, checked against the question "Can I name one
  source of my own ideas of right and wrong?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What is one thing you think is right that you learned from your own
upbringing or people?" Listen without arguing, then tell them one of yours.

## Resources

- The claim that values vary across cultures and are shaped by background and position is a
  widely held anthropological and ethical observation, not a single factual finding; it is
  treated here as a framework for understanding, not a ranking of any tradition. The
  "understand vs. agree" distinction is a standard tool of civil discourse.
- On explicit instruction and worked examples: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1> (S-011).
