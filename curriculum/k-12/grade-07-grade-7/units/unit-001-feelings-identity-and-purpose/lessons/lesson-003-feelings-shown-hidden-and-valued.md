# Lesson 3 — Feelings Shown, Hidden, and Valued

## Summary

Learners discover that cultures — and families within them — differ in which feelings are
**shown**, **hidden**, or **valued**, and that these are learned **display rules**, not
universal facts. They sort feelings across the three columns, hear examples from several
traditions held **side by side and never ranked**, and name the norms of their own home or
community.

## Objectives

- Explain how cultural norms shape which feelings are shown, hidden, or valued. _(D01.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

In one home, grief is wailed loudly so the whole street can hear; in another, it is held
quietly behind a calm face. In one community, anger is spoken directly and cleared the same
day; in another, it is cooled in silence and repaired through actions later. In one place,
gratitude is said with many warm words; in another, it is shown by quietly returning the
favor. No one of these is the "right" way to feel or to show — they are different learned
habits, passed down like recipes or songs. Every family, village, and city teaches its own.

## Materials

- Shown/hidden/valued chart
- Feeling-rule cards
- Sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the shown/hidden/valued chart.
- Copy feeling-rule cards for each group and a sorting page for each learner.
- Prepare one worked example to sort together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: (1) **cultures teach
"display rules"** — learned norms about which feelings to show, hide, or value — and these
differ; (2) **norms differ *within* a culture too**, by family, region, and generation, so no
culture is a single monolith; and (3) **no norm is ranked above another** — this is the
unit's non-ranking, global-anthropological commitment (the display-rules concept traces to
Ekman & Friesen, 1969). Use the "some places / other places" phrasing, and let learners
always return to "it depends." Be careful never to teach one community's way as exotic or
superior. Model the sort as a worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Distinguish
**evidence from values**: *that* norms differ is a documented fact; *that* we should honor
every way without ranking is a value this curriculum holds — name it as one. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you named the mix precisely. Today a new question: when you
   feel something, who decides whether you show it, hide it, or hold it up as good? (Pause.)
   The answer often lives in the culture around you.
2. **Meet the three columns (10 min).** Look at the chart. **Shown** — feelings a community
   lets you express openly. **Hidden** — feelings it teaches you to hold in. **Valued** —
   feelings it praises, like courage or calm. These are **display rules**: learned, not born.
   Worked example: *grief.* In some communities people wail aloud together; in others they
   keep a still, dignified face. The feeling is real in both — the *rule* about showing it
   differs.
3. **Sort with a partner (15 min).** Take feeling-rule cards: joy at a win, anger at an
   injustice, fear before a risk, gratitude for a gift. For each, ask: in *my* community, is
   this shown, hidden, or valued? Compare with your partner — if your answers differ, you have
   found two norms living side by side.
4. **The big idea (10 min).** Discuss together: why do you think cultures teach different
   rules? (To keep harmony, to survive hardship, to honor elders, to protect the vulnerable.)
   Notice: every rule makes sense *inside* its own world — and no world's rule is "the normal
   one."
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, place three feelings your own family shows,
   hides, or values — one in each column — and write one sentence: *In my home, we ___, and
   that is just one way among many.*
6. **Close (5 min).** Feelings are universal; the rules about showing them are local. Learning
   both is how you read a room — and respect a neighbor.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank of feelings and a one-line description of each column;
  sort as a whole group first.
- **Extension:** Name one feeling your *family* shows that your *wider community* hides (or
  the reverse), and explain the difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place a feeling as shown, hidden, or valued for
  a given culture — and say "it depends on the culture" without ranking one way as correct?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name a norm for my own community *and* hold it as
  one way among many, not the only right way?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home which feelings your family shows openly and which it holds quietly — and
why. Notice together that other families do it differently.
