# Lesson 2 — The Precise Words for the Mix

## Summary

Learners climb from a **vague** feeling word ("weird," "off," "fine") toward the **precise**
language for a mixed feeling. They practice separating a mix into its two parts, choosing the
exact joining word (**and**, **but**, **because**), and naming a one-word precise term such as
**bittersweet** or **nostalgic** — learning that precise naming is what lets a feeling be
understood, shared, and worked with.

## Objectives

- Describe mixed or conflicting feelings in increasingly precise language — separating the
  parts, choosing a joining word, and reaching for exact terms. _(D01.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

"I feel weird." That sentence tells someone almost nothing, and it tells *you* almost
nothing. "I feel **excited and nervous** about starting a new school" tells you exactly what
is happening and what you might need. Precision is not fussiness; it is how a feeling becomes
usable — like the difference between saying "bad weather" and "rain with high wind, coming
from the sea." In any life, on a farm or in a city, the more exactly you can name it, the more
clearly you can respond to it.

## Materials

- Word-ladder poster
- Precise-words page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the word-ladder poster (vague at the bottom, precise at the top).
- Copy a precise-words page for each learner.
- Arrange pairs for the climbing practice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **precision is a skill that
can be taught and practiced**, and it has a shape — split the mix into its parts, choose the
joining word, then reach for the exact term. Model the ladder as a worked example before
independent practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Acknowledge that words for precise
feeling-states exist in every language and are not all translatable one-to-one — which is why
we hold languages side by side as wealth, never as "better or worse" (the Japanese "mono no
aware," the Portuguese "saudade," the German "Fernweh," the Hindi/Sanskrit "viraha" — the pang
of a loved one's absence — the Tagalog "kilig" — the giddy shiver of excitement — and the
Arabic "tarab" — feeling stirred to fullness by music — all name nuances English must work
harder to reach). Naming precisely is a value this curriculum holds (RULER's "Labeling"), but
*whether* to share a feeling aloud is always the learner's choice — never pressure disclosure.
See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you met the **both/and** — two feelings at once. Try to
   recall the word you learned for happy-and-sad together. (Pause.) Today you learn to name
   the mix with exact words.
2. **Climb the ladder (10 min).** Look at the poster. The bottom rung says **"weird"** — a
   vague word that hides more than it shows. Climb: "weird" might really be **"excited AND
   nervous."** Another rung up, **"proud AND lonely."** Higher still, one exact word:
   **bittersweet** — happy and sad in the same moment. At the top, **nostalgic** — warm AND
   sad about something past. Watch how each rung names more.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Take a vague word you have actually used: "off," "weird,"
   "fine." Split it: what are the **two feelings** hiding inside? Choose the joining word —
   **and** (both at once), **but** (they pull against each other), **because** (one caused the
   other). Say the full sentence: *I felt "weird" — really **nervous AND proud** about
   performing for the first time.*
4. **Climb your own (15 min).** On your page, write a vague word at the top, then climb three
   rungs toward precision: the two parts, the joining word, and (if you can) one exact term.
   Share your top rung with a partner and hear theirs.
5. **Close (5 min).** A feeling you can name precisely is a feeling you can carry, share, and
   use. Add your best precise pair to the class word wall.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter ladder with the first rung filled, and a short menu of exact
  terms (bittersweet, nostalgic, torn, ambivalent, relieved-but-sad) with one-line meanings.
- **Extension:** Distinguish two pairs for the same vague word — one joined by "and," one by
  "because" — and explain how the joining word changes the meaning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner move from a vague word to a two-part pair with
  a joining word, and (ideally) a precise one-word term?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name **two** parts, choose a **joining word**, and
  say the **same moment** for both?"

## Home connection

This week, when someone at home says "fine" or "weird," gently try climbing the ladder together
— "what are the two feelings inside that word?" — and see what you both find.
