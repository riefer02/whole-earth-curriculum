# Lesson 4 — No Culture Is the Default

## Summary

Learners examine the assumption that any one way of showing feeling is the "normal" one, and
replace it with the habit of holding norms **side by side** and never ranking them. They act
and compare two ways for the same feeling, name the **ethical and egalitarian** danger of
treating one's own way as the default, and extend the idea to the display rules of **digital**
spaces.

## Objectives

- Explain how cultural norms shape which feelings are shown, hidden, or valued — and why no
  one norm is the "default." _(D01.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

You meet someone whose way of feeling is different from yours: they grieve loudly where you
grieve quietly, or thank with gifts where you thank with words. It is easy, in that moment, to
quietly decide that *your* way is the normal one and *theirs* is odd. But "normal" is just
"what I am used to." A fish does not know it is wet. Your family's way is one way, floating in
a wide world of ways — in a fishing village by the sea or a farming town in the hills, every
home teaches its own, and none is the measuring stick for the rest.

## Materials

- Two-ways poster
- Scenario cards
- Both-are-real page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-ways poster (two ways for one feeling, with an "=" between them).
- Copy scenario cards for each group and a both-are-real page for each learner.
- Prepare one scenario to work together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **no culture's way of showing
feeling is the default or the measuring stick — norms are held side by side and never ranked.**
This is the unit's egalitarian commitment made explicit: the *ethics* lens asks what happens
when we forget it (we misread, exclude, and sometimes punish people whose ways differ), and
the *egalitarianism* lens asks who gets to decide what counts as "normal" (usually the
majority or the powerful). Extend honestly to **digital norms** — the *technology* lens: online
spaces teach their own display rules (emoji, "lol," muted reactions, read receipts, "likes" as
approval), and these differ across apps and communities, so a learner can now read a *digital*
culture the way they read a face-to-face one. Model the "whose way is normal?" question as a
worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Distinguish **evidence from values**: that
norms differ is a fact; that we must not rank them is a value this curriculum holds — name it
as one. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you sorted feelings into shown, hidden, and valued. Today the
   sharper question: whose way is "normal"? (Pause.) The honest answer is: no one's.
2. **Meet the two ways (10 min).** Look at the poster. One way: grief shared aloud, a whole
   street wailing together. Another way: grief held quietly, a still face, folded hands. Between
   them an **"="** — both are real, neither is the default. The feeling is the same; the
   *rule* about showing it differs.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Take a scenario: two people meet — one greets with a warm hug,
   the other with a bow and quiet words. Ask the question out loud: **"Whose way is normal?"**
   Answer it together: **both** — they are two learned ways, and the only mistake is deciding
   one is the measuring stick for the other.
4. **Act and compare (15 min).** In groups, take scenario cards and act each pair of ways, then
   place an "=" between them and say "both are real." Notice what the assumption of a default
   would cost: someone would be told their way is wrong.
5. **Digital norms (10 min).** Turn to screens. Online spaces teach display rules too: an emoji
   can be loud joy or polite cover; "lol" can hide hurt; a "like" can be approval or habit. Two
   apps, two communities, two sets of rules — and again, no default.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: "normal" is only "what I am used to." Hold every way side by
   side, and you stop mistaking your own home for the whole world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("One way is ___, another way is ___, and both are
  real because ___") and work one scenario as a whole group.
- **Extension:** Compare the display rules of two different online spaces you know, and name
  which feeling each one rewards and which it hides.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name two norms for the same feeling and say why
  neither is the default — without ranking them?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I hold both ways side by side, or did I quietly pick
  one as the 'real' one?"

## Home connection

Notice a moment this week when someone's way of showing feeling differed from yours — at home,
at a shop, in a message — and practice saying "two ways, both real" instead of "odd."
