# Lesson 10 — Building a Routine That Serves My Goals

## Summary

Learners build a personal daily routine — **study, sleep, movement, and rest** — that serves a
**goal they choose**, grounded in evidence about sleep and movement and in the unit's whole
arc. They draft their own 24 hours, add an **if-then plan** for the hard moments, set a way to
**track and adjust**, and close the unit by retrieving everything it taught.

## Objectives

- Build and follow a personal routine (study, sleep, movement) that supports their own goals.
  _(D01.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Big things are built of small, repeated things. A house is bricks laid one by one; a field is
rows planted one by one; a skill is practice returned to day after day. A routine is how your
goals stop being wishes and start being *days*. Study is how you grow your mind; sleep is how
the body repairs and the brain keeps what you learned; movement is how the whole system stays
strong and clear. Choose a goal, then build the day that feeds it — in a city apartment or a
village house, the pattern is the same.

## Materials

- Routine-wheel poster
- My-routine page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the routine-wheel poster (a 24-hour wheel of study, sleep, movement, meals,
  and rest).
- Copy a my-routine page for each learner.
- Prepare a short fact summary on sleep and movement amounts (see Resources).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a routine is how a goal
becomes days — it is built, not wished; (2) it rests on three evidence-backed blocks — study
(with breaks, spaced and retrieved), sleep (about 8–10 hours for teens, 9–12 for younger
children — AASM), and daily movement (WHO) — plus rest and meals; and (3) it must be *followed
and adjusted*, not just written.** Use if-then planning for the hard moments (Duckworth, Gendler
& Gross, 2016) and keep amounts flexible — every family, shift, and body differs, and a routine
that cannot bend will break. The *egalitarianism* lens is explicit: a learner with chores, care
duties, or long travel has different hours than one without, so teach the routine as "within
your real day," never as a middle-class default schedule. The *environment* lens: sleep and
movement are tied to daylight and season, and many people's days follow the sun and the land,
not a clock. Model one routine as a worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). This
lesson also closes the unit, so build in a brief **retrieval practice** pass across all four
strands (spacing/retrieval strengthen memory — Dunlosky et al., 2013). Distinguish **evidence
from values**: that sleep, movement, and spaced study support health and learning is documented;
that every learner's goals deserve equal honor is a value this curriculum holds — name it as
one. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have traveled the whole unit: mixed feelings, cultural norms, a
   changing identity, the labels that would narrow it, and the loop that learns from a
   decision. Now the last piece: a routine that turns your goals into days.
2. **Recall the unit (10 min).** Quick retrieval, no notes: name one mixed feeling and its
   precise words; name how cultures differ in which feelings are shown, hidden, or valued; name
   one way your identity has changed and one way it may keep growing; name how a label can
   narrow a person; and name the four steps of the decision loop. Check against a neighbor.
3. **Meet the wheel (10 min).** Look at the poster: a day divided into **study** (with breaks),
   **sleep** (about 8–10 hours), **movement** (daily), and **meals & rest**. Worked example:
   *My goal is to improve at drawing. My routine: 30 minutes of practice after school, a walk
   in the afternoon, screens off an hour before sleep, lights out by ten.* Each block serves
   the goal.
4. **Build your own (15 min).** On your page, write one goal. Then draft your real day —
   study, sleep, movement, meals, rest — using amounts that fit *your* life, not someone
   else's. Add one **if-then plan** for the hard moment: *If I feel like skipping practice,
   then I will do just five minutes.* And one way to **track** it (a tick chart, a note).
5. **Check and adjust (5 min).** Swap with a partner. Does every block serve the goal? Is the
   if-then concrete? Plan *how you will know* by the end of the week what to keep and what to
   adjust — a routine is a living thing, not a cage.
6. **Close (5 min).** Small, repeated actions, day after day, add up to the life you want. You
   are the author of that life. Start tomorrow.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a partly filled routine wheel and a menu of goals; write the if-then as
  a class first.
- **Extension:** Connect each block to *why* it serves your specific goal, and plan one
  adjustment you would make if a block fails this week.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner draft a routine with study, sleep, and movement
  that serves a named goal, plus an if-then plan and a way to track and adjust?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The my-routine page, kept for the unit's "personal routine built
  and followed" artifact.

## Home connection

Share your routine with someone at home and ask them to help you track it this week — then
adjust it together, not as a grade but as a living plan.
