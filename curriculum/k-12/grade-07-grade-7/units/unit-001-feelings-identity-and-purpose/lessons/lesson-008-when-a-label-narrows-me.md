# Lesson 8 — When a Label Narrows Me

## Summary

Learners explain how stereotypes and labels can **limit** a person's sense of self — by
narrowing what a person believes they may do, be, or become, and by getting "inside" as a
voice that says "not for people like you." They name who benefits and who is left out, connect
the narrowing to media's repeated images, and practice **pushing back**: redefining the label
as one small part of a much larger whole.

## Objectives

- Explain how stereotypes and labels can limit a person's sense of self. _(D01.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

A label is not just words. Tell a person often enough that they are "not the kind who" does
math, or leads, or is brave, and the label can move inside them — until they begin to say it
to themselves. Then it does real work: it shrinks the map of what they believe is possible.
The good news is the door is not locked. A label can *narrow* you, but it cannot *finish* you —
not if you learn to see it as one small box inside a much bigger life.

## Materials

- Narrowing-box poster
- Limit-and-push-back cards
- My-many-boxes page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the narrowing-box poster (a figure in a closing box with an open door).
- Copy limit-and-push-back cards for each group and a my-many-boxes page for each learner.
- Prepare one scenario to work together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a stereotype or label
can limit a person's sense of self — it narrows what they believe they may do or become, and it
can be *internalized* as an inner voice; (2) this narrowing is not evenly distributed — some
people are labeled more and get to be seen as individuals less, which is the *egalitarianism*
lens; and (3) the narrowing can be resisted — the door is not locked.** The *ethics* lens asks
what it costs others when we repeat a limiting label; the *technology* lens notes that media
and feeds repeat the same narrow images until they feel inevitable. Hold the research honestly:
the "stereotype threat" finding (that worrying about confirming a negative stereotype can
under-cut performance; Steele & Aronson, 1995) is well known but its size and boundaries are
debated — teach it as "labels can get inside and weigh on a person," not as a fixed law. Always
end in agency and hope: labels can narrow, but never finish. Keep examples low-stakes and
never require disclosure. Model one limit-and-push-back as a worked example (Kirschner, Sweller
& Clark, 2006). Distinguish **evidence from values**: that labels can limit is observable; that
every person deserves a wide, open sense of what is possible is a value this curriculum holds —
name it as one. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned to spot a label. Today the harder question: what
   does a label *do* to a person? (Pause.) It can narrow the map of what they think is
   possible.
2. **Meet the narrowing box (10 min).** Look at the poster: a person inside a closing box
   labeled **"not for people like you."** Worked example: *a learner is told "people like you
   aren't good at math." At first they answer back. But said often enough, the label moves
   inside — and soon they stop raising their hand. The box has narrowed what they believe they
   may do.*
3. **Name the limit (15 min).** In groups, take limit-and-push-back cards: told they are "not
   the kind who" leads, dances, is brave, or thinks deeply. For each, ask: **How does this
   limit the person?** (What would they stop trying?) And **who benefits, who is left out?**
   (Often the label protects someone else's spot or keeps a gate closed.)
4. **Where the images come from (10 min).** Discuss: why do these labels feel so true? Media
   and feeds repeat the same narrow images — the same faces doing the same things — until a
   type starts to look like a law. It is not a law. It is a habit of the images we are shown.
5. **Push back (10 min).** On your page, list the labels others have offered you. Then rewrite
   each one: not "I am ___" but "one small part of me is ___, and I am also ___." Practice one
   push-back line aloud with a partner: *"I am more than that one box."*
6. **Close (5 min).** A label can narrow you, but it cannot finish you. The door is open.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames ("People say I am ___, but I am also ___, ___, and ___")
  and work one card as a whole group first.
- **Extension:** Write about a label you have partly *internalized*, and name one concrete
  first step to loosen it — then test that step this week.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain how a label narrows a person's sense of
  self and offer one push-back that redefines it as a part, not the whole?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The my-many-boxes page, kept for the unit's stereotype-and-label
  analysis.

## Home connection

Notice a moment this week when you or someone you love almost believed a limiting label — and
try saying, out loud, "I am more than that one box."
