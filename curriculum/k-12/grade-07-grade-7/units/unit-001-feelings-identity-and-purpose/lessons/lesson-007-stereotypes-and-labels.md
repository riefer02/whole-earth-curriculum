# Lesson 7 — Stereotypes and Labels

## Summary

Learners recognize **stereotypes** and **labels**, and see how they work: a stereotype
generalizes a few traits to a whole group, and a label shrinks a whole person to a single box.
They spot both in everyday language and media, separate *describing one person* from
*generalizing about a group*, and practice restoring the whole person the label left out.

## Objectives

- Recognize stereotypes and labels. _(D01.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

"The quiet one." "The athlete." "The troublemaker." "Girls are ___." "Boys are ___." "People
from ___ are ___." These are shortcuts — fast, flat summaries that fit in one small box. But a
person is not a box. The quiet one also sings loudly in the kitchen. The athlete also writes
poems. The troublemaker also cares for a younger sibling with patient hands. Wherever you live,
the shortcut is the same temptation: it is easier to carry a label than to carry a person's
whole story.

## Materials

- Label-vs-person poster
- Stereotype-spotting cards
- Whole-person page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the label-vs-person poster (a single label beside the many-part whole person).
- Copy stereotype-spotting cards for each group and a whole-person page for each learner.
- Prepare one label to work together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **a stereotype is a fixed,
over-simple belief about a group — a few traits generalized to everyone in it; a label is a
name or category stuck on a person that can crowd out the rest of who they are.** Both are
*shortcuts*, and both can be true of a small part while missing the whole. Teach the
distinction concretely: describing one person you actually know is different from generalizing
"all ___ are ___." Keep the work safe: use everyday, low-stakes examples and media examples,
never ask learners to air real slurs or personal wounds, and always restore agency and hope
(the next lesson is "how they limit us and how we push back"). The *egalitarianism* lens asks
who gets labeled and who gets to be seen as an individual; the *technology* lens notes that
media and feeds repeat the same narrow images, teaching us to see types instead of people.
Model spotting one label as a worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Distinguish
**evidence from values**: that stereotypes exist and over-generalize is observable; that every
person deserves to be seen as a whole individual is a value this curriculum holds — name it as
one. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have mapped your changing self. Now meet the forces that would
   *freeze* it. Try to recall a label someone put on you — or on a group you belong to.
   (Pause.) Today you learn to see the shortcut for what it is.
2. **Meet the shortcut (10 min).** Look at the poster. On one side, a small box: **"the quiet
   one."** On the other, the whole person: singer, joker, runner, dreamer, worrier. Worked
   example: *the quiet one* is a **label** — one box. *All quiet people are shy and unfriendly*
   is a **stereotype** — it generalizes one trait to a whole group and predicts the rest.
3. **Spot them (15 min).** In groups, take stereotype-spotting cards: "all ___ are ___"
   sentences and everyday labels. For each, ask two questions: **What does this assume?** and
   **What does it leave out?** Notice: the label might name one real thing — and still miss
   almost everything.
4. **The difference that matters (10 min).** Discuss together: what is the difference between
   *describing one person you know* and *generalizing about a whole group*? One is observation;
   the other is a shortcut that rides ahead of the evidence.
5. **Restore the whole person (10 min).** On your page, write one label in the small box. Then,
   in the large space, draw or write the many true parts of a real person that the label misses.
   Close by naming the rule you now know: **a label is a shortcut; a person is a story.**

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter label and three example facets, and work the whole-person page
  as a group before independent work.
- **Extension:** Rewrite a stereotype as a fair, specific statement about one person, and
  explain what changed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner recognize a stereotype and a label, and name
  what each assumes and leaves out?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name an assumption *and* something it leaves out —
  and did I restore a whole person, not just argue with the label?"

## Home connection

This week, notice one label or "all ___ are ___" sentence you hear at home or in media — and
quietly name one thing about a real person it leaves out.
