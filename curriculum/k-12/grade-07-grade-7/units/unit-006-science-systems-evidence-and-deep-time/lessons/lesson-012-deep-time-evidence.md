# Lesson 12 — Reading Deep Time

## Summary

Learners read Earth's deep past through three kinds of evidence — **fossils**, **rock
layers**, and **ice cores** — and meet **deep time**: the Earth is about 4.6 billion years
old. They build and read a layer stack, order the evidence, and hold origins as *models with
evidence*, alongside other ways of knowing.

## Objectives

- Explain how evidence (fossils, rock layers, ice cores) tells the story of deep time and
  past life. _(D06.S4.07.02)_

## Connection

Stand at a cliff cut by a river and you may see stripes of rock, one on top of another — the
deeper stripes are older. Crack open the right rock and you may find the shape of a shell or
a leaf that lived long ago. Drill into a great ice sheet and each annual band of ice holds a
bubble of the air that year. Together these are a *book* the Earth has been writing for
billions of years. Reading it — oldest at the bottom, newest at the top — is how we know about
dinosaurs, ice ages, and life far older than any human.

## Materials

- Deep-time evidence cards
- Layered-past page
- Layered sand or paper strips

## Preparation

- Copy the evidence cards and the layered-past page; gather sand/paper strips for layer stacks.
- Have the worked example ready: a stack of layers, a fossil in the deepest layer — the fossil
  is *older* than the layers above it (the principle of superposition).
- Retrieval: recall from Grade 4 (D06.S4.04.02) that fossils and rock layers give clues about
  Earth's long history.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) in undisturbed rock,
deeper layers are older (superposition); (2) fossils in those layers tell us what lived then;
(3) ice cores record past air, year by year; and (4) all together they reveal deep time — an
Earth about 4.6 billion years old.** Use explicit instruction with the layer-stack worked
example first, then hands-on building (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The superposition
principle, the fossil record, and ice-core science (e.g., the Vostok and Greenland cores) are
established geology, summarized by the USGS and IPCC. Critically, hold origins as the Overview
directs: these are **models with evidence**, held as inquiry *alongside* other ways of knowing,
never as creed (`docs/philosophy.md` §11). Different peoples hold different stories of how the
world and life began; this lesson teaches how scientists read *evidence*, not what anyone
*must* believe. The global lens: every culture has looked at fossils (found shells on
mountaintops, bones in the earth) and wondered; science gives one rigorous way to read them.
The environment lens: deep time gives perspective on how slowly — and how fast — Earth can
change, which matters for how we care for it now.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of the oldest thing you have ever held — a stone, a shell, a
   grandparent's story. How do we know how old the *Earth itself* is?
2. **Three kinds of evidence (10 min).** **(1) Rock layers:** in undisturbed rock, the deeper
   a layer, the older it is. **(2) Fossils:** the shapes of past life trapped in those layers.
   **(3) Ice cores:** long columns of ice whose yearly bands trap bubbles of past air.
3. **Worked example (5 min).** A stack of layers: top = youngest, bottom = oldest. A fossil in
   the *deepest* layer is older than everything above it. Draw this on your page.
4. **Build and read (20 min).** Layer your sand or paper strips, bury a small "fossil" in one
   layer, then "read" your stack oldest-to-youngest. Order your evidence cards: which tells us
   about deep past life? about past air? Check with a partner.
5. **Deep time (5 min).** The Earth formed about **4.6 billion years ago**. On that scale, all
   of human history is the last sliver. Add the milestones (first life, first fish, dinosaurs)
   to your time bar.
6. **Close (5 min).** Fossils, layers, and ice are a book the Earth wrote. We read it as a
   *model with evidence* — one story among the many peoples tell about beginnings.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the layer-stack activity and describe each step aloud so learners with
  low vision can follow; provide "older" and "younger" labels to place.
- **Extension:** Compare two kinds of evidence — fossils (reveal past *life*) and ice cores
  (reveal past *air and temperature*) — and state what each is best at showing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order layers oldest-to-youngest and explain what
  a fossil or ice band reveals?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed layered-past page (labeled layers + placed fossil),
  kept as the unit's deep-time evidence note.

## Home connection

Find a place where layers show (a riverbank, a road cut, a stack of old papers or sediment).
Read it oldest-to-youngest and describe one thing each layer might hold.
