# Lesson 7 — Force, Mass, and Acceleration

## Summary

Learners meet the relationship that rules everyday motion: **force = mass × acceleration**.
By pushing light and heavy objects with the same effort, they discover for themselves that
more force means more acceleration, and more mass means less — and they restate it in words
and arrows.

## Objectives

- Explain force, mass, and acceleration and use them to describe everyday motion.
  _(D06.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

Kick a light, empty ball and a heavy, sand-filled ball with the same effort. The light one
shoots away; the heavy one barely moves. Push a shopping cart when it is empty and when it is
full. Throw a small stone and a big one. In every case, the same story repeats: a **force**
(a push or pull) changes motion, and how much it changes depends on the object's **mass**.
This one idea explains a kick, a car braking, a rocket launch, and a falling leaf.

## Materials

- Force-and-motion cart set
- F = ma page
- A stopwatch or steady count

## Preparation

- Gather a light and a heavy object (or cart) for each group, and a way to time.
- Have the worked example ready: the same push on a light cart and a heavy cart; the light
  one speeds up more (greater acceleration).
- Retrieval: recall from Grade 6 (D06.S3.06.02) that balanced and unbalanced forces decide
  whether motion changes.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **force, mass, and
acceleration are linked — F = m × a — so (a) a bigger force gives a bigger acceleration, and
(b) a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration for the same force.** Use explicit instruction
with the light-cart/heavy-cart worked example first, then hands-on guided practice (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006). This is Newton's second law of motion (Isaac Newton, *Principia
Mathematica*, 1687), foundational physics taught worldwide. Acceleration here means *any*
change in motion — speeding up, slowing down, or turning — not just "going faster." The
egalitarian lens (`docs/philosophy.md` §4): physics is everyone's — the same law pushes a
donkey cart in one country and a bullet train in another; no group owns it. The global lens:
people have applied force-and-motion ideas in every culture — from levers and wheels in
ancient engineering to the arrows and sailing ships of many seafaring peoples — long before
Newton named the law.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you pushed something heavy and something light. Which
   sped up more? Today you learn the rule that predicts it.
2. **Meet the three quantities (10 min).** **Force** is a push or a pull. **Mass** is how
   much matter (how much "stuff") an object has — roughly, how hard it is to move. **
   Acceleration** is how quickly motion changes (speeding up, slowing down, turning).
3. **The rule (5 min).** **force = mass × acceleration**. In words: a bigger force makes a
   bigger change in motion; a bigger mass makes a smaller change for the same force.
4. **Try it (20 min).** Push your light object and heavy object with the *same* effort. Which
   speeds up more? Time each across the same distance. On your page, draw the force arrow and
   the acceleration arrow for each. Write the rule in your own words.
5. **Worked example check (5 min).** Same force on two carts: light mass → long acceleration
   arrow; heavy mass → short arrow. Check your drawing against this.
6. **Close (5 min).** More force → more acceleration; more mass → less acceleration. You have
   just met the law that describes every push and pull on Earth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the push-and-compare activity and describe results aloud; provide the
  sentence starter "When I push with the same force, the ___ object speeds up ___ because ___."
- **Extension:** Rearrange the law to find acceleration (a = F ÷ m) and predict the
  acceleration for a simple number pair (e.g., force 10, mass 2).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner state, in words, that more force gives
  more acceleration and more mass gives less, and apply it to a new push-and-pull example?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I connect a bigger force to more change in motion,
  and a bigger mass to less change — with a real example?"

## Home connection

Push or throw two things of different mass with the same effort at home (an empty and a full
bottle, a light and heavy ball). Describe which speeds up more and why.
