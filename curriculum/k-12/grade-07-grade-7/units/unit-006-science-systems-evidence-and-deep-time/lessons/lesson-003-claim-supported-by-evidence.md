# Lesson 3 — Is the Claim Supported by Evidence?

## Summary

Learners move from correlation to **evaluation**: given a claim, they weigh whether the
evidence actually supports it, name what **further evidence** would help, and practice the
precise, fair answer — "supported," "weak," or "not enough to say yet."

## Objectives

- Evaluate whether a claim is supported by evidence and identify what further evidence
  would help. _(D06.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

A friend tells you a herbal tea "cures colds," and shows you one person who got better after
drinking it. A store ad says its shoes "make you faster." A headline claims a certain food
"is bad for you." Some of these are supported by strong evidence; some are supported by
almost none. Every day, claims like these compete for your money, your health, and your
belief. The skill that protects you is simple: **match the strength of your belief to the
strength of the evidence**, and know what is still missing.

## Materials

- Claim-and-evidence card set
- Evidence-evaluation page
- Highlighters

## Preparation

- Copy the claim-and-evidence card set and the evidence-evaluation page.
- Have the worked example ready: the claim "eating breakfast improves memory," supported by
  one small study of 20 students for one week with no comparison group — a weak base for a
  strong claim.
- Retrieval: recall the four-step causation checklist from Lesson 2; evaluation is its cousin.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **evaluate a claim by (1)
naming exactly what is claimed, (2) weighing the evidence offered, and (3) naming what
further evidence would settle it — and "not enough to say yet" is a precise, mature answer.**
Use explicit instruction and a worked example first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006), then
guided practice. The concepts of sample size, control groups, and replication are standard
ideas in scientific method and statistics. The technology lens (`docs/philosophy.md` §4): the
internet makes claims travel faster than ever, so the *skill* of weighing evidence — not just
the facts — is what learners most need to thrive in a media-saturated world. The egalitarian
lens: advertising and misinformation often target people with the least access to good
evidence; teaching everyone to evaluate claims is a way of evening the scales. The global
and anthropological lens (`docs/philosophy.md` §4, §11): claims — and ways of knowing — come
from every culture. A single anecdote about a herbal tea is not the same as a whole healing
tradition: traditional and Indigenous medicines are knowledge systems in their own right,
held respectfully alongside scientific medicine. This lesson teaches learners to weigh the
evidence *for one claim*, never to dismiss a tradition; "not enough to say yet" is respect
for the evidence, not disrespect for the people. Keep it
age-appropriate and non-dogmatic: the goal is not to call all ads lies, but to ask "what is
the evidence, and what is missing?"

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a claim you heard recently — an ad, a rumour, a health tip.
   How sure were you it was true? Today you learn to *grade* a claim by its evidence.
2. **Three questions (10 min).** To evaluate any claim, ask: **(1)** What exactly is claimed?
   **(2)** What evidence is offered, and how strong is it? **(3)** What further evidence
   would settle it?
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Claim: "Eating breakfast improves memory." Evidence offered:
   one small study of 20 students for one week, no comparison group. Weigh it: a small sample
   and no control make the evidence **weak**. Further evidence that would help: a larger
   sample, a control group, repetition by other researchers, and a "how" (a mechanism).
   Verdict: **not enough to say yet** — the claim might be true, but the evidence does not
   yet support it.
4. **Grade your own (20 min).** Take your card set. For each claim, fill your three columns
   and grade it: **supported**, **weak**, or **not enough to say yet**. Check with your
   partner — did you both name *specific* missing evidence, not just "more proof"?
5. **Close (5 min).** Match your belief to your evidence. "I don't know yet" is a strong,
   honest position — and the starting point of real knowledge.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two claims (one clearly weak, one clearly strong) and a sentence starter
  ("The claim is ___. The evidence is ___ because ___. To settle it I would need ___.").
- **Extension:** Compare two *sources* on the same claim (who made it, how, and with what
  interest) and explain which is more trustworthy.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner grade a claim's evidence support and
  name at least one specific piece of further evidence?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name the claim, weigh the actual evidence, and say
  *specifically* what is missing — not just 'more proof'?"

## Home connection

Pick one claim you hear this week (an ad, a remedy, a headline) and run the three questions
at home. Note what evidence was offered and what was missing, and share your grade.

## Resources

  evaluates claims. The value that everyone deserves the skill to evaluate claims is a value
  commitment, not a factual claim (`docs/philosophy.md` §5). For a global, accessible framing
  of evidence-based reasoning in health, see the World Health Organization's emphasis on
