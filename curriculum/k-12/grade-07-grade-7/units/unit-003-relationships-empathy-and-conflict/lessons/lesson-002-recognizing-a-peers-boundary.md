# Lesson 2 — Reading the Boundary: Recognizing a Peer's No

## Summary

Learners learn to recognize when a peer has set a boundary by reading three kinds of cue —
**words**, **body**, and **voice** — even when the word "no" is never spoken. They practice
spotting the signal in short scenes, then practice the one respectful response: **stop, the
first time, without argument.**

## Objectives

- Recognize when a peer has set a boundary, from their words, body, and voice, and respond
  respectfully. _(D02.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Not every "no" uses the word "no." A friend steps back when you lean in. A classmate goes
quiet and looks away when you ask to borrow their phone. Someone holds up a palm, or laughs
a little and says "…maybe later," or answers in a flat voice. These are all boundaries, said
without the word. The skill is to read them — and to treat the *softest* signal as seriously
as the loudest one. This is true everywhere, even though the gestures differ from place to
place: in some cultures people signal "stop" or "I'm not comfortable" with an open palm, in
others with a step back or a downcast look. Reading the whole person — not only the words —
is a universal human skill.

## Materials

- Reading-the-signal poster
- Cue cards
- Pencils and paper

## Preparation

- Print or draw the reading-the-signal poster.
- Copy and cut the cue cards (one scene per card).
- Arrange pairs for practice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **boundaries are
often signaled without the word "no," through words, body, and voice — and the respectful
response is always to stop the first time, without arguing**. Keep scenes small and safe
(borrowing, standing close, a touch on the shoulder, messaging); use hypothetical
characters, never real classmates, so no one is put on the spot. Emphasize that a soft or
hesitant "no" is still a no — hesitation is not an invitation to push. Note the cultural
range of body language honestly (a downcast look, a step back, an open palm) and never rank
one custom above another. Model reading one cue card aloud as a worked example before pairs
work alone (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Yesterday you learned to *set* a boundary with yes,
   maybe, and no. Today you learn to *read* one — because most people signal a limit
   without ever saying the word "no." Why might someone not say it out loud? (They might
   feel awkward, unsure, or not want to hurt feelings.)
2. **Meet the three cues (10 min).** Look at the poster. A person signals a boundary three
   ways at once: **words** ("no," "stop," "not now," "I'd rather not"), **body** (a raised
   palm, leaning back, stepping away, turning aside, crossing arms), and **voice** (flat,
   quiet, uncertain, or firm). Worked example: *"Sure… maybe later"* said with a step back
   and a turned shoulder — the words sound like yes, but the body and voice say no. Read
   the whole person.
3. **Practice reading (15 min).** In pairs, take a cue card. One partner reads or acts the
   scene; the other names the cue — *"I noticed the body — she stepped back"* — and says
   what the respectful response is: *stop, the first time.* Swap cards and roles.
4. **The soft no is still a no (10 min).** Discuss: which is harder to read, a loud "NO"
   or a quiet "…maybe later"? A soft or hesitant no is still a no — hesitation is not an
   invitation to push. Write one sentence: *When I'm not sure, I will ___ (ask, pause,
   stop and check).*
5. **Close (5 min).** Respect means reading the signal you were given, not the one you
   hoped for. Tomorrow you will learn what to do after a boundary gets crossed — how to
   repair a relationship after a disagreement.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give pairs just the body and voice cards first, then add word cards; provide
  a response sentence starter ("I'll stop — thanks for telling me").
- **Extension:** Write a short scene of your own where a character sets a boundary without
  the word "no," and swap with a partner to read each other's cue.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner name at least one verbal and one nonverbal
  cue in a scene, and state a stop-the-first-time response?
- **Peer check:** Pairs confirm each other caught the cue — *"You noticed the body cue; the
  respectful response is to stop."*

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how people in your family show "no" or "not comfortable" without
saying the word — a look, a gesture, a tone — and share one cue you learned to read today.
