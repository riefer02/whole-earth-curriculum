# Lesson 10 — Mutual Aid: Why We Help

## Summary

Learners meet **mutual aid** — helping one another, among equals, so everyone both gives
and receives — through four living traditions from Kenya, Indonesia, the Andes, and North
America. They plan or join a small, doable mutual-aid action for their own school or
community, and reflect honestly on their **motives** for helping: care, duty, recognition,
or a mix.

## Objectives

- Organize or join a mutual-aid action in their school or community. _(D02.S4.07.01)_
- Reflect on the motives for helping, such as care, duty, or recognition. _(D02.S4.07.02)_

## Connection

In Kenya, people speak of **harambee** — "all pull together" — when a community joins to
build a school or help a family. In Indonesia, **gotong royong** names neighbors sharing the
burden of building and gathering. In the Andes, **mink'a** (or *minga*) is the old practice
of farming the terraces together so no one harvests alone. Across North America, a **barn
raising** gathered neighbors to lift a family's barn in a single day, the favor returning
later to whoever helped. Different names, same thing: **mutual aid** — helping where
everyone both gives and receives, no one above and no one below. And behind every act of
help sits a motive: sometimes **care** (I help because I feel for you), sometimes **duty**
(I help because it is right and owed), sometimes **recognition** (I help to be seen as good).
Often it is more than one at once.

## Materials

- Mutual aid traditions poster
- Plan-a-help page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the mutual aid traditions poster.
- Copy a plan-a-help page for each learner.
- Arrange for the group to choose one small action together (or prepare several options).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **mutual aid is
helping among equals — everyone gives and receives — and it appears in living traditions
across the world; and that honest reflection on motives (care, duty, recognition) makes our
helping truer**. Present the four traditions with respect as living practices, never as
curiosities or a ranking, and note that details vary by community and are best learned from
people who practice them (the poster is a starting point, not the last word). Honor the
egalitarian lens: mutual aid is not charity from above — it is solidarity among equals.
Keep motives non-judged: all three motives are human and common, and naming them honestly is
the skill (inquiry over creed — you are not grading anyone's reasons). The action must be
small, safe, and genuinely doable — a shared cleanup, a study circle, a letter of thanks, a
classroom "help board" — with every learner offered a real part. Model the planner once as
a worked example before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (10 min).** This unit began with boundaries and repair, moved through
   empathy and belonging, and now ends where care becomes action. Look at the poster: four
   traditions, one idea — **mutual aid**. In mutual aid, everyone both gives and receives;
   no one is only the helper and no one only the helped. (Retrieval: name the four
   traditions and their homes.)
2. **Meet the motives (10 min).** Behind every act of help sits a motive. **Care** — I help
   because I feel for you. **Duty** — I help because it is right, because I owe it to my
   community. **Recognition** — I help because I want to be seen as good. Worked example,
   said aloud: *"When I help a classmate carry books, it is partly care (I like them) and
   partly duty (we share a classroom). Sometimes I also just want to be thanked — that's
   recognition."* All three are human; naming them honestly is the skill.
3. **Plan a small action (20 min).** As a group, choose one small, doable mutual-aid action
   — a shared cleanup, a study circle, a "help board," a thank-you letter to someone who
   serves the community. On your page, fill in: the **need**, the **people**, the **roles**
   (what each person gives and receives), and the **steps**. Sign up for a real part.
4. **Reflect on your motives (10 min).** On your page, write one honest line for each: *My
   care says ___. My duty says ___. My want of recognition says ___.* There is no wrong
   answer. Then note which motive is strongest in you today, and what you want it to be.
5. **Close (5 min).** Mutual aid is how care becomes shared action — everyone gives,
   everyone receives. You have now traveled the whole arc: from boundaries, through empathy,
   to acting together. Carry all of it with you, everywhere you belong.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-chosen action and a roles word bank; ask the learner to fill
  only the "need" and their own role, plus one motives line.
- **Extension:** Distinguish mutual aid from charity in one or two sentences (mutual aid is
  among equals, everyone gives and receives; charity flows one way), and note why the
  difference matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner take a real part in the planned action and
  name what they will give and receive?
- **Performance (portfolio):** The completed plan-a-help page, kept as evidence of organizing
  or joining a mutual-aid action.
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their motives note names care, duty, and recognition
  honestly, without judging any as "bad."

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time the community helped together — a shared harvest, a
cleanup, a fund for a family — and share the action your class is planning, and the part
you chose.

## Resources

- On mutual aid as a widespread human practice: Kropotkin, *Mutual Aid: A Factor of
  Evolution* (1902; the term's canonical source — held here as a description of a practice,
  not a claim that helping is "good").
- On named communal-help traditions (harambee, gotong royong, mink'a/minga, barn raising):
  the "Communal work" entry in the open encyclopedia; verify local details with community
  members.
- On cooperative structures and why cooperation helps groups thrive: Johnson & Johnson,
  social interdependence theory; and Slavin, *Cooperative Learning*.
- On modeling a new skill before independent practice: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
