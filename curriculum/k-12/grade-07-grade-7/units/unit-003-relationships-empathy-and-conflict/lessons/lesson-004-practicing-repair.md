# Lesson 4 — Practicing the Repair Conversation

## Summary

Learners put the three repair steps into **one continuous conversation**, start to finish,
using invented, safe scenarios. They practice the full loop — accountable apology, real
listening, an agreed next step — and learn that the last, quiet step is **follow-through**:
doing the next step you promised, which is what actually rebuilds trust.

## Objectives

- Repair a relationship after a disagreement by apologizing, listening, and agreeing on
  next steps. _(D02.S1.07.02)_
- Respect a boundary someone else has set, including the boundaries that remain after
  repair. _(D02.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

The bridge that broke between two people is rebuilt one plank at a time — and the last
plank is the one no one sees: you do the thing you promised. A repair conversation that
ends in "next time I will ask first" means nothing if, next time, you read their notebook
again. This is true in a friendship, a family, a classroom, a neighborhood, anywhere people
keep promises to one another. The words open the door; the follow-through walks through it.

## Materials

- Repair scenario cards
- Repair card (from Lesson 3)
- Pencils and paper

## Preparation

- Copy the repair scenario cards (one per pair).
- Re-copy the repair card as a reference.
- Arrange pairs facing each other.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a repair
conversation is a single loop — apologize, listen, agree — and it is only complete when the
agreed next step is actually done**. Use invented, safe scenarios; never script a real
classmate's conflict, and never role-play anything like trauma. The fishbowl (whole-group
watch) is a good structure in large groups: model once, then let pairs run in parallel
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Watch for the hollow apology ("sorry you felt that
way") and coach it toward accountability ("I did X, and it hurt you"). Honor the
egalitarian lens: repair is between equals — no one "wins" a repair, and the listener's
choice to forgive or not is theirs alone. Note that boundaries stay real after repair:
making up does not erase a boundary; if someone still says no, you still stop. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Name the three repair steps without looking — apologize,
   listen, agree. (Retrieval: this is the third time you have touched them.) Today you run
   all three as one conversation, then add the hidden fourth step.
2. **Watch a full repair (10 min).** Two volunteers (or you and a partner) run one scenario
   in the middle while everyone watches for each step: Did the apology name their own
   action? Did the listener hear without defending? Did they agree on a concrete next step?
   Use a scenario card.
3. **Practice the loop (20 min).** In pairs, take a scenario card and run the full
   conversation. Swap roles and run it again the other way. Go slowly; the silence in the
   "listen" step is doing real work. Try at least two scenarios.
4. **The hidden fourth step (10 min).** The repair is not finished when the words stop.
   It finishes when you **do** the next step. Write one line: *I promised "next time I
   will ___," and the way I will keep that promise is ___ .* A repair without follow-through
   is just words.
5. **Boundaries still stand (5 min).** Close by naming it plainly: making up does not erase
   a boundary. If someone says no — before, during, or after repair — you still stop.
6. **Close (5 min).** You can now set a boundary, read one, and repair after a break. Next
   you turn outward: how another person's whole life shapes the way they see things.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Run only the apology and listen steps first; provide the next-step sentence
  starter ("Next time I will ___ instead of ___").
- **Extension:** Add a "check-back" fourth move — returning a week later to ask "did I keep
  my word?" — and write why checking back matters to trust.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner run all three steps in order, with an
  accountable apology, a quiet listening turn, and a concrete next step?
- **Peer check:** The listening partner says back one thing they heard and one next step
  they would actually accept.
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their follow-through line names a *real, doable* action.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a promise that mattered because the person actually kept it — and
notice, this week, one small promise you can keep as follow-through.
