# Lesson 1 — Boundaries: The Yes, the No, and the Maybe

## Summary

Learners meet a **boundary** as the line where comfortable ends and uncomfortable
begins, expressed with three signals — **yes**, **maybe**, and **no**. They name their own
limits in small, safe terms, practice saying a boundary clearly, and learn that a **no is
complete**: it does not need a reason to be respected.

## Objectives

- Set a personal boundary and say it clearly, and respect a boundary someone else has
  set. _(D02.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Everyone has a line where comfortable ends and uncomfortable begins. You feel it when a
stranger stands too close on a crowded bus, when someone borrows your things without
asking, when a person assumes a hug that you did not offer, when someone keeps messaging
after you have gone quiet. That line is a **boundary**, and it is yours to set. The exact
customs differ everywhere — in some places people greet with a bow, in others a handshake
or a nod, in others a kiss on the cheek — so there is no single "default" way to be near
someone. What is the same in every place is this: each person gets to say what is okay for
them, and everyone else is asked to respect it.

## Materials

- Yes/No/Maybe poster
- Boundary circle page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the Yes/No/Maybe poster (three signals, each with a distinct shape).
- Copy a boundary circle page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for discussion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a boundary is a
person's own line between comfortable and uncomfortable, and it is always the person's
right to set it — while a "no" from anyone is complete and needs no explanation to be
respected**. Keep the topic safe: invite only small, everyday examples (borrowing,
standing close, touching, messaging), never require a learner to disclose anything
private, and never push into pain. Name the cultural range of greetings (bow, handshake,
nod) side by side, never ranked, and do not hold any custom up as the "normal" one. Model
one clear boundary sentence as a worked example before learners write their own
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Note honestly that in some homes and cultures
asserting a boundary to an older person is hard or unsafe — the right to set a limit is
real even when it is hard to exercise, and a trusted adult can help. If a learner
discloses harm, follow your safeguarding duty (believe them, do not promise secrecy, tell
a trusted adult). See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Last year you learned to choose assertion over being
   passive or aggressive. Today you meet the thing assertion protects: your **boundary** —
   the line where comfortable ends and uncomfortable begins. Without saying anything
   private: name one thing you are simply not okay with (a touch, a borrowed item, a
   late-night message). You don't have to explain why.
2. **Meet the three signals (10 min).** Look at the poster. **YES** — welcome, comfortable.
   **MAYBE** — ask first, it depends. **NO** — not okay, stop. The difference between the
   three is the difference between "come in," "knock first," and "please don't." Worked
   example, said aloud: *"I'm happy to share my pens — YES. For my phone, ask me first —
   MAYBE. Please don't read my notebook — NO."*
3. **Name your own limits (15 min).** On your circle page, write or draw **one** small,
   safe thing for each ring: one thing that is welcome (public), one that needs asking
   first (personal), and one that is not okay (close). Keep it to everyday things. Share
   one with a partner only if you are comfortable.
4. **Say it clearly (10 min).** A boundary only works when it is said. Practice these
   clear, kind sentences: *"I'm not comfortable with that." / "Please stop." / "I need some
   space." / "No, thank you."* Say one aloud to a partner; a clear "no" is not rude, it is
   honest.
5. **Respect the other side (5 min).** Now flip it: when someone says no to you, you stop —
   the first time, without arguing. A no is complete. It does not need a reason to be
   respected.
6. **Close (5 min).** Your boundaries are yours; other people's are theirs. Tomorrow you
   will learn to *read* when a peer has set a boundary without even saying the word.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the sentence starters on a card and a word bank (borrow, touch,
  space, time, phone, message) for the circle page.
- **Extension:** Write a boundary for each of three areas — your body, your belongings, and
  your time — and note how the words you would use differ in each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one welcome, one ask-first, and one
  not-okay thing, and say a boundary sentence clearly without aggression or apology?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their three ring entries and checks that each names
  a *real, small* limit, and that their "no" sentence is clear and kind.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how people in your family or culture show a limit — a word, a gesture,
a custom — and share one boundary sentence you practiced today, if you like.
