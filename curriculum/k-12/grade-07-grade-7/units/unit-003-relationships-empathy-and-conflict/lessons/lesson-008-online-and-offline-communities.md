# Lesson 8 — Two Kinds of Gathering: Online and Offline

## Summary

Learners examine how **online and offline communities differ** and how people belong in
each. They compare face-to-face belonging (body, voice, one place, now) with online
belonging (words and images, across distance, often persistent and large), map the
communities they belong to in each, and ask who can join each kind — noticing that access
is not even for everyone.

## Objectives

- Examine how online and offline communities differ and how people belong in each.
  _(D02.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

A **community** is a group you belong to and that partly belongs to you. You are in offline
communities — a family, a class, a neighborhood, a team, a place of worship — where you sit
in the same room and read faces, voices, and bodies. You may also be in online communities —
a game with a guild, a study group, a hobby forum, a group chat — where you may never meet
the others, and where what they know of you is mostly words and images. Both are real
belonging, but they work differently: offline belonging is local and immediate; online
belonging can reach across the whole planet and can last long after you log off. And not
everyone can join each kind equally — the internet is not evenly available to everyone on
Earth, which matters to who gets to belong where.

## Materials

- Two kinds of gathering poster
- Belonging map page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-kinds-of-gathering poster.
- Copy a belonging map page for each learner.
- Arrange pairs and space for a human network.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **online and
offline communities are both real ways to belong, but they differ — offline is local,
face-to-face, and immediate; online reaches across distance, runs on words and images, and
can persist — and access to the online kind is uneven across the world**. Hold the
technology lens honestly (neither worship nor fear): online community is not "fake" and
offline is not automatically "better"; each fits different needs. Hold the egalitarian lens:
name plainly that internet access varies by cost, geography, and disability (World Bank,
Digital Dividends), so "everyone can join online" is not yet true. Do not require learners
to reveal which online spaces they use, and never ask for account names. Model one entry on
the belonging map as a worked example before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have spent this unit on relationships between people.
   Now zoom out: where do those relationships live? Name one group you belong to. (A
   community is a group you belong to, and that partly belongs to you.)
2. **Meet the two kinds (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Offline:** you share a place and a
   time; you read bodies and voices; it is small and local, and it happens now. **Online:**
   you share words and images; you may never meet; it can be large and far, and it can last
   — a post stays up long after you leave. Worked example: *"In my class (offline) I belong
   by showing up and speaking; in a hobby group (online) I belong by posting and replying."*
3. **Feel the reach (10 min).** Stand as a group. First, make a tight face-to-face circle —
   that is offline belonging. Then stretch string between learners standing far apart — that
   is online reach: you can belong to people who are not in the room. What is gained? What
   is lost?
4. **Map your belonging (15 min).** On your page, list one or two communities in each
   column — offline and online — and beside each, one sentence on *how* you belong there.
   You may leave either column light; not everyone is online, and that is fine.
5. **Ask who can join (5 min).** Discuss one question: who can *not* join an online
   community, and why (cost, no device, no connection, a disability that design ignored)?
   Belonging is not evenly open to everyone — noticing that is part of caring about it.
6. **Close (5 min).** You belong in more than one way, and each way has strengths and
   limits. Tomorrow you will ask how these communities change over time.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a list of example communities (family, class, team, group chat, game,
  forum) to choose from.
- **Extension:** Choose one online community and one offline community and write two
  differences in how a newcomer is welcomed into each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one offline and one online
  community and state one way belonging differs between them?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their map lists at least one "how I belong" sentence
  for each community they named.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home which communities they belong to in person and which online, and how
belonging feels different in each — then share one thing you noticed about access.
