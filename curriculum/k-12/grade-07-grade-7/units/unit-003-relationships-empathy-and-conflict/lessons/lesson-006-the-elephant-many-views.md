# Lesson 6 — The Same Thing, Many Views

## Summary

Learners move from knowing that views differ to **holding several true views of one
situation at once**. Using the same river seen by a fisher, a farmer, a child, and an
elder, they name what shapes each view, practice speaking from a view unlike their own, and
begin the core move of **empathy across difference**: seeking to understand, not to win.

## Objectives

- Explain how a person's background, culture, and experiences shape their point of view.
  _(D02.S2.07.01)_
- Practice empathy across difference by seeking to understand views unlike one's own.
  _(D02.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

One river, four people: the fisher sees a way to market, the farmer sees water for the
crops, the child sees the place they learned to swim, the elder sees what floods their home
each rainy season. All four are true, and none is the whole. The world is full of these
moments — a new road is progress to one neighbor and the loss of a quiet field to another;
a rule feels fair to some and heavy to others. Empathy across difference is not agreeing
with every view; it is the skill of *seeking to understand* a view unlike your own before
you judge it.

## Materials

- Same river, four views poster
- Multiple-views page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the same-river poster.
- Copy a multiple-views page for each learner.
- Arrange small groups of four.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **several views of
one situation can all be true, and empathy across difference means holding them together
long enough to understand a view unlike your own — not to win an argument**. Keep the
global/anthropological lens alive: the fisher–farmer–child–elder example is deliberately
everyday and cross-cultural (a river, a field, a market, a home) so it works in a city, a
village, a coast, or a mountain town. Model speaking from one unfamiliar view aloud as a
worked example before groups try it (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Guard the
egalitarian lens: the goal is not to rank views or to "convert" anyone, and empathy is not
agreement — a learner can understand a view and still hold a different one. Keep all
disclosure optional. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Yesterday you learned a point of view is a lens built
   from background, culture, and experience. Today's harder move: holding *several* true
   views of *one* thing at once.
2. **Meet the four views (10 min).** Look at the poster. One river, four people. Name what
   each sees, then name what *shapes* it: the fisher's work, the farmer's field, the
   child's memory, the elder's flooded home. None is wrong; each is a true part. The whole
   truth is all of them together.
3. **Speak from an unfamiliar view (15 min).** In your group, each person takes one role.
   In the first person, speak that role's view of the river — *"The river is…"* — and add
   one thing that shapes it. Then try a view that is *not* like your own life: what does it
   ask you to notice that you had not?
4. **Map the views (10 min).** On your page, write one shared situation (a change in your
   town, a school rule, a game, a festival). Fill three windows: three different views,
   each with a "what shapes this view" line. For one window, write one sentence of empathy:
   *"I can see how ___ would make them feel ___ ."*
5. **Close (5 min).** Empathy across difference is not agreeing with everyone. It is the
   discipline of understanding a view before you judge it — and the humility of knowing your
   own view is also just one part. Tomorrow you will practice the full skill: seeking to
   understand, not to win.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the shared situation and two pre-filled windows; ask the learner to
  add a third view and one empathy sentence.
- **Extension:** Find a point where two of your windows *agree* even though they seemed
  opposite, and write what they share.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a view unlike their own *and* name
  something that shapes it, without mocking or dismissing it?
- **Peer check:** In groups, partners confirm each view was spoken "in character" and fairly
  — *"You named what shapes that view."*
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their empathy sentence names the *other* person's
  feeling or need, not just their own.

## Home connection

Listen to someone at home describe a shared situation, and before you answer, say back what
you heard them value or fear — then notice whether they feel understood.
