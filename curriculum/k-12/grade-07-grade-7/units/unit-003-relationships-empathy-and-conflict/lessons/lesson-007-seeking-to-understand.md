# Lesson 7 — Seeking to Understand, Not to Win

## Summary

Learners practice the full skill of **empathy across difference** through a four-move
listening loop — **listen, repeat back, ask a question, check** — aimed at understanding a
view unlike their own. They learn the key distinction that **understanding is not
agreement**, and that curiosity, not argument, is the honest first move across a difference.

## Objectives

- Practice empathy across difference by seeking to understand views unlike one's own.
  _(D02.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

When you meet a view unlike your own, the pull is to argue — to win. Empathy across
difference asks the opposite first move: *understand before you are understood.* You listen,
you say back what you heard ("so it sounds like you…"), you ask a question you genuinely
don't know the answer to ("what is it about this that matters most to you?"), and you check
("did I get that right?"). This is how people across every culture bridge real differences —
not by agreeing, but by understanding. Understanding does not mean you have changed your
mind; it means you have *seen* the other person, and that is the honest first move.

## Materials

- Listening loop poster
- View cards
- Pencils and paper

## Preparation

- Print or draw the listening loop poster.
- Copy the view cards (safe, invented differences of view).
- Arrange pairs facing each other.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **empathy across
difference is a practiced loop — listen, repeat back, ask a question, check — and its aim is
understanding, not agreement or victory**. Keep the differences of view small, invented,
and safe (a school rule, a food custom, a change in a neighborhood), never real conflicts
between classmates and never beliefs a learner would be graded on (inquiry over creed).
Guard the key distinction in your own language: understanding is not agreement, and no
learner is asked to change a view. Model the loop once as a worked example before pairs
practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Honor the global lens by noting that
"listening to understand" is a value held in many knowledge traditions, named differently
in each — leave room for learners to name their own. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You can hold several true views of one thing. Now the
   live skill: when you *disagree* with someone, how do you still seek to understand them?
   (Notice the pull to argue — name it, don't feed it.)
2. **Meet the four moves (10 min).** Look at the loop poster. **Listen** (don't plan your
   reply). **Repeat back** ("so you're saying…"). **Ask a question** you don't know the
   answer to ("what matters most to you here?"). **Check** ("did I get that right?").
   Worked example, said aloud: *"So you think the new rule helps because it keeps things
   fair — what makes it feel fair to you? … Did I understand you?"*
3. **Practice the loop (20 min).** In pairs, take a view card. One partner states a view;
   the other runs the four moves — no arguing, no "yes but." Then swap roles. Try two
   different cards. Notice the moment curiosity replaces the urge to win.
4. **Name the difference (10 min).** Write one sentence: *Understanding a view means ___
   but agreeing with it means ___.* Understanding does not require you to change your mind —
   it requires you to actually *see* the other person.
5. **Close (5 min).** Empathy across difference is a skill you can get better at, and the
   first move is always the same: seek to understand. Tomorrow you will step back and look
   at where all these relationships live — the communities you belong to, online and off.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only "listen" and "repeat back" first; provide the repeat-back
  sentence starter ("so you're saying…").
- **Extension:** Run the loop on a view you actually disagree with, and record one genuine
  new thing you understood — then note, in one sentence, where you still disagree and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner repeat back accurately, ask a genuine
  question, and check understanding — without arguing or dismissing the view?
- **Peer check:** The speaker confirms whether they felt understood — *"Yes, you got it"* or
  *"not quite — let me say it again."*
- **Self-check:** The learner checks their sentence distinguishes *understanding* from
  *agreeing*.

## Home connection

This week, when someone at home disagrees with you, try the loop once before answering —
repeat back what you heard, then ask a real question — and notice what changes.
