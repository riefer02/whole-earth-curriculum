# Lesson 3 — Repair: Apologize, Listen, Agree

## Summary

Learners meet the **repair conversation** as three steps — **apologize** with
accountability, **listen** to how it felt for the other person, and **agree** together on
next steps. They learn that repair is not erasing what happened but naming it, hearing the
hurt, and choosing a different next step, and they practice the first real sentence of each
step.

## Objectives

- Repair a relationship after a disagreement by apologizing, listening, and agreeing on
  next steps. _(D02.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Every relationship gets bent at some point: a sharp word, a borrowed thing returned broken,
a promise forgotten, a boundary crossed. What happens *after* decides whether the
relationship heals or quietly breaks. Repair is a skill, not magic — and people everywhere
have worked out versions of it. Many traditions repair harm by *restoring* the relationship
rather than only punishing: peacemaking circles among Indigenous nations, the Diné
(Navajo) principle of hózhó — restoring balance and harmony — and the way modern
restorative practices ask three questions: *What happened? Who was harmed? How do we make
it right?* The same three steps run through all of them: name it honestly, hear the hurt,
and agree on what comes next.

## Materials

- Repair poster
- Repair card
- Pencils and paper

## Preparation

- Print or draw the repair poster (three steps).
- Copy a repair card for each learner.
- Arrange pairs for practice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **repair is a
three-step skill — an accountable apology, real listening, and an agreed next step — and it
restores a relationship rather than only ending the conflict**. Use invented, safe
disagreements (a forgotten promise, a borrowed item, an overheard remark), never a real
classmate's conflict, and never require a learner to confess anything. Hold the
global/anthropological lens honestly: present restorative practices, Diné peacemaking, and
related traditions side by side as living knowledge, never as curiosities or a ranking;
the "what happened / who was harmed / how do we make it right" questions are a modern
restorative frame (Zehr; IIRP), and Diné hózhó is a real principle of balance — teach both
with respect and invite learners to name repair practices from their own cultures. Model
the full conversation once as a worked example before pairs practice (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Note that an apology is not the same as being forgiven — the other person
chooses; our job is the honest offer and the changed next step. See `docs/facilitation.md`
and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You can set a boundary and read one. What happens when a
   boundary still gets crossed, or a disagreement leaves things bent? Today: **repair** —
   the skill that restores a relationship after a break.
2. **Meet the three steps (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Step 1 — Apologize with
   accountability:** *"I'm sorry I ___ . It was wrong, and it hurt you."* Not "sorry you
   felt that way" — that blames the other person's feelings. **Step 2 — Listen:** *"How did
   it feel for you?"* Then stay quiet and hear the answer, without defending yourself.
   **Step 3 — Agree on next steps:** *"Next time I will ___ . What do you need from me?"*
   Worked example, said aloud: *"I'm sorry I read your notebook without asking. How did it
   feel for you? … I hear that it felt like a break of trust. Next time I will ask first,
   and you can tell me what you need from me to trust me again."*
3. **Write your first sentences (15 min).** Choose one small, invented disagreement. On your
   card, write one real sentence for each step — an accountable apology, a listening
   question, and a next step. Use the starters.
4. **Practice aloud (10 min).** In pairs, take turns speaking your three sentences. The
   listener's only job is to *hear* — no defending, no interrupting. Afterward, name one
   thing that felt different about being truly listened to.
5. **Close (5 min).** Repair does not erase what happened. It names it, hears the hurt, and
   chooses a different next step. That is how trust grows back. Tomorrow you will practice
   the whole conversation, start to finish.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three sentence starters in large print; practice only the
  apology step first, then add listening.
- **Extension:** Write two versions of an apology — accountable and hollow — and explain in
  one sentence why the accountable one can rebuild trust while the hollow one cannot.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner produce an accountable apology that names
  their own action, a listening question, and a concrete next step?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their three sentences and checks: does the apology
  name *what I did* (not the other person's feelings)? Does the next step name *my* future
  action?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how people in your family or community make things right after a
disagreement — a word, a custom, a ritual — and share the three repair steps you learned.
