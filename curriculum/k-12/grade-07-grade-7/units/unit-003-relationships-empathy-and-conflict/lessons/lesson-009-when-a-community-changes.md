# Lesson 9 — When a Community Changes

## Summary

Learners examine how communities change over time — through migration, new roads and
buildings, new people, and new ways of doing things — and why change is **both hard and good
at once**. They name what is lost and what is gained in a change, learn that the same change
can help some and burden others, and hold both feelings without choosing one.

## Objectives

- Explain how communities change over time and why change can be both hard and good.
  _(D02.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

No community stands still. A village gets a road, and the market grows — but the quiet
field where children played is gone. New families arrive with new languages and foods, and
the neighborhood is richer — but the people who were there first may feel crowded or
unseen. A coast, a mountain town, a city block, a school: everywhere, change brings both
loss and gain in the same breath. The skill is not to pick one feeling and throw the other
away — it is to hold both: this change is hard *and* good, and it lands differently on
different people.

## Materials

- A community changes poster
- Change reflection page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-panel poster.
- Copy a change reflection page for each learner.
- Arrange pairs for sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **communities
change over time, and change is both hard and good — it brings loss and gain in the same
breath, and it lands differently on different people**. Keep the examples global and
ordinary (a road, a market, migration, a new building), never one region's "default" and
never a story that ranks one culture above another. Guard the egalitarian lens: the same
change often helps some and burdens others, and naming who carries the cost is part of
caring — without making anyone a villain. Invite learners to name a change from their own
community only if they wish; no one is required to share. Model one loss-and-gain pair as a
worked example before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Note that grief
for what is lost is real and legitimate — it is not "resisting progress." See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have mapped where you belong. Now ask: how does that
   place change? Think of one thing that has changed in your neighborhood or school — a new
   building, a new family, a new road, a closed shop. (Don't say it yet.)
2. **Meet the three panels (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Before:** a few houses and a
   quiet field. **Change arrives:** a road, a new building, new families. **After:** more
   homes and a shop — and something lost. Notice: the change is not all good or all bad.
   It is **both**.
3. **Name the loss and the gain (15 min).** On your page, choose one change (real or
   invented) and fill two columns: **what was lost** and **what was gained.** Worked example:
   *"A new road: lost — the quiet field where we played; gained — the market can come and
   go, and the clinic is reachable."* Both are true.
4. **Who feels it most? (10 min).** Share with a partner. Then ask: does this change help
   everyone equally? Often it helps some and burdens others — the shopkeeper gains, the
   family whose field was cut loses. Naming who carries the cost is part of caring about the
   change.
5. **Close (5 min).** Change is not something to merely accept or to merely resist. It is
   something to understand: what is lost, what is gained, and who carries each. Tomorrow you
   will turn to action — how people care for one another when things are hard.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-chosen change and one loss and one gain already filled; ask the
  learner to add a second gain.
- **Extension:** Write one paragraph arguing *for* the change and one arguing *for what was
  lost*, then one sentence holding both together.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one loss and one gain from the
  same change, and say who might feel each most?
- **Self-check:** The learner checks that both columns are about the *same* change and that
  neither column is empty.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what their neighborhood or village was like when they were young, and
listen for one thing they miss and one thing they are glad changed.
