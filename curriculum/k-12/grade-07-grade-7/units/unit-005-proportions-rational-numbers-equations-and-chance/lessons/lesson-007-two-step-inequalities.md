# Lesson 7 — Two-Step Inequalities

## Summary

Learners write a **two-step inequality** for a real situation ("at most", "at least", "no more
than") and solve it with the same reverse-order undoing as equations, learning when to **flip the
sign**. They graph the solution set on a number line and check a boundary and a non-boundary
value.

## Objectives

- Write and solve a two-step inequality for a real situation and graph its solution set.
  _(D05.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

A family can spend *at most* $11 on snacks, and each snack costs $2 plus a $3 flat delivery fee.
How many snacks can they buy? The unknown *x* must satisfy **2x + 3 ≤ 11**. Solving gives
**x ≤ 4** — they can buy up to 4 snacks, and 4, 3, 2, 1, or 0 all work. Unlike an equation (one
answer), an inequality names a whole *range* of fair answers. "At least" and "at most" limits —
budgets, capacities, age limits, weight limits on a bridge — are everywhere, and they turn into
inequalities. Fair rules often live on one side of a line; inequalities are how we name that side.

## Materials

- Inequality number lines
- Inequality problem cards
- Counters

## Preparation

- Copy inequality number lines and problem cards.
- Have worked examples ready: 2x + 3 ≤ 11 → x ≤ 4; 3x − 4 > 14 → x > 6.
- Recall from Lesson 6: two-step equations and reverse-order undoing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: (1) an **inequality** says one
side is greater/less than (or equal to) the other, so it has a *range* of solutions, not one; (2)
solve it exactly like a two-step equation — undo in reverse order on both sides; (3) **flip the
sign** when you multiply or divide both sides by a negative; and (4) graph with a **closed circle**
for ≤ or ≥ (boundary included) and an **open circle** for < or > (boundary not included), shading
the solution side. Because solving inequalities is a foundational skill, use explicit instruction,
worked examples, and guided practice before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).
Watch for the slips of forgetting to flip the sign when dividing by a negative, and of using an
open circle for "≤". Retrieval: ask learners to recall two-step equations — the solving is the same;
only the answer (a range) and the sign-flip rule differ. The egalitarian lens (`docs/philosophy.md`
§4): inequalities name limits ("at most", "at least") so rules and budgets can be stated and
checked fairly by everyone. The critical-thinking lens (`docs/philosophy.md` §7): testing a
boundary value and one inside value is honest checking of a range.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time, one equation gave one answer. Today the answer is a whole range —
   "at most this much" or "at least that much."
2. **Meet the inequality (10 min).** **2x + 3 ≤ 11** reads "2x + 3 is less than or equal to 11."
   The solution is not one number but every number that makes it true. "≤" means "at most"; "≥"
   means "at least."
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Solve **2x + 3 ≤ 11**. Undo in reverse: subtract 3 from both
   sides → **2x ≤ 8**; divide both sides by 2 → **x ≤ 4**. Graph: a **closed** circle at 4 (because
   "≤" includes 4), shading everything to the left.
4. **Worked example — flip the sign (10 min).** Solve **−2x + 3 < 11**. Subtract 3 → −2x < 8.
   Divide both sides by **−2** — because you divided by a negative, **flip the sign**: **x > −4**.
   Check with a value: x = 0 (which is > −4): −2(0) + 3 = 3 < 11 ✓.
5. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take your inequality cards: write each as an inequality,
   solve it (flipping the sign when dividing by a negative), graph the solution, and check one
   boundary and one inside value. Trade and check each other.
6. **Close (5 min).** An inequality's answer is a range. Solve like an equation, flip the sign when
   dividing by a negative, and graph with a closed circle for ≤ or ≥, an open circle for < or >.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only "≤" and "≥" inequalities with positive coefficients, graphing on a ready
  number line; describe each step aloud so learners with low vision can follow by listening.
- **Extension:** Solve inequalities that require flipping the sign and write an inequality for a
  two-sided situation of the learner's own (e.g., a minimum and a maximum together).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner write a two-step inequality from a situation,
  solve it (including the sign-flip rule), and graph the solution set with the correct circle?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I flip the sign when I divided by a negative? Is my
  circle closed for ≤/≥ and open for </>? Did I shade the correct side, and does a boundary and an
  inside value both check?"

## Home connection

At home, find a real "at most" or "at least" limit — a budget, a count, a weight or age limit —
write it as an inequality, solve it, and name the whole range of answers it allows.

## Resources

- Solving and graphing two-step inequalities is standard in middle-grades mathematics; see John A.
  Van de Walle, *Elementary and Middle School Mathematics* (10th ed., 2019). The sign-flip rule
  follows from the order properties of real numbers (multiplying by a negative reverses order).
  The claim that limits stated as inequalities let everyone check rules fairly is a value
  commitment; testing boundary values is a critical-thinking practice (`docs/philosophy.md` §7).
