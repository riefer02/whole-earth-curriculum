# Lesson 3 — Space and Time: The Grammar of Movement

## Summary

Learners meet **spacing** and **timing** — the "grammar" that makes movement and games work.
They practice finding open space, and moving at the right moment, through simple drills and a
small game, then name where they used each one.

## Objectives

- Apply strategies such as spacing, timing, and teamwork in a game or sport. _(D03.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

Watch any good game — a football match in Brazil, a cricket field in India, a game of tag in
a schoolyard anywhere — and you will see the same two hidden rules at work. Players who are
clumped together get in each other's way; players who spread out find room to move. And the
best pass is not the fastest one but the one thrown at the *right moment* — a second too
early and it is wasted, a second too late and the opening is gone. Spacing and timing are
not talent you are born with; they are grammar you can learn, and they work in every game on
Earth.

## Materials

- Space and time diagram
- Cones or markers
- A soft ball (or rolled-paper ball)

## Preparation

- Mark out a playing area with cones or natural boundaries.
- Print or draw the space and time diagram.
- Clear a safe space to move in.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **spacing and timing
are learnable strategies, not inborn gifts** — and that everyone, of every body and ability,
uses them. Model each drill as a worked example (find the open space yourself; freeze on the
count; show "too early / on time / too late") before learners try it (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Adapt every activity so all bodies can join: spacing can be done by walking,
rolling, wheeling, or pointing; timing can be marked by a nod or a hand signal as well as a
run. Keep it cooperative — the goal is to *help the group find space and time*, never to
eliminate anyone. See `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have been reading your body's internal signals. Now
   your body gets to *move*. Think of a game you love: where do players stand — bunched up
   or spread out? That is **spacing**.
2. **Meet spacing (10 min).** Look at the diagram. **Spacing** is spreading out so every
   player has room and no one crowds another. Drill: with a partner or a small group, walk
   around the marked area. On "freeze," check — are you all spread out, or bunched? Move
   again until everyone is in open space.
3. **Meet timing (10 min).** **Timing** is moving or passing at the exact right moment, not
   before and not after. Drill: stand in a circle and pass a soft ball. The catch works only
   if the pass comes *when* the receiver is ready — not too early, not too late. Try it at
   a slow tempo, then a faster one. Feel "on time."
4. **Put them together (15 min).** Play a short, cooperative game: the whole group's job is
   to keep a ball moving to everyone in the area, using open space and well-timed passes,
   with nobody left out. Count how many clean passes you can make together.
5. **Close (5 min).** Name it: where did you use spacing today? Where did you use timing?
   You will add the third piece — teamwork — next time.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice spacing first (just spread out), then add timing only after spacing
  feels steady; allow walking pace throughout.
- **Extension:** Add *anticipation*: read where the space will open *before* it does, and
  move a beat early. Explain how that changes the game.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name and demonstrate spacing (finding open
  space) and timing (moving or passing at the right moment) in the drill or game?
- **Performance (peer):** A partner watches one round and reports one moment of good spacing
  and one moment of good timing they saw.

## Home connection

Watch any game — on a screen, at a park, or in a neighbor's yard — and name one moment of
good spacing and one moment of good timing. Say why each one worked.
