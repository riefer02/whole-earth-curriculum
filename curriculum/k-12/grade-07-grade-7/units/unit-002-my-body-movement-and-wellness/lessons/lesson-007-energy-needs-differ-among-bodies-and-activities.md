# Lesson 7 — Energy Needs Differ Among Bodies and Activities

## Summary

Learners discover that **energy needs differ** among bodies and activities — bigger bodies and
more active days need more fuel, and a growing body needs extra fuel on top of its daily
activity. They match bodies-and-activities to fuel amounts and name their own day, without
counting, judging, or ranking any body.

## Objectives

- Describe how energy needs differ among bodies and activities, and why restricting food can
  harm a growing body. _(D03.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

Food is **fuel**. Just as a truck carrying a heavy load uses more fuel than a bicycle on a flat
road, a bigger body and a more active day use more energy than a smaller body at rest — and a
body that is *growing* is like a building under construction: it needs extra material and extra
fuel on top of everything else. There is no single "right" amount of food for every person,
because bodies and days differ — the farmer hauling sacks, the student walking kilometers to
school, and the fisher paddling at dawn all need different amounts. Energy needs are
individual and real, not a number to compete over.

## Materials

- Fuel gauge chart
- Energy needs page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fuel gauge chart (three bars of different heights, clearly labeled).
- Copy an energy needs page for each learner.
- Arrange a space where learners can also feel the difference between rest and movement.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **energy needs are
individual and differ by body size and activity — and this is neutral science, never a ranking
of bodies**. The evidence: human energy requirements vary with age, sex, body size, and
physical activity level, and rise steeply during the adolescent growth spurt, when energy is
spent on growth itself (FAO/WHO/UNU, 2004). Keep it strictly non-judgmental: a bigger body
needing more fuel is a fact, not a comment on anyone; no learner should be asked to count
calories, weigh themselves, or compare bodies. Use "different, not better or worse" as the
refrain. This lesson sets up the next one honestly: because a growing body needs fuel,
restricting food harms it. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have been reading signals and moving your body. Today
   you meet what powers all of it: **energy** from food. Think: what did your body do today
   that needed energy?
2. **Meet the fuel gauge (10 min).** Look at the fuel gauge chart. Three bars: a **smaller
   body on a restful day** needs less fuel; a **bigger body on a restful day** needs more; a
   body on a **very active day** needs the most. Why? More body to run means more energy; more
   movement means more energy. And there is one more bar hiding in the picture: a **growing
   body** needs *extra* fuel, because growth itself costs energy.
3. **Match bodies and activities (15 min).** On your page, match each body-and-activity to a
   fuel amount: resting and reading; walking to school; hauling water; training hard for a
   match; being mid-growth-spurt. Explain your matches to a partner.
4. **Name your own day (10 min).** Write one sentence: *Today my body ___ (rested / moved a
   little / moved a lot), so it needs ___ fuel — enough, not too little.* Share if you are
   comfortable.
5. **Close (5 min).** Different bodies, different days, different fuel — and none of it is a
   ranking. Tomorrow you will ask the important next question: what happens if a growing body
   is denied the fuel it needs?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a simpler two-bar version (rest day vs active day) and the sentence starter
  "When I ___, my body needs ___."
- **Extension:** Connect to the growth spurt from the last lesson: explain *why* a growing
  body needs extra fuel even on a restful day.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain why a bigger or more active body needs
  more fuel, and name the extra fuel a growing body needs?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their own-day sentence and checks that it names both the
  activity and the fuel need — enough, not too little.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what their body did today and notice together how a more active day means
more fuel. Share your own-day sentence if you like.
