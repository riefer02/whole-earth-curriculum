# Lesson 8 — Fueling a Growing Body: Why Restriction Harms

## Summary

Learners learn **why restricting food harms a growing body** — slowed growth, weaker bones,
tiredness, and trouble concentrating — and that eating disorders are serious, treatable
illnesses, never a choice or a failure. They close by naming one way to fuel their own growing
body and one trusted adult to talk to, with no shame and no judgment of any body.

## Objectives

- Describe how energy needs differ among bodies and activities, and why restricting food can
  harm a growing body. _(D03.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

You learned that a growing body is a building under construction, needing extra fuel. So what
happens if the building is denied its materials? The work slows or stops. A growing body that
is not given enough food can grow more slowly, build weaker bones, feel tired and dizzy, and
find it harder to focus and remember. Sometimes this happens not because food is unavailable
but because a person, often in pain about their body, restricts what they eat on purpose — and
that can become an **eating disorder**, a serious illness that is *not* a choice, not a
weakness, and not anyone's fault. The honest, hopeful truth: eating disorders are treatable,
and asking for help early is one of the bravest things a person can do.

## Materials

- Fueled vs restricted diagram
- My fuel-and-care page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fueled-vs-restricted diagram.
- Copy a "My fuel-and-care page" for each learner.
- Prepare (privately) a list of trusted adults and local or national helplines to offer anyone
  who asks — never read aloud as a class list.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It must be handled with tenderness and without
any hint of blame. The facts to land: undernutrition during the growing years can delay growth
and puberty, weaken bones, and cause tiredness and poor concentration; eating disorders are
serious illnesses with both mental and physical harm, and they are treatable (Nemours
KidsHealth, 2022; WHO, 2026). The values to hold *as values, distinct from those facts*: every
body deserves dignity; no body is a failure; and help is always worth asking for. Never
comment on any learner's body or eating, never teach calorie-counting or "good/bad food"
language, and never require disclosure. Watch for distress; if a learner shares that they or a
friend may be struggling, respond calmly, thank them for their courage, and connect them to a
trusted adult or support service in private — do not promise secrecy. Note that media and
advertising constantly push narrow, edited images of bodies; naming that pressure is part of
caring for oneself. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You learned that energy needs differ among bodies and
   activities, and that a growing body needs extra fuel. Today you ask the honest question:
   what happens if a growing body is denied the fuel it needs?
2. **Meet the two panels (10 min).** Look at the fueled-vs-restricted diagram. **Fueled**, a
   growing body gets the food and energy it needs: it grows, plays, and feels strong.
   **Restricted**, the same body is denied fuel: growth slows, bones weaken, the body feels
   tired and dizzy, and thinking gets foggy. This is what restriction *does* — it is never a
   comment on any person's worth.
3. **Name it without blame (10 min).** When a person restricts food on purpose, especially
   because they are in pain about their body, it can become an **eating disorder**. Say it
   plainly: an eating disorder is a serious illness — like any illness — and it is *not* a
   choice, not a weakness, and not anyone's fault. The good news is that it is treatable, and
   catching it early helps a person recover.
4. **Write your fuel-and-care (10 min).** On your page, write: one way you will fuel your
   growing body (enough food and water, not too little); one way you already care for your
   body; and one **trusted adult** you could talk to if you or a friend ever felt food or body
   worry was taking over. This is private — you do not have to share it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your body is not a project to shrink or fix; it is the place you live,
   and it is growing on purpose. If food or body worries ever feel too heavy, that is a signal
   to ask a trusted adult — and asking is strength, not weakness.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sentence starters "One way I fuel my body is ___. A trusted adult I can
  talk to is ___." Keep the focus on fueling and asking for help.
- **Extension:** Separate fact from value: write one *fact* about restriction from the lesson
  and one *value* about how people deserve to be treated, and label each clearly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain, without blame, why restriction harms a
  growing body and name one trusted adult to talk to?
- **Portfolio:** The "fuel-and-care" note (one way to fuel, one way they care for their body,
  one trusted adult), kept privately as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, share with a trusted adult one thing you learned about why a growing body
needs fuel. You do not need to share anything personal. Remember: a trusted adult is always a
good person to talk to.
