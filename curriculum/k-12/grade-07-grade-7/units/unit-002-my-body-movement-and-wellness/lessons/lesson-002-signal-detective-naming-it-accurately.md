# Lesson 2 — Signal Detective: Naming It Accurately

## Summary

Learners practice the *accuracy* half of reading signals: telling hunger, fatigue, and stress
apart from the feelings they are easily confused with. They sort **scenario cards**, discover
the **clues** that distinguish one signal from another, and rename a real moment of their own
more accurately.

## Objectives

- Notice and name internal body signals such as hunger, fatigue, and stress accurately in
  myself. _(D03.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Sometimes the body's message gets mistranslated. You reach for food when you are actually
just tired, or bored, or thirsty. You think you are lazy when you are really running on four
hours of sleep. You feel a racing heart before a performance and call it fear when it might
be excitement — the two feel almost the same. Naming a signal *accurately* matters because
the helpful response is different: thirst wants water, fatigue wants rest, stress wants a
slow breath. Whether you live beside a busy road or a quiet river, learning to read the
message before you act on it is a skill that serves every life.

## Materials

- Signal detective chart
- Signal detective cards
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the signal detective chart (three rows: hunger, fatigue, stress — each with
  "what it feels like" and "what it is confused with").
- Copy and cut one set of signal detective cards per pair.
- Arrange a space where learners can sort cards and, for the low-tech variant, move to three
  corners.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **accuracy is a
skill you can practice** — a signal can be misread, and the point is not to be perfect but to
get better at listening. Keep it shame-free: misreading a signal is ordinary, never a
failing. Model the worked example ("I thought I was hungry; the clue was that water fixed
it — so it was thirst") before pairs sort independently (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).
Remind learners that every body differs — one person's hunger feels like a hollow stomach,
another's like a headache — so the "clue" is always *their own* body's pattern. This is
egalitarian at its root: there is no single correct body, so there is no single correct
clue-list. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Yesterday you named three signals: hunger, fatigue, and
   stress. Without looking, say them again and point to where each one lives in your body.
   Today you become a **signal detective**: your job is to tell them apart.
2. **Meet the clues (10 min).** Look at the signal detective chart. **Hunger** usually builds
   slowly and lives in the stomach; it is easily confused with **thirst** (dry mouth, also
   eased by a drink) and **boredom** (an urge to do something, not an empty stomach).
   **Fatigue** (heavy eyes, heavy body) is easily confused with **stress** (which can also
   make you feel drained). **Stress** (fast heart, tight chest) is easily confused with
   **excitement** — both speed the heart; the clue is whether the situation is one you are
   dreading or one you are looking forward to. Worked example: *I felt like eating, but the
   clue was a dry mouth — it was thirst.*
3. **Sort the cards (15 min).** With a partner, sort the scenario cards under the signal
   that names each one most accurately. When you disagree, say *why* — name the clue. Check
   your sorts against the chart.
4. **Rename a real moment (10 min).** Think of one moment this week when you may have
   misnamed a signal — "I thought I was ___ , but the clue was ___ , so it was ___ ." Write
   it down. Share with a partner if you are comfortable.
5. **Close (5 min).** Misreading a signal is not a mistake to hide; it is information about
   how to listen better next time. The more accurately you name it, the more helpfully you
   can respond.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort a smaller set (two signals at a time) and use the chart as a checklist;
  provide the sentence frame "I thought ___, but the clue was ___, so it was ___."
- **Extension:** Write one new "tricky" scenario where two signals feel nearly the same, and
  name the single clue that would settle it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer):** In pairs, learners check each other's sorts against the chart and
  name the clue for any card that was hard.
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their "rename a real moment" sentence and checks that
  it names both the first (mistaken) reading and the more accurate one, plus a clue.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they misread their own body — thought they were hungry when
they were thirsty, or tired when they were stressed. What clue told them the difference?
