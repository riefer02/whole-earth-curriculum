# Lesson 6 — My Changing Body in Motion

## Summary

Learners describe how movement changes during puberty — lengthening limbs, a shifting center of
gravity, and changing strength and coordination — and, crucially, how to **adapt** activities
to their own changing body. Growth is named as normal, uneven, and never shameful, and every
body is honored.

## Objectives

- Describe how movement changes during puberty and adapt activities to my own changing body.
  _(D03.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

During these years your body is not just getting taller — it is *rearranging itself*, and that
changes how it moves. Your arms and legs lengthen faster than you are used to, so a reach you
once knew now overshoots; your center of gravity shifts as you grow, so balance feels
different for a while; strength and coordination come at their own uneven pace. This is not
something going wrong — it is the ordinary work of growth, and it happens at a different time
and speed for every person. The skill is not to stop growing; it is to *adapt the activity to
the body you have today* — the same way every athlete, dancer, and worker does all their life.

## Materials

- Changing-body diagram
- Movement adaptation cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the changing-body diagram.
- Copy and cut the movement adaptation cards.
- Arrange a space with room to try a few simple movements at different reaches and tempos.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Hold it with care: **growth during puberty is
normal, uneven, and never shameful — and no learner's body should be singled out or compared.**
The science to land: during the adolescent growth spurt the long bones lengthen, which shifts
the body's center of gravity and temporarily changes coordination, while strength catches up
at its own pace (WHO, 2024; WHO, 2006). Name every pace as normal — growth begins at different
times and speeds for different people, and a wide range of heights and shapes is normal, not a
ranking. Frame adaptation as *skill*, never limitation: adapting is what skilled movers do.
Avoid measuring anyone publicly or commenting on any individual's body; keep all examples
general. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/development.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have been moving with strategy. Today you notice
   something that changes *how* you move: your own growing body. Think: has a movement you
   used to do easily felt a little different lately? (No need to say it aloud.)
2. **Meet the changes (10 min).** Look at the changing-body diagram. During **puberty** the
   body goes through a **growth spurt**: arms and legs lengthen, so your reach changes; your
   **center of gravity** — the point the body balances around — shifts as you grow taller, so
   balance feels different; strength and **coordination** catch up at their own pace. All of
   this is normal, and it happens at a different time for every person.
3. **Match changes to adaptations (15 min).** Sort the adaptation cards: longer limbs → adjust
   your spacing and reach; shifting balance → widen your stance and slow the tempo while you
   relearn; changing strength → pick the load and pace that fit *today*; shifting coordination
   → practice a skill a few times rather than expecting it to feel instantly familiar. Try one
   of these adaptations with a partner — a reach, a balance, or a throw.
4. **Adapt an activity of your own (10 min).** Choose one movement or game you like. Name one
   change you have noticed in your body, and write one adaptation you will try. Example: *My
   reach has changed, so I will adjust my spacing and take one extra practice before I aim.*
5. **Close (5 min).** Growing is not a problem to fix; it is the body doing its job. Adapting
   to the body you have today is a skill every good mover uses — and it will serve you for
   life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sentence frame "My body has changed in ___ , so I will ___ ." and match
  one change to one adaptation at a time.
- **Extension:** Design an adaptation for a *specific* activity (a dance step, a throw, a
  climb) and explain the body change it responds to and why it helps.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one way movement changes during puberty and
  describe one adaptation to their own body?
- **Portfolio:** The written "movement-adaptation reflection" (one change + one adaptation),
  kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

Ask a trusted adult or older sibling about a time their own body changed and a movement felt
different — what did they adapt, and how did it turn out? Share one adaptation you wrote today.
