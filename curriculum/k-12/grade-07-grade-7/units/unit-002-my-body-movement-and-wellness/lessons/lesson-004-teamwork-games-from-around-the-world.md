# Lesson 4 — Teamwork: Games from Around the World

## Summary

Learners meet teamwork through three team games from different world regions — **kabaddi** and
**kho-kho** from South Asia, and **sepak takraw** from Southeast Asia. They learn each game as
a living tradition, play simple adapted versions that include every body, and name the
teamwork — shared roles, communication, helping each other — that makes each one work.

## Objectives

- Apply strategies such as spacing, timing, and teamwork in a game or sport. _(D03.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

People everywhere have invented games, and almost all of them are about *teamwork* — not one
hero, but a group that shares roles and helps each other. In **kabaddi**, a game played across
South Asia, one raider crosses into the other side chanting "kabaddi, kabaddi" on a single
breath, trying to tag defenders and get back before the defending side — working together —
can catch them. In **sepak takraw**, played across
Southeast Asia, three players pass a rattan ball over a net using feet and knees, so no one
person can win alone. These are not "simple" games — they are living knowledge passed from one
generation to the next, and every one of them runs on the same teamwork you use in your own
neighborhood. Each game also grew up in a particular place — a dusty field, a courtyard, a
cleared beach — so the game fits the land, and the land shapes the game.

## Materials

- Games-of-the-world cards
- Cones, a soft ball, a small ring or tied cloth

## Preparation

- Print or draw the games-of-the-world cards.
- Set up three simple playing areas, one per game.
- Read the adapted, low-contact rules below and pick which versions fit your space and group.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **teamwork is a
universal human skill — sharing roles, communicating, and helping each other — and games from
many cultures are one living proof of it**. Present each game as a real tradition with
respect, never as a curiosity or a ranking: name its home region, and note that rules vary
from place to place and are best learned from people who play it (the cards are a starting
point, not the last word). The three games here are a sample, not the whole map: to span
more continents, add one from Africa, Oceania, or the Americas — for example, lacrosse of the
Indigenous nations of North America, kilikiti of Samoa, or genna of Ethiopia — and present it
with the same respect: name its home, note that rules vary, and learn from people who play
it. Adapt every game so all bodies and abilities join — tag can be a
touch, a tap, or a call; kicking can be a pass with any part of the body; no one is forced
into contact. Play cooperatively: the aim is for the *whole group* to experience each game,
not to win. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have learned spacing and timing. Now add the third
   piece: **teamwork** — sharing roles and helping each other. Name a team you have been part
   of. What made it work?
2. **Meet three games (10 min).** Look at the games-of-the-world cards. **Kabaddi** (South
   Asia): a raider crosses the line chanting on one breath, must touch a defender and get
   back before being caught; the defending side works together to catch the raider. **Kho-kho** (South Asia): chasers take turns chasing
   runners around a row, passing the chase by touch — pure timing and teamwork. **Sepak
   takraw** (Southeast Asia): three players keep a rattan ball up over a net with feet and
   knees, passing to set each other up. Each game needs roles, talk, and help.
3. **Play adapted versions (20 min).** In small groups, try each game in a simple, safe form:
   a gentle tag-and-return for kabaddi, a take-turns chase for kho-kho, and keep-the-ball-up
   passes for sepak takraw (no contact, no one left out). Rotate so everyone tries all three.
4. **Name the teamwork (10 min).** After each game, stop and name it: what role did each
   person play? How did you communicate? Where did one person help another? Write one word
   or sentence for each game.
5. **Close (5 min).** The rules differ on every continent, but the teamwork is the same
   language. You will now use spacing, timing, *and* teamwork together in a game of your own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Play one game at a time with simplified roles; allow a walking pace and a
  "tag by calling the name" option for anyone who prefers no contact.
- **Extension:** Invent one small rule change to a game that makes it more inclusive, and
  explain how it changes the teamwork needed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner take a role, communicate with teammates, and
  help another player during the adapted games?
- **Peer check:** In pairs, name one moment a partner used teamwork — a role, a call, or an
  act of help — and say how it helped the group.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a game they played as a child — what region it came from and how
players helped each other. Share the three games you met today.
