# Lesson 1 — Reading My Body's Signals

## Summary

Learners notice and name three internal body signals — **hunger**, **fatigue**, and
**stress** — accurately in themselves. They do a slow **body scan**, learn what each signal
feels like in the body, and mark on their own body where each one shows up — without
judging any signal as good or bad.

## Objectives

- Notice and name internal body signals such as hunger, fatigue, and stress accurately in
  myself. _(D03.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

You have probably felt all three of these today without stopping to name them: a hollow or
rumbling feeling in your stomach before a meal, heavy eyelids when you stayed up late, and a
fast heart right before something that mattered. These are not moods or guesses — they are
**signals your body sends**, the way a dashboard light tells a driver that the tank is low.
Whether you live in a high-rise city, a fishing village by the sea, or a mountain farm, the
same three signals speak, and learning to read them accurately is the first step to caring
for the body you live in.

## Materials

- Body signal map
- My body signals page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the body signal map: one body, three labeled signals.
- Copy a "My body signals page" for each learner.
- Arrange a circle or a quiet space for a slow body scan.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **internal body
signals are information, not weaknesses** — hunger, fatigue, and stress are neutral facts
the body reports, and naming them accurately (not "I'm just off today" but "I'm hungry" or
"I'm tired") is a skill. Normalize every body's difference: some people feel hunger as a
hollow stomach, others as a headache or a dip in energy; the *same* signal can feel
different in different bodies, and all of it is normal. Model one body scan as a worked
example — name a signal from a safe, ordinary moment of your own — before learners try their
own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Never ask a learner to reveal a private distress;
invite only small, ordinary signals. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Last year you learned that the mind and the body are one
   thing talking to itself — that a worry can make a stomach feel tight. Today you turn the
   volume up on three signals that run in the background all day. Think: when did you last
   feel truly hungry? What told you?
2. **Meet the three signals (10 min).** Look at the body signal map. **Hunger** — an empty
   or rumbling stomach. **Fatigue** — heavy eyelids, heavy shoulders, a body that wants
   rest. **Stress** — a fast heart, a tight chest, quick shallow breaths. Worked example:
   *This morning I noticed my stomach felt hollow around mid-morning. That was hunger, not
   boredom.* Say each signal and point to where it lives in your own body.
3. **Body scan (10 min).** Sit still and close your eyes or look down. Slowly move your
   attention from your head to your toes. As you pass your stomach, ask: *hunger?* As you
   pass your eyes and shoulders, ask: *fatigue?* As you pass your chest, ask: *stress?*
   Notice what is actually there — not what you expect.
4. **Name it (15 min).** On your page, mark where each of the three signals shows up in
   *your* body. Finish one sentence for each: *I know I am hungry when ___. I know I am
   tired when ___. I know I am stressed when ___.* Share one with a partner if you are
   comfortable.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every body sends these signals, and no signal is a failure. Naming
   them accurately is the first move of caring for yourself. Tomorrow you will practice
   telling one signal apart from another.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the sentence starters and a picture bank of the three signals; match
  each signal to a body part together first.
- **Extension:** For each signal, add one *early* warning sign — the small version that
  shows up before the signal is strong — and one helpful response.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name each of the three signals and point to
  where it shows up in their own body?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their three sentences and checks that each names a
  body feeling (not just "I felt bad") and a real place in the body.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home where they feel hunger, tiredness, and stress in their own body, and
share one small example of your own. Notice: does the same signal show up differently in
different people?
