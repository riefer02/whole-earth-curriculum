# Lesson 7 — Social Media & Mental Health: What It Does to How We Feel

## Summary

Learners analyze how social media can affect **mental health** — the ways it can lift
(connection, learning, creativity) and weigh down (comparison, endless scrolling, lost
sleep) — and learn to notice how it actually makes them feel. Framed as self-awareness and
choice, never as fear or shame about using social media.

## Objectives

- Analyze how social media can affect mental health. _(D03.S4.07.02)_

## Connection

Social media is a tool, and like any tool it can build or it can weigh. It can connect you
to people and ideas across the world — and it can also pull you into comparing yourself to
carefully edited lives, into endless scrolling, and into late nights that steal your rest.
Neither story is the whole truth. The skill is to notice *what it actually does to you* —
because the same app that lifts one person can quietly weigh down another, and the first
step to using it well is seeing it clearly.

## Materials

- Lift-or-weight diagram
- Feeling check-in cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the lift-or-weight diagram.
- Copy the feeling check-in cards.
- Prepare to keep the feeling check-in private; never require a learner to disclose their
  own habits or struggles.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Hold it **without fear-mongering**: social
media is not all bad, and the goal is *awareness and choice*, not guilt. The facts to
land: social media can support connection, learning, and creativity, and it can also be
linked to comparison, disrupted sleep, and negative feelings when use is heavy or
mindless — the WHO notes that adolescence is a critical time for mental health and that
digital habits can shape it (WHO adolescent mental health; UNESCO media and information
literacy). The values held *as values*: a learner is not bad for using social media, and
"balance" is a choice to be made, not a grade to earn. Keep all reflection private and
invite-not-require disclosure; watch for distress and connect anyone who shares that they
are struggling to a trusted adult in private. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Think honestly: how do you usually feel *after* time on
   social media — calm, connected, tired, restless? (No need to say it aloud.) Today you
   look at that question with clear eyes.
2. **Meet the lift and the weight (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Social media can
   **lift**: connection with others, learning and new ideas, creativity and self-
   expression. It can also **weigh down**: comparing ourselves to others, endless
   scrolling, and lost sleep. Both columns are real — the question is which one is bigger
   for *you*, and when.
3. **Analyze the mechanisms (10 min).** Why does it work this way? Feeds are *designed* to
   keep you scrolling; posts are *edited* highlight reels that make real lives look
   perfect; and late-night use can steal the sleep your brain needs to feel steady. Name
   these, and notice they are features, not personal failings.
4. **Do a private check-in (15 min).** Use the feeling cards, or a two-column page: write
   what **lifts** you and what **weighs you down** in your own use. Then note one feeling
   you have after using it. This is private — you do not have to share it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Social media is not the boss of how you feel — unless it is, and
   then noticing is the first step to choosing differently. Awareness is power.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame "Social media lifts me when ___, and weighs me down when
  ___." Focus on naming one of each.
- **Extension:** Distinguish a *fact* (heavy use is linked with worse sleep and more
  comparison) from a *value* (balance is worth choosing), and label each in writing.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one way social media can lift
  and one way it can weigh down mental health, and describe the role of design (scrolling,
  edited posts, sleep)?
- **Portfolio:** A private lift-and-weight reflection note, kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, ask a trusted adult how they notice screens affecting their own mood —
most adults feel the pull too. You do not need to share your own habits, only what you
learned about noticing.
