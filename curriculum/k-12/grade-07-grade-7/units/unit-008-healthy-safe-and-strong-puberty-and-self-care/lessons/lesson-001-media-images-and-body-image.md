# Lesson 1 — Media Images & Body Image: What Are We Really Seeing?

## Summary

Learners discover that most media images of bodies are **chosen, edited, filtered, and
repeated** — not snapshots of real people — and begin to see how this can shape how a
person feels about their own body. Every body is named as real and worthy; no learner's
body is compared or judged.

## Objectives

- Analyze how media images can shape how a person feels about their body. _(D03.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Think of the pictures you see every day — on screens, on packages, on walls, on the
bottles and boxes in a market. Almost none of them are plain snapshots. Someone chose
the person, chose the pose, chose the lighting, and then a computer or an app changed it:
skin smoothed, shape altered, colors pushed, parts removed. Then that one edited image is
copied thousands of times, until a person can start to feel that *their* real, unedited
body is the one that is wrong. It is not. The image is the thing that is not real — and
noticing that is a skill you can build.

## Materials

- Real-people vs media-image diagram
- A few printed or shown ads/images (age-appropriate, varied, global)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the real-people vs media-image diagram.
- Gather a few images of bodies from magazines, posters, or screens — choose varied and
  global examples, and check that none shame any body.
- Prepare to keep every comment about *images*, never about any learner's own body.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Hold it with care: **no learner's body is
ever compared, ranked, or commented on** — every example is about *images*, never about a
person in the room. The factual ground to land: media images of bodies are routinely
constructed — subjects are selected for a narrow "ideal," then digitally altered
(retouched, filtered, re-proportioned) and repeated at scale, so the images people see
over-represent one body type that most real bodies do not match (UNESCO media and
information literacy; WHO adolescent health). The value held *as value, distinct from
that fact*: every body is real and worthy, and no one's body is a problem to fix. Heavy
exposure to idealized, edited images is linked in research to body dissatisfaction, and
the WHO notes that media influence can widen the gap between how adolescents see
themselves and the ideals they are shown (WHO adolescent mental health) — name this as a
*risk to notice*, not as fear. Watch for distress; keep the closing
private and never require disclosure. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and wonder (5 min).** Where do you see pictures of bodies every day — screens,
   walls, packages, markets? List a few places silently. Today's question: are the bodies
   in those pictures real, or made?
2. **Meet the diagram (10 min).** Look at the two panels. On the left, **real people** —
   every body a different height, width, shape, and color, and every one real. On the
   right, **what media often shows** — one narrow shape, edited and repeated three times
   over. What is different between the two sides? (Talk about the *images*, not any
   person.)
3. **Name the tools (10 min).** Images are changed with tools you can name: **choosing**
   (only certain bodies get shown), **filtering** (color and texture changed), **retouching**
   (parts smoothed, slimmed, or removed), and **repeating** (one image copied everywhere).
   For each tool, say what it does and why a company might use it.
4. **Spot it in a real ad (15 min).** With a partner, look at one of the images. Say out
   loud what was probably chosen, filtered, or retouched. Ask the key question: *is this a
   picture of a real person, or a picture of an idea someone is selling?*
5. **Close (5 min).** Your body is real; many images of bodies are not. That is not a
   reason to hate the images — it is a reason to stop measuring yourself against them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the sentence frame "This image was probably ___ (chosen / filtered /
  retouched / repeated), so it is not a plain photo." Focus on naming one tool.
- **Extension:** Write one *fact* about media images (they are edited) and one *value*
  about bodies (every body deserves respect), and label which is which.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two ways a media image is
  changed and explain that an edited image is not a plain snapshot of a real body?
- **Portfolio:** A short note (three ways images are changed + one sentence on what this
  means for how they feel about their own body), kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, talk with a trusted adult about an image you both see often. Together,
name one way it was probably chosen or edited. You do not need to share anything personal
about your own body.
