# Lesson 4 — Evaluating Food Ads: Spotting the Claim, Asking for Evidence

## Summary

Learners evaluate a **whole food advertisement** using a four-part read — claim, feeling
appeal, fine print, and missing evidence — first on a worked example, then on real ads.
Every family's way of eating is honored; evaluation targets the ad, never a person's food
or body.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate food marketing and advertising by telling claims apart from
  evidence. _(D03.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Last lesson you learned the difference between a claim and evidence. Now you put it
together on a *whole* ad. Real advertisements are not just one claim — they are a package
of pieces working together: a big promise up top, a warm feeling in the middle, and tiny
limits down at the bottom. Reading them piece by piece is like reading a map: once you can
label the parts, the ad stops doing its work on you without your permission.

## Materials

- Reading-a-food-ad diagram (worked example)
- Real food ads (varied, age-appropriate)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the reading-a-food-ad diagram.
- Gather several food ads from varied places and countries.
- Prepare to keep all evaluation about *ads*, never about anyone's eating or body.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is a **worked example + guided practice**
structure: model the four-part read on the diagram, then have learners apply it. The four
labels: **claim** (the promise), **feeling appeal** (the emotion the ad stirs — fun,
belonging, warmth, energy), **fine print** (the tiny text that limits the claim), and
**missing evidence** (no tests, no amounts, no independent check shown). The facts: food
marketing uses these tactics worldwide, and marketing to children of foods high in fat,
salt, and sugar is widespread enough that the WHO recommends limits (WHO marketing
recommendations). The values held *as values*: every way of eating is honored, no food is
"good" or "bad," and we evaluate the *message*, never a person. Avoid calorie/diet talk
entirely. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Quick recall: what is the difference between a claim and
   evidence? (A claim is what they say; evidence is what can be checked.) Today you
   evaluate a whole ad.
2. **Worked example (10 min).** Look at the reading-a-food-ad diagram. Walk it together:
   the **claim** is "Fuels your day!"; the **feeling appeal** is the happy sun and warm
   colors; the **fine print** is the tiny "one serving… not a substitute"; and the
   **missing evidence** is that no test or amount is shown. Notice how the pieces work as a
   team.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, take one real ad and label all four
   parts. Ask: what would it take to *prove* the claim? What evidence is the ad not
   showing you? Write or say your four labels.
4. **Independent check (10 min).** Choose a second ad and do the four-part read on your
   own. Then trade verdicts with your partner: did you find the same claim? The same
   missing evidence?
5. **Close (5 min).** You now have a tool you can pull out anywhere — a market, a screen,
   a street. The ad can make its promise; you get to ask for the evidence.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the four-part frame "Claim: ___. Feeling appeal: ___. Fine print: ___.
  Missing evidence: ___." Label one part at a time with a partner.
- **Extension:** Write a full evaluation of one ad and state exactly what evidence would
  settle the question — then compare with a partner.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner label at least three of the four parts
  (claim, appeal, fine print, missing evidence) on a real ad and name one missing piece of
  evidence?
- **Portfolio:** The four-part food-ad evaluation, kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, evaluate one food ad you see this week — on a package, a screen, or a
wall — with a trusted adult. Name the claim and the evidence together. Keep it about the
ad, never about anyone's eating.
