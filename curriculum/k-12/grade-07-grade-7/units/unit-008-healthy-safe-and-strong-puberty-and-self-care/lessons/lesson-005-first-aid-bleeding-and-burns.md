# Lesson 5 — First Aid Basics: Bleeding & Burns

## Summary

Learners explain basic first aid for **bleeding** — cover and press with a clean cloth —
and for **burns** — cool under cool running water, never ice — and, crucially, when to get
adult or medical help. First aid is taught factually and safely, with the emphasis on
getting help when unsure.

## Objectives

- Explain basic first aid for bleeding and burns, and when to seek adult or medical help.
  _(D03.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

An emergency is not something you plan for — it is something that happens in an ordinary
moment: a cut in the kitchen, a burn from a pan, a bad scrape on the road. In those first
seconds, knowing two or three calm, correct actions — and knowing when to get help — can
keep a small injury from becoming a big one. First aid is not about being a hero; it is
about being *ready*, and about being willing to ask for help.

## Materials

- Bleeding-and-burns first-aid diagram
- First-aid scenario cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the bleeding-and-burns first-aid diagram.
- Copy and cut the scenario cards.
- Arrange space to practice the motions safely; all practice is on imagined injuries only.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Teach first aid **factually and safely**,
and lead with the rule: **when unsure, get an adult or medical help.** The facts to land:
for bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth and keep pressing, and get medical
help if bleeding is heavy, spurting, or will not stop; for burns, cool the burn under cool
running water (not ice) for about 20 minutes if possible, remove tight items such as rings,
do not pop blisters, and get medical help for large or deep burns or burns to the face,
hands, feet, or joints (IFRC Global First Aid Reference Centre; WHO burns fact sheet). Do
not teach advanced or uncertain techniques; this age needs the safe basics plus the help-
seeking reflex. Reassure learners that getting help is part of first aid, not a failure.
Remind them that hands-on practice with a certified instructor (Red Cross/Red Crescent or
equivalent) is the best way to build confidence. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Think of a time someone got a small cut or a burn. What
   did people do first? Today you learn the calm, correct first steps.
2. **Bleeding (15 min).** Look at the bleeding panel. The steps: **cover** the cut with a
   clean cloth, **press firmly** and keep pressing, and **get adult or medical help** if the
   bleeding is heavy or will not stop. Practice the motions with a partner on an imagined
   cut — cloth on, press, hold. Say aloud when you would get help.
3. **Burns (15 min).** Look at the burns panel. The steps: **cool** the burn under cool
   running water — never ice, which can harm the skin — for about 20 minutes if you can;
   **remove** tight items like rings; and **get adult or medical help** for large or deep
   burns or burns to the face, hands, or feet. Never pop a blister. Practice the motions.
4. **Scenario practice (10 min).** Draw a scenario card. Decide the steps and — always —
   whether to get help. Share your reasoning with a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** First aid is calm + correct + ask for help. You now know the first
   moves for two of the most common injuries — and that asking for help is always part of
   the plan.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frames "For bleeding I ___, then ___." and "For a burn I ___, never
  ___." Practice one injury at a time.
- **Extension:** Explain *why* each step works (pressure slows bleeding; cool water cools
  the burn and eases pain; ice can damage skin further) and name a case where help is
  essential.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the steps for bleeding and for burns,
  and name when to get adult or medical help?
- **Portfolio:** A first-aid note (steps for bleeding + burns + when to get help), kept as
  a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, walk through the steps with a trusted adult at home — where are the
clean cloths, and who would you call for help? Knowing the plan ahead of time is part of
being ready.
