# Lesson 3 — Food Marketing: Claims vs. Evidence

## Summary

Learners distinguish a **food-marketing claim** — a promise designed to sell — from
**evidence** that can be checked, and begin to ask the questions that separate them.
Every family's way of eating is honored; no food is labeled good or bad, and no body is
judged.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate food marketing and advertising by telling claims apart from
  evidence. _(D03.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Walk through any market, shop, or screen full of food and you will hear promises:
"gives you energy," "natural," "just like homemade," "makes you strong." Some promises
are true; some are only partly true; some are just a feeling dressed up as a fact. The
difference between them is not a secret — it is a question you can learn to ask: **what
is the claim, and what is the evidence?** A claim is what someone *says*; evidence is
what can be *checked*.

## Materials

- Claim-vs-evidence diagram
- Food packages or ads (varied, clean, age-appropriate)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the claim-vs-evidence diagram.
- Gather a few empty packages, labels, or printed food ads from varied places.
- Prepare to keep comments about *products*, never about anyone's eating or body.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The core skill is exactly the standard:
**tell a claim apart from evidence.** The facts to land: food marketing worldwide makes
health-linked promises (energy, growth, naturalness, strength) that are often emotional
appeals rather than evidence, and advertising aimed at young people is widespread and
powerful — which is why the WHO recommends limits on marketing foods high in fat, salt,
and sugar to children (WHO marketing recommendations; WHO healthy diet). The values held
*as values*: every family's way of eating is honored, and no food is "good" or "bad" — the
lesson evaluates *claims and evidence*, never a person's food. Keep all examples about
products, never about any learner's own eating, body, or family. Avoid calorie or diet
talk entirely. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Think of a food ad you know by heart — a jingle, a
   mascot, a slogan. What promise does it make? Today you learn to test that promise.
2. **Meet claim vs evidence (10 min).** Look at the diagram. A **claim** is what the
   package *says*: "makes you strong!" **Evidence** is what can be *checked*: what is
   actually in it, what the label lists, what independent tests show, and who is saying it
   and why. Say the difference in one sentence: *a claim is what they say; evidence is
   what can be checked.*
3. **Practice the evidence questions (15 min).** With a partner, pick one package or ad.
   Read its claim aloud, then ask the four evidence questions from the diagram. Does the
   package *show* you the evidence, or does it only *promise*? Talk about what you would
   need to check to find out.
4. **Name the appeal (10 min).** Many food claims appeal to a feeling — fun, belonging,
   energy, being "natural" — rather than to facts. Name the feeling an ad is selling.
   (Remember: the ad is the thing being judged, never the food or the person.)
5. **Close (5 min).** You do not have to distrust every food — you only need to know the
   difference between a promise and a fact. Asking for evidence is a skill you will use
   for life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the frame "The claim is ___. The evidence I would need is ___." Focus
  on naming one claim and one evidence question.
- **Extension:** Compare two products' claims against their labels and write which claim
  is better supported by evidence, and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a claim and ask at least one evidence
  question, and explain the difference between a promise and a checkable fact?
- **Portfolio:** A claim-versus-evidence note (one product's claim + the evidence
  questions they would ask), kept as a portfolio artifact.

## Home connection

If it feels safe, look at one food package at home with a trusted adult. Find the claim
and read the label together, then decide what evidence you would want. Keep it about the
package, never about anyone's eating.
