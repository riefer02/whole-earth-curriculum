# Lesson 4 — The Spread of Paper

## Summary

Learners apply the many-causes/many-consequences tool to a real world change: the
**spread of paper**. Invented in China around the 2nd century BCE and improved there,
paper traveled west along trade routes to Central Asia, Baghdad, and Europe. They
analyze why it spread and what it changed.

## Objectives

- Analyze the multiple causes and consequences of the spread of paper as one major
  change. _(D08.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Think about what you would write on if there were no paper and no screens — a leaf? a
strip of bark? smooth clay? People did all of these, and each had a problem: leaves
crumble, bark is heavy, clay is bulky. Then, in China, people learned to make a thin,
light, cheap sheet from beaten plant fibers — paper. That one idea did not stay in one
place. It traveled, and as it traveled it changed every society it reached. How and
why it spread is a story with many causes and many consequences — exactly the tool we
built yesterday.

## Materials

- Paper-spread map sequence
- Cause and consequence cards

## Preparation

- Copy the map sequence and cards.
- Retrieval: recall the many-causes/many-consequences diagram from Lesson 3; today we
  fill it with real history.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) paper was
invented in China (fragments date to the 2nd century BCE; traditionally improved and
standardized by Cai Lun around 105 CE); (2) it spread west — to Korea and Japan, then
along the Silk Roads to Central Asia (Samarkand), to Baghdad, and on to Europe by the
12th–15th centuries; (3) causes of the spread include cheaper/better material than
bamboo, silk, or parchment, rising demand for records, the trade routes themselves,
and — by tradition — papermakers captured at the Battle of Talas (751 CE) carrying the
skill; (4) consequences include more books, wider literacy, cheaper record-keeping and
administration, and, later, printing (movable type developed in China).** Model the
first stop explicitly, then let learners complete the rest (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). These are established historical facts (see Encyclopaedia Britannica, "paper").
The technology lens: paper is a technology — a made thing that changed how humans
think, remember, and govern. The global lens: the spread ran east *and* west, through
many peoples, not one; credit belongs to Chinese papermakers, and later to the many
hands that carried and adapted the craft. The egalitarian lens: before paper, writing
materials were costly, so reading and writing were held by the few; cheap paper widened
access — a consequence that was not shared instantly or equally, but trended that way.
The ethics lens: knowledge traveling is a good, but note that one route of travel was
through war and capture (Talas); we can honor the invention while also noticing the
costs and the unequal paths by which ideas move.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what did we learn about big changes? (Many
   causes, many consequences.) Today we apply it to one real change: paper.
2. **Meet paper (10 min).** Paper was invented in China; the oldest fragments date to
   around the 2nd century BCE, and by tradition a court official named Cai Lun (c. 105
   CE) improved and standardized it. It was thin, light, and far cheaper than bamboo,
   silk, or animal parchment.
3. **Trace the spread (15 min).** Follow the map sequence: China → Korea and Japan →
   Central Asia (Samarkand) → Baghdad → Europe (12th–15th centuries). At each stop,
   name one cause that carried it and one consequence it brought.
4. **Sort causes and consequences (15 min).** With a partner, sort the cards: which
   are causes of paper's spread, which are consequences? Then build your own
   diagram for paper, marking one intended and one unintended consequence.
5. **Close (5 min).** One invention, many causes, many consequences — and the
   knowledge, carried by people, traveled farther than any single sheet. Next we meet a
   different kind of achievement: a *number* that changed the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sequence the four stops only, with a sentence starter at each ("At this
  stop, paper changed ___").
- **Extension:** Connect paper to movable type in China (Bi Sheng, c. 1040s) and trace
  how cheaper books multiplied the consequences of paper across centuries.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner name at least two causes and
  two consequences of paper's spread, in order across regions?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed paper-spread diagram, kept as evidence of
  cause-and-consequence analysis.

## Home connection

Find one thing in your home that was invented far away and traveled to you (a food, a
tool, a word). Ask: how did it get here, and what changed because it did?
