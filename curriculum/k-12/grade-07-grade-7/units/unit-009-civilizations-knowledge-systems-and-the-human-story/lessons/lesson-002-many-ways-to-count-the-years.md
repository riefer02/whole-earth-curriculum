# Lesson 2 — Calendars: Many Ways to Count the Years

## Summary

Learners compare two ways of counting the years: a **solar** calendar (the Gregorian
year of 365 days) and a **lunar** calendar (the Islamic Hijri year of about 354 days).
They see that the same day has different numbers in different calendars, and that a
calendar's "year one" is a choice a people makes — not a fact written in the sky.

## Objectives

- Compare two different calendars or ways of counting years used by different peoples,
  and explain why the same day can carry different numbers. _(D08.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

If you and a friend each count the same pile of stones — one of you by tens, the other
by sevens — you finish with different numbers, but the stones did not change. Calendars
work like that. People everywhere watch the same sky, but they count its cycles in
different ways: some follow the Sun, some follow the Moon. So the very same day can be
called different years and months, depending on whose calendar you are using. Neither
is "wrong" — they are different rulers for the same sky.

## Materials

- Calendar comparison page
- Sun and Moon cards

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison page and cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Lesson 1 that periods are human choices; today, the *numbers*
  of the years are choices, too.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a solar
calendar counts by the Sun's year (about 365 days); (2) a lunar calendar counts by the
Moon's cycles (about 354 days, roughly 11 days shorter); (3) because the two years are
different lengths, the same day has different numbers in each; and (4) "year one" is a
choice each people makes.** Use the worked example (Gregorian vs. Hijri) with explicit
instruction first, then let learners sort and compare (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). These are established facts: the Gregorian calendar is a solar calendar
(introduced 1582, year counted from a traditional Christian starting point); the
Islamic Hijri calendar is lunar (twelve lunar months, year 1 = the Hijra of 622 CE in
the Gregorian calendar). The global lens: the same sky holds many calendars — Hebrew,
Chinese, Buddhist, Maya, and others — none is the "real" one. The egalitarian lens:
saying "the year is 2026" quietly centers one tradition; other peoples are living in
different years right now, and their calendars are equally real. The critical-thinking
lens: a calendar's starting point is a value-laden choice, not a discovery. The ethics
lens: how we count shared time can include or exclude people; honoring many calendars
is a small act of care.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what is a "period," and who chooses its lines?
   (People choose them.) Today: people also choose how to *count the years*.
2. **Meet two calendars (15 min).** A **solar year** follows the Sun: about 365 days —
   the Gregorian calendar, used widely today. A **lunar year** follows the Moon: twelve
   months of about 29.5 days, about 354 days total — the Islamic **Hijri** calendar,
   used by Muslims worldwide. Because 354 is about 11 days shorter than 365, the
   two calendars drift apart, and the same day lands on a different date in each.
3. **Same sky, different numbers (10 min).** On your comparison page, record the same
   "today" twice — once in each calendar (your facilitator or a chart can help). What
   is different: the year? the month? the day? What stayed the same?
4. **Sort sun and moon (10 min).** With a partner, sort the calendar facts: which
   belong to a solar calendar, which to a lunar one? Check each other's sorts.
5. **Close (10 min).** A calendar's "year one" is a choice — one people counts from a
   founder's journey, another from a birth, another from a creation. The sky does not
   say "year one." People do. Tomorrow we will see that big changes, like calendars,
   have *many* causes.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the Sun/Moon sort; offer the two year-lengths as "longer
  (Sun)" and "shorter (Moon)" to begin.
- **Extension:** Explain a lunisolar calendar (such as the Hebrew or Chinese calendar),
  which adds a leap month so lunar months stay in their seasons, and compare it with
  the two calendars above.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner state why a solar and a lunar year
  differ in length, and show the same day recorded differently in each?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I explain that the same day has different
  numbers in different calendars, and that a calendar's starting point is a choice?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what calendar or year-count their family or community uses — for
festivals, seasons, or faith — and compare its year with the Gregorian year.

## Resources

- The lengths of the solar (≈365.24 days) and lunar (≈354 days, twelve synodic months)
  years, the Gregorian reform of 1582, and the Hijri calendar's start in 622 CE are
  standard, widely documented facts (see Encyclopaedia Britannica entries "calendar,"
  "Gregorian calendar," and "Islamic calendar"). The claim that many calendars coexist
  (Hebrew, Chinese, Buddhist, Maya, and others) is likewise standard; it is held here
  as a global-egalitarian commitment, not a ranking.
