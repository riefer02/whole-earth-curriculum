# Lesson 11 — What Colonization Took

## Summary

Learners explain how **colonization** affected colonized peoples: the loss of **land**
(dispossession), the loss of **culture** (languages and practices suppressed, children
taken to distant schools), and the loss of **self-rule** (foreign rule imposed). They
hold these losses with care and accuracy, and always pair them with what peoples kept
alive — language, story, and resistance — so the lesson ends in dignity, not despair.

## Objectives

- Explain how colonization affected colonized peoples, including the loss of land,
  culture, and self-rule, with care and accuracy. _(D08.S4.07.02)_

## Connection

Imagine someone came and said your home now belonged to them, that your language
could no longer be spoken in school, and that strangers would make your community's
rules. You would feel the loss in three places at once: the ground under you, the
words in your mouth, and the power to decide your own life. This is what colonization
did, on a vast scale, to peoples across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania. It is
a hard thing to look at — and we look at it carefully and honestly, and we also look
at what people kept alive against it.

## Materials

- Three-losses page
- Then-and-still cards

## Preparation

- Copy the page and cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Lesson 10 the forms of resistance; today we meet the
  injustice those forms so often answered.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) colonization
is one people taking and ruling another's land and life; from the late 1400s onward,
European powers colonized much of the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania; (2) its
effects include loss of land (dispossession of Indigenous peoples), loss of culture
(languages and practices suppressed; in some places children were taken to distant
boarding or residential schools where their languages were punished), and loss of
self-rule (foreign governments and borders imposed, often ignoring existing peoples);
(3) accuracy and care: we name these losses plainly but without gratuitous detail, and
we always pair them with continuity — peoples kept their languages, stories, and
resistance alive, and continue to.** These are documented facts (see Encyclopaedia
Britannica, "colonialism"; UNESCO and Indigenous organizations document language loss
and revitalization). Teach with care (`docs/facilitation.md`): name feelings before
facts, keep detail non-graphic, and end in dignity and agency, never despair. The
ethics lens: colonization was an injustice — the lesson's moral clarity is honest, not
preachy. The egalitarian lens: telling this from the perspective of colonized peoples,
not the colonizers, is the whole point — history from many sides, never one people's
triumph. The global lens: colonization touched every inhabited continent; the examples
are deliberately not one nation's story. The critical-thinking lens: question whose
voice the history books use — "exploration" and "settlement" are words that can hide
what colonized peoples called invasion.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what forms of resistance did we meet? (Protest,
   boycott, refusal, art, storytelling.) Today: what those forms were answering.
2. **Meet colonization (10 min).** **Colonization** is one people taking and ruling
   another's land and life. From the late 1400s onward, European powers colonized much
   of the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.
3. **Three losses (15 min).** Fill the three columns. **Land** — homelands taken from
   the peoples who lived there (**dispossession**). **Culture** — languages and
   practices suppressed; in some places, children were taken to distant schools where
   their own languages were punished. **Self-rule** — foreign governments and borders
   imposed, ignoring the peoples already there.
4. **What people kept alive (10 min).** Sort the then-and-still cards: pair each loss
   with something that continued — a language still spoken, a story still told, a
   people still here. Resistance (Lesson 10) and survival are the other half of this
   story.
5. **Close (10 min).** The loss was real, and it matters. And so does this: peoples
   endured, kept their languages and stories, and continue today. We end there — in
   dignity, not despair.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the three columns with a picture and a starter sentence for
  each ("They lost their ___; they kept their ___").
- **Extension:** With a named source, analyze one people's specific experience — one
  loss and one way they preserved their language or self-rule — at a respectful depth.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner name the three losses and
  pair each with one thing peoples kept alive, with accuracy and care?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed three-losses page, kept as evidence of
  explaining colonization's effects with care.

## Home connection

If it is right for your family, ask an elder what your community kept alive through a
hard time — a language, a story, a practice — and notice that continuity is its own
quiet victory.

## Resources

- The global reach of European colonization from the late 1400s and its effects —
  dispossession of Indigenous peoples, suppression of languages, and the imposition of
  foreign rule — are documented in Encyclopaedia Britannica ("colonialism") and in
  UNESCO's work on language loss and revitalization. The boarding/residential-school
  practice of punishing children's languages is documented (e.g., the work of national
  truth and reconciliation commissions in Canada, and similar bodies elsewhere); it is
  stated here without graphic detail. The pairing of loss with continuity is a
  values-and-accuracy commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4), not a historical claim.
