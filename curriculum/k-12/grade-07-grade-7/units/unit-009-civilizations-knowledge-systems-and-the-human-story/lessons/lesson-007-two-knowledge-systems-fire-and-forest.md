# Lesson 7 — Two Knowledge Systems: Fire and the Forest

## Summary

Learners compare two knowledge systems for understanding and caring for the natural
world: Aboriginal Australian **cultural burning** and formal **fire science**. They see
that each knows fire and land in its own way — one through knowledge passed down for
generations, one through measuring and testing — and that held side by side, neither
ranked above the other, they can teach each other.

## Objectives

- Compare two different knowledge systems — indigenous ecological knowledge and formal
  science — in how each understands and cares for the natural world. _(D08.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

Two people can care for the same garden very differently. One learned from a
grandparent exactly when to plant and when to prune, watching the sky and the soil.
Another learned from books and instruments, measuring the light and testing the soil.
Both keep the garden alive; neither's way cancels the other's. That is how it is with
caring for the land on a larger scale. Today we put two ways of knowing fire and
forest side by side — not to pick a winner, but to see what each one sees.

## Materials

- Two-ways comparison page
- Knowledge-system cards

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison page and cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Lessons 5–6 how non-European achievements (zero, the Canon)
  moved through the world; today we compare two *ways of knowing*, not two achievements.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a knowledge
system is a whole way of knowing and caring — how a people observes, remembers, and
acts; (2) Aboriginal Australian cultural burning uses low, carefully timed "cool"
fires to renew the land, protect habitat, and reduce fuel for dangerous fires — a
practice developed over tens of thousands of years and still practiced today; (3)
formal fire science measures and models fire behavior — fuel, weather, spread — and
tests predictions; (4) the two are held side by side, never ranked: each is real,
rigorous in its own way, and they can inform each other.** These are documented facts
(see CSIRO and Indigenous fire organizations such as the Firesticks Alliance, which
describe cultural burning's long history and its benefits for land health and fire
risk). Model the two-column worked example first, then let learners sort
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The environment lens: this is about caring for
living systems — a relationship to land, not just facts about it. The global lens:
cultural burning is one Indigenous practice among many around the world (Māori
kaitiakitanga, and others); formal science is also one tradition among many, not the
default. The egalitarian lens: ranking "science" above Indigenous knowledge is itself
a legacy of colonial power; holding them equal is a small act of repair. The ethics
lens: how we treat knowledge we did not grow up with is an ethical choice — respect,
credit, and partnership over dismissal. Caution: avoid the trap of presenting
Indigenous knowledge as only "ancient" or only about the past; it is living and
current.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what made the Canon of Medicine travel? (It was
   useful, and it was carried in books and teaching.) Today: two *ways of knowing*,
   side by side.
2. **Meet two systems (15 min).** One: **indigenous ecological knowledge** — here,
   Aboriginal Australian **cultural burning**. For tens of thousands of years, people
   have lit low, carefully timed "cool" fires that clear old growth, protect habitat,
   and reduce the fuel that feeds huge, dangerous fires — knowledge passed from elders
   to children. Two: **formal science** — fire scientists measure fuel, weather, and
   spread, build models, and test predictions. Both understand fire; both care for the
   land.
3. **Compare (15 min).** On your two-ways page, record side by side: What does each
   system *know* about fire? How does each *care* for the land? How is each knowledge
   *passed on*?
4. **Sort (10 min).** With a partner, sort the knowledge-system cards into the two
   columns. Check each other: did any practice belong to both? (Many do — timing,
   observation, care.)
5. **Close (5 min).** Neither system is "better." Each sees part of the truth, and
   together they see more. Tomorrow we turn to people on the move — chosen and forced.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the card sort with two starter labels ("passed down, careful
  fire" vs. "measured and tested"), and one sentence each.
- **Extension:** Compare a second pairing — e.g., Pacific wayfinding (stars, swells,
  birds) vs. formal navigation (instruments, charts) — using the same two-ways page.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name one way each system
  understands fire and one way each cares for the land, without ranking them?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I describe both systems accurately and hold
  them side by side, rather than calling one better?"

## Home connection

Ask an elder or a caregiver how your family or community reads the seasons, weather,
or plants — a kind of knowledge passed down outside books. Compare it with what a
weather report or a textbook says, and notice that both have something true.

## Resources

- Aboriginal Australian cultural burning — its long history and its benefits for land
  health and reduced fire risk — is described by CSIRO (Australia's national science
  agency) and by Indigenous fire organizations such as the Firesticks Alliance
  (<https://www.firesticks.org.au/>). The practice is a living, current one, developed
  over tens of thousands of years; it is presented here as an equal knowledge system,
  never as "primitive" or merely "ancient." Formal fire science (measuring fuel,
  weather, and fire spread) is standard fire ecology. The claim that the two can
  inform each other is a partnership value, not a scientific finding.
- On explicit instruction and worked examples: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
