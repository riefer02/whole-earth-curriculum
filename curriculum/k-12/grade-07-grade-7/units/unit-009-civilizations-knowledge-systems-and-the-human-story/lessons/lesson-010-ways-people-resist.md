# Lesson 10 — Ways People Resist

## Summary

Learners analyze five forms of **resistance** — **protest, boycott, refusal, art, and
storytelling** — and how people choose among them. Through short, age-appropriate
cases, they see that resistance takes many forms, chosen for a people's goal, risk,
and community, and that dignity and agency sit at the center of all of them.

## Objectives

- Analyze forms of resistance — protest, boycott, refusal, art, and storytelling — and
  how people choose among them. _(D08.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

When something is unfair, there is not just one way to answer it. You could march and
shout (protest), stop buying or doing something (boycott), simply say no (refusal),
make something beautiful or powerful (art), or keep the story alive so no one forgets
(storytelling). People have used every one of these — and more — to push back against
injustice. The question is not only *what* people did, but *why* they chose that way,
with the risks and resources they had. Today we meet five forms, and the choosing.

## Materials

- Resistance-forms wheel
- Resistance case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the wheel and case cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Lesson 8 the difference between choice and coercion; today,
  resistance is people *choosing* to push back against coercion and injustice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) resistance
takes many forms — protest, boycott, refusal, art, storytelling — and they are often
combined; (2) examples: the Salt March (India, 1930) — Gandhi led a long walk to make
salt in protest of the British salt tax, an act of protest and civil disobedience; the
Montgomery bus boycott (USA, 1955–56) — people stopped riding segregated buses for over
a year; capoeira (Brazil) — an art of movement and song created by enslaved Africans,
carrying strength and identity; refusal — enslaved and colonized people's everyday acts
of saying no; storytelling — oral traditions that keep memory and resistance alive; (3)
people choose a form based on their goal, the risk they face, and their community and
resources.** These are documented facts (Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Salt March,"
"Montgomery bus boycott," "capoeira"; UNESCO inscribed the "capoeira circle" in 2014,
registered as S-056). Teach with care: resistance is agency and dignity, not glorified
violence; the forms studied here are nonviolent and artful. The ethics lens: resistance
is a response to injustice — the moral question is what wrong it answers and how. The
egalitarian lens: resistance is the voice of those with less power; honoring it is
honoring dignity, not chaos. The global lens: these forms appear on every continent —
India, the USA, Brazil, and beyond — no region is the default. The critical-thinking
lens: question *why* a form was chosen, and notice that the choice is shaped by
context, not by one "right" way to resist.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: how did geography both connect and separate
   peoples? (Rivers, oceans, deserts, mountains.) Today: what people *do* about
   injustice.
2. **Meet five forms (15 min).** Look at the wheel. **Protest** — march and speak
   (the Salt March, India 1930: a long walk to make salt, defying an unjust tax).
   **Boycott** — stop buying or using (the Montgomery bus boycott, USA 1955–56:
   people stopped riding segregated buses for over a year). **Refusal** — simply say
   no. **Art** — make something that carries strength (capoeira, Brazil: movement and
   song created by enslaved Africans). **Storytelling** — keep the story alive so no
   one forgets.
3. **Sort the cases (15 min).** With a partner, sort the resistance case cards by
   form. Notice: many cases fit more than one form — resistance often combines them.
4. **Why that form? (10 min).** For one case, answer: what was the people's *goal*?
   What *risk* did they face? What *resources* did they have? Why did that shape
   their choice of form?
5. **Close (5 min).** Resistance is not one thing; it is a choice made with care and
   courage. Tomorrow we meet what resistance was often answering: colonization.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only three cases with a starter sentence each ("This was ___
  (form) because ___").
- **Extension:** Analyze how two forms combined (protest + boycott, or art +
  storytelling) and explain how each strengthened the other.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner name the five forms and
  explain why a people chose one form over another in a specific case?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The annotated resistance wheel, kept as evidence of analyzing
  forms of resistance.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time people in your community spoke up or made change —
how did they do it (protest, boycott, refusal, art, storytelling), and why that way?
