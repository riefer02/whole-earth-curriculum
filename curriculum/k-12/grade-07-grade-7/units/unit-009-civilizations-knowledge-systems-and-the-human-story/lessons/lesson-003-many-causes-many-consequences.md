# Lesson 3 — Many Causes, Many Consequences

## Summary

Learners build the historian's habit of looking for **multiple causes** and **multiple
consequences**. A big change rarely has one cause or one result; it has many, some
**intended** and some **unintended**. They learn and practice the diagram that holds
this thinking, readying them to apply it to a real change next lesson.

## Objectives

- Analyze a change in terms of multiple causes and multiple consequences, including
  intended and unintended results. _(D08.S1.07.02)_

## Connection

Why did you end up at this school, or learn to ride a bike, or make the friend you
made? The honest answer is almost never "one reason." It was a mix — a family move, a
neighbor's kindness, a lucky moment, a wish you had. And each of those choices set off
other things you did not plan. History is the same. A big change — an invention that
spreads, a people that migrates — has many causes, and many consequences, some of which
nobody saw coming. The historian's job is to look for *all* of them, not just the
famous one.

## Materials

- Cause-and-consequence diagram
- Statement cards

## Preparation

- Copy the diagram and statement cards.
- Have the worked example ready: "a new road opened through a village" — causes (need
  to move goods, money and labor, a decision by leaders), consequences (faster travel,
  new shops, more noise, some families displaced).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) a major change
has multiple causes; (2) it has multiple consequences; (3) some consequences are
intended, some are not, and consequences fall on different people unequally.** Model
the worked example explicitly before independent work (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006); this is a skill, and skills are taught with a worked example and guided
practice. The critical-thinking lens: resist single-cause and single-effect stories —
they flatten the real, tangled past. The global lens: the same change looks different
from different places; a road is a blessing to a trader and a loss to a displaced
family, and both views are true. The egalitarian lens: always ask *who* gains and *who*
is left out of a consequence — consequences are not shared equally. The technology
lens: inventions are a frequent "change" in this frame, and their spread (next lesson)
is a model case of many causes and consequences. Keep it concrete; the abstract
diagram is earned through the familiar examples first.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what is a calendar's "year one"? (A choice a
   people makes.) Today we learn to see that big changes, like calendars, have many
   causes — not one.
2. **Meet the diagram (10 min).** Look at the diagram. On the left: **causes** (the
   things that make a change happen). In the middle: **the change**. On the right:
   **consequences** (the results). Notice there are *many* on each side.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Follow along as we fill the diagram for "a new road
   opened through a village." Causes: people needed to move goods, money and labor were
   available, leaders made a decision. Consequences: faster travel (intended), new
   shops (partly intended), more noise and some families moved from their homes
   (unintended, and not shared equally).
4. **Practice with cards (20 min).** With a partner, sort the statement cards into
   causes and consequences for a given change, then build your own diagram for a change
   you know — a new phone, a bridge, a festival that grew. Mark one consequence as
   intended and one as unintended.
5. **Close (5 min).** Check each other: did you find more than one cause and more than
   one result? Tomorrow we apply this tool to a real, world-changing invention: paper.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work only with the worked example, using sentence starters ("One cause
  was…", "One result was…"); identify intended vs. unintended with a partner.
- **Extension:** Rank which cause mattered most and defend the ranking; add a
  consequence that would fall on a *different* group of people.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner produce at least two causes and two
  consequences for a change, and distinguish an intended from an unintended result?
- **Peer-check:** Partners trade diagrams and add one cause or consequence the other
  missed, then explain why it counts.

## Home connection

Pick one change in your home or neighborhood (a new shop, a road, a device). List two
causes and two consequences of it, and ask someone at home if they can add a third.

## Resources

- Multiple causation and intended/unintended consequences are core historical-thinking
  concepts; for a classroom-friendly framing, see the historical-thinking resources of
  the Stanford History Education Group (sheg.stanford.edu), which treat sourcing and
  causality as teachable skills. The claim that consequences fall unevenly on different
  people is a fairness commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4), not a factual finding.
- On explicit instruction and worked examples for novice skills: Kirschner, Sweller &
