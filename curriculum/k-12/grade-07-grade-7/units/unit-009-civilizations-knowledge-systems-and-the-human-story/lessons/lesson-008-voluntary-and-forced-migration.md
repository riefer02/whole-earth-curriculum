# Lesson 8 — Choosing to Move, Forced to Move

## Summary

Learners compare **voluntary** and **forced** migration. Voluntary migration is chosen —
people move for work, family, safety, or hope (**push** and **pull** factors). Forced
migration is not chosen — people are taken or driven out, as in the transatlantic
slave trade. They analyze the causes and effects of each on the people who moved and
the places involved.

## Objectives

- Compare voluntary and forced migration and analyze their causes and effects on the
  people who moved and the places involved. _(D08.S3.07.01)_

## Connection

People move for all kinds of reasons. Your own family may have moved — for work, for
family, for a safer or better place — and if so, that move was (at least in part) a
*choice*. But not all moving is chosen. Some people in history were taken from their
homes against their will, in chains. The difference between "we chose to go" and "we
were made to go" changes everything about the journey — its causes, its dangers, and
its effects on everyone involved. Today we hold those two kinds of moving side by
side, with care.

## Materials

- Two-migrations comparison page
- Push-and-pull cards

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison page and cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Lesson 4 how paper — and with it, people and ideas — moved
  along trade routes; today we focus on the people themselves, chosen and forced.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) migration is
the movement of people from one place to another; (2) voluntary migration is chosen —
push factors (few jobs, hardship, danger at home) and pull factors (work, family,
freedom, safety elsewhere); (3) forced migration is not chosen — people are taken or
driven out, as in the transatlantic slave trade, in which at least 12 million Africans
were forcibly transported across the Atlantic (a topic studied in full in Grade 8);
(4) effects fall on the people who move (loss, separation, but also, in voluntary
cases, opportunity) and on the places involved (both the places left and the places
entered change).** These are documented facts (the transatlantic figure is standard in
the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, slavevoyages.org; see also Encyclopaedia
Britannica). Teach this with care: name feelings before facts, keep detail
non-graphic, and always connect to dignity and survival, not despair (`docs/
facilitation.md`). The ethics lens: the difference between choice and coercion is a
moral line — respecting it is why we treat the two kinds of movement differently. The
egalitarian lens: forced migration is an injustice, not a "migration like any other";
calling it by its right name honors the people who endured it. The global lens: both
kinds of movement have happened across all continents and in all eras; no region is
the default. The critical-thinking lens: question stories that say people "moved
willingly" when records show they were taken — the word "migration" can hide coercion.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: how did two knowledge systems each care for the
   land? (One by passed-down practice, one by measuring — neither better.) Today: two
   kinds of moving.
2. **Meet migration (10 min).** **Migration** is the movement of people from one place
   to another. **Voluntary migration** is chosen: people move because of **push**
   factors (few jobs, hardship, danger at home) and **pull** factors (work, family,
   freedom, safety elsewhere).
3. **Meet forced migration (10 min).** **Forced migration** is not chosen. One terrible
   example: in the transatlantic slave trade, at least 12 million African people were
   taken by force and carried across the Atlantic over centuries — a crime against
   human dignity. (You will study this in full in Grade 8; today we name it honestly
   and briefly.)
4. **Compare causes and effects (15 min).** On your page, compare: What *caused* each
   kind of move? What were the *effects* on the people who moved? What were the
   *effects* on the places left behind and the places entered?
5. **Sort push, pull, coercion (10 min).** With a partner, sort the cards: which
   reasons are push, which are pull, and which are coercion (force)? Close: the
   difference between "we chose" and "we were taken" changes everything.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only push vs. pull sorting with two starter labels, and one
  sentence each about causes and effects.
- **Extension:** Analyze a second forced migration — the forced removal of Indigenous
  peoples from their homelands — and compare its causes and effects with the first.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/portfolio):** Can the learner distinguish voluntary from
  forced migration and name a cause and an effect of each?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed two-migrations comparison, kept as evidence of
  migration analysis.

## Home connection

Ask, gently and with permission, whether anyone in your family has a story of moving —
chosen or not. Listen; then notice whether the story was a choice, and how that shaped
it.

## Resources

- The transatlantic slave trade's scale (at least 12 million Africans forcibly
  transported) is documented by the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database
  (<https://www.slavevoyages.org/>); the UNESCO "Routes of Enslaved Peoples" project
  also documents this history. It is named here briefly and without graphic detail,
  for fuller study in Grade 8. Push/pull factors in voluntary migration are a standard
  geographic framing. The claim that coercion differs in kind from choice is a moral
  distinction (`docs/philosophy.md` §4), not a factual finding.
