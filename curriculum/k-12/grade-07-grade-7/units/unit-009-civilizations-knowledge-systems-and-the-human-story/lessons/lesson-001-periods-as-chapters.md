# Lesson 1 — Why Historians Divide Time into Periods

## Summary

The human past is one long, unbroken story. Historians divide it into **periods** —
"chapters" — to make it easier to hold, compare, and talk about. Learners see that
dividing lines are human **choices**, drawn differently by different peoples, and
practice placing and defending lines of their own.

## Objectives

- Explain why historians divide time into periods, and recognize that periodization is
  a choice different peoples make differently. _(D08.S1.07.01)_

## Connection

Think about a book you love, or a TV season, or your own life so far. A story that
never paused would be impossible to hold in your head. So we cut it into chapters —
"the part before I moved," "the year we got the dog," "this year, now." Historians do
the same thing with the whole human story: they cut one endless line into chapters so
they can hold it, compare it, and talk about it. The trick is remembering that *we*
draw the lines.

## Materials

- Timeline strips
- Period cards

## Preparation

- Copy the timeline strips and period cards.
- Retrieval: recall from Grade 6 the difference between a primary and a secondary
  source (D08.S1.06.01); today builds on that by asking how historians *arrange* time.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) the past is
one continuous line; (2) periods are chapters historians choose to make it manageable;
(3) the lines are choices, not facts — different peoples draw them differently, and
that is worth noticing.** Use explicit instruction with the book-chapter worked example
first, then let learners draw their own lines (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The
global lens (`docs/philosophy.md` §4): periodization is not universal — "ancient,
middle, modern" is a mostly European frame; other traditions divide time by dynasties,
by lunar cycles, by eras of a people's own story. The egalitarian lens: no people's way
of slicing time is the "correct" one; calling one period scheme "the" timeline silently
centers one perspective. The critical-thinking lens: question the answer — a period is
a tool for thinking, not a fact about the world. The ethics lens: who gets to name the
periods shapes whose story is remembered; naming is a small act of power, handled with
care.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Quick recall: what is the difference between a primary and a
   secondary source? (A primary source comes from the time; a secondary source
   comments on it later.) Today we ask a new question: how do historians *arrange*
   time itself?
2. **One long line (10 min).** The human past is one continuous line — it has no real
   breaks. Look at the timeline diagram. Then think of a book: where would you put the
   chapter breaks, and why? That is exactly what historians do with time.
3. **Meet "periods" (10 min).** A **period** (or **era**) is a stretch of time grouped
   together because people see something shared in it. Look at the period cards: deep
   past, ancient, middle, recent. Notice: these are not written on the Earth — people
   chose them.
4. **Draw your own lines (20 min).** On your timeline strip, draw dividing lines and
   name the periods. First divide your *own* life into chapters. Then, with a partner,
   try dividing a *world* story — where would you put the lines, and what would you
   name each stretch? Defend one choice aloud: "I put a line here because…"
5. **Close (5 min).** A period is a tool, not a fact. Different peoples divide time
   differently — and tomorrow we will see how they *count the years* differently, too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start only with the learner's own life line; offer three starter labels
  ("before school," "now," "ahead") to choose from.
- **Extension:** Compare two schemes — e.g., a European "ancient/middle/modern" frame
  against a dynasty-based or era-based frame — and name one thing each makes visible
  and one thing each hides.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner explain *why* a line was placed
  and name the period? Do they describe the line as a choice rather than a fact?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I explain why the line goes where it does, and
  did I notice that another person might draw it somewhere else?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you the "chapters" of your family's or community's story —
the big turning points they remember. Notice where they put the dividing lines, and
why.

## Resources

- The idea that periodization is a human convention rather than a fact of nature is
  standard historiography; see, for example, the entry "historiography" in
  Encyclopaedia Britannica (which treats the periodizing of history as a historians'
  method and convention, not a fact of nature). The claim that "ancient/middle/modern"
  is a European frame, while other traditions periodize by dynasty or era, is well
  documented and treated here as a global-egalitarian caution, not a ranking.
- On explicit instruction for novice content: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006),
  <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1>
