# Lesson 7 — Designing for Everyone: Accessibility and Testing for Barriers

## Summary

Learners discover that **accessibility is not a special case but good design**: a barrier is a
design choice that stops some people, and removing it helps everyone. Using the worked example
of a stairs-only doorway versus a **ramp and wide door**, they choose one real thing to
redesign, identify who might be blocked and why, redesign it, and **test it for barriers** —
the same discipline behind curb cuts, captions, alt text, and clear signage worldwide.

## Objectives

- Design something with accessibility in mind so more kinds of people can use it, and test it
  for barriers. _(D07.S4.07.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever noticed the curb "ramp" where a sidewalk meets the road? It was made so a person
using a wheelchair could cross — but it also helps the person with a cane, the person wheeling
a cart, the parent pushing a stroller, and the delivery worker with a heavy load. That is the
secret of accessible design: **when you design for the edges, you design better for
everyone.** A caption helps a Deaf viewer *and* a viewer in a noisy room; a large-print sign
helps low-vision readers *and* people in a hurry. Today you become the designer who asks,
before finishing, *who might be blocked here — and how do I let them in?*

## Materials

- Design-for-everyone poster (blocks vs includes)
- A small design to improve
- Barrier checklist

## Preparation

- Print or draw the design-for-everyone poster.
- Copy a barrier checklist for each group.
- Have each pair or group choose a small, real thing to redesign — a sign, a game rule, a
  menu, a seat, a path, or a page of instructions from the room or the learner's day.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **accessibility is
design for the widest range of bodies and minds, and barriers are found by testing, not by
guessing** — a learner should be able to name at least one barrier, one fix, and one way to
test the fix. Model the doorway worked example fully (explicit instruction for a novice skill
— Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the egalitarian lens central: the question is never
"what's wrong with this person" but "what did the design assume, and who did it leave out."
Draw on the full range of disability — mobility, vision, hearing, cognition, language,
height, strength, and more — and note that most people will be disabled at some point in a
life, temporarily or permanently, so this is designing for *all of us* (the social model of
disability, held as a frame). Digital examples (captions, alt text, screen-reader support)
tie to the technology lens; WCAG names four principles — perceivable, operable,
understandable, robust — that learners can apply to any design.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have made images, sound, and bodies carry meaning. Now
   you design *things* — and the first question is always: *who can use this, and who cannot?*
2. **Meet the worked example (15 min).** Look at the poster. **Design that blocks:** a doorway
   reached only by stairs stops the person using a wheelchair and the person using a cane.
   **Design that includes:** the same doorway with a ramp beside the stairs and a wide door
   lets the wheelchair user, the cane user, and the person carrying a heavy load all enter.
   Notice: the ramp was "for" wheelchairs, but it helps *everyone*. That is the test of good
   design — does the widest range of people get in?
3. **Choose and question a design (10 min).** With your group, pick your small design to
   improve. Use the barrier checklist: *who might not be able to use this?* Think across
   mobility, vision, hearing, thinking, language, height, and strength. Name the barriers you
   find — be specific ("the sign is too high to read from a seated position," "the rule is
   only spoken, not shown").
4. **Redesign (10 min).** Change the design so the barrier is gone. Add a ramp, a caption, a
   larger or pictured sign, a second way to give the answer, a lower reach, a clearer step. You
   do not have to build it — draw it, describe it, or act it out.
5. **Test for barriers (10 min).** Swap with another group. They try your redesigned thing as
   a *specific different user* — seated, eyes closed, no sound, in a hurry, a different
   language — and report one barrier that remains. Fix what you can.
6. **Close (5 min).** You did what curb-cut makers and caption writers and sign designers do:
   you asked who is blocked, and you let them in. Next lesson you weigh the trade-offs that
   every design choice carries.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a ready example with one obvious barrier (a sign too small to read) and
  one suggested fix, with a sentence frame: "This design blocks ___ because ___; I would
  ___."
- **Extension:** Check a redesign against the WCAG four principles — perceivable, operable,
  understandable, robust — and name which principle each fix serves.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name at least one barrier (and who it
  blocks) and one fix that removes it, and describe how they would test the fix?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The redesigned thing (drawing, description, or role-play) plus the
  barrier checklist, kept for the unit portfolio.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I point to the barrier I removed and say *which*
  people it now includes, and how I know (tested) it works?"

## Home connection

Find one barrier in your own day — a step, a too-small sign, a spoken-only instruction — and
sketch or describe one change that would remove it, and who it would help.
