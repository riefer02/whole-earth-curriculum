# Lesson 2 — Perspective, Framing, Emphasis — and Revising After Feedback

## Summary

Learners discover that **perspective, framing, and emphasis** are deliberate choices that
shape what a viewer notices and feels — a wide frame makes a subject small, a close frame
makes it important, and emphasis pulls the eye to one thing. They notice that these choices
carry power, and that the same tool can be used honestly or to manipulate. Then they apply
the ideas to their own Lesson 1 artwork, give and receive **specific feedback**, and make
**one revision** so their message lands more clearly.

## Objectives

- Explain how perspective, framing, or emphasis shapes what a viewer notices and feels about
  an image. _(D07.S1.07.02)_
- Create an artwork that communicates a personal message and revise it after feedback.
  _(D07.S1.07.01 — the "revise after feedback" half)_

## Connection

Think of two photos of the same leader: one shot from below, so the person towers over you
like a mountain; one shot at eye level, so they look like a neighbor you could talk to.
Neither photo lied — both are the same real person — but each *chose* where to stand and what
to leave in the frame, and that choice changed how you felt. Posters, ads, news photos, and
game menus all do this on purpose. Today you learn to read that choice in others' images —
and to *make* it, deliberately, in your own, so your message is the one you actually mean.

## Materials

- Framing-and-emphasis poster (wide / close / emphasis)
- Your artwork from Lesson 1
- Feedback slip

## Preparation

- Print or draw the framing-and-emphasis poster, including the perspective notes (low angle,
  high angle, eye level).
- Have learners bring the artwork they began in Lesson 1.
- Copy a feedback slip for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Two ideas land together: (1) **perspective,
framing, and emphasis are choices with effects**, and (2) **feedback is information that
sharpens the work, not a verdict on the person** (see `docs/assessment.md` on mastery and
feedback). Model the three choices on the poster, then model giving one *specific, kind*
feedback note before learners give each other any. Keep the ethics lens visible: the same
framing tools that make a hero look heroic can make a stranger look dangerous — ask learners
to notice *who* benefits from an image's choices (egalitarianism), and hold that the tool
itself is neutral while its *use* has consequences. Feedback stays about the artwork, never
the learner, and a learner may always choose which feedback to act on.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Last lesson you chose a message and planned how to carry
   it. Today you learn three levers that change how a viewer *notices and feels* — and you
   use them to make your message land.
2. **Meet the three levers (15 min).** Look at the poster. Here is the same simple scene — a
   person walking toward a mountain — drawn three ways. A **wide frame** shows the whole
   landscape, so the person looks small (part of something big). A **close frame** shows only
   the person, so they look important. **Emphasis** draws the eye to one thing — one dark,
   bold figure against a light field. Now add **perspective** — where the viewer stands. A
   *low angle* (looking up) makes a subject look powerful; a *high angle* (looking down)
   makes it look small; *eye level* makes it look like an equal. Every poster, photo, and
   game menu you have ever seen used these levers. Ask yourself, always: *who chose this
   frame, and what did they want me to feel?*
3. **Apply it to your artwork (10 min).** Look at your own piece. Which lever could make
   your message stronger — a closer frame on your symbol? more emphasis on the main thing?
   a lower angle so the subject feels strong, or eye level so it feels like a friend? Choose
   **one** change.
4. **Feedback pairs (10 min).** Swap artworks with a partner. On your slip, write two
   specific things: "What I noticed first was ___" and "One change that would make your
   message stronger is ___." Be kind and specific — *not* "it's bad," but "the hands are
   small, and your message is about caring, so making the hands bigger could help." Then
   switch roles.
5. **Revise (10 min).** Read your partner's note. Choose what to act on — you are the
   author; feedback is information, not an order. Make **one revision** to your artwork.
6. **Close (5 min).** You read images and you sharpened your own. Remember: the same tools
   can honor or mislead, so ask who benefits from a frame. Keep your revised artwork — it is
   your portfolio's first finished piece.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Limit the task to one lever — choose *framing* (wider or closer) or *emphasis*
  (darken the main thing) — with a sentence frame: "I made ___ bigger/darker/closer because
  my message is ___."
- **Extension:** Make a second version of the same artwork with the *opposite* framing choice
  and write one sentence comparing what each makes a viewer feel.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner name which lever (perspective, framing,
  or emphasis) they changed, and connect it — with a reason — to their message or to what a
  viewer notices and feels?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The revised artwork plus the feedback slip, showing the note
  received and the revision made.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to one thing I changed after feedback and
  say *why* it makes my message clearer?"

## Home connection

Look at one photo, poster, or screen at home. Name the frame (wide or close), the angle
(low, high, or eye level), and one thing emphasized — then say what the maker probably
wanted you to feel.
