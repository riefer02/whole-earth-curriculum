# Lesson 4 — Performing with Dynamics, Phrasing, and Ensemble — and Revising by Recording

## Summary

Learners perform a short piece — a **chant, rhythm pattern, round, or melody** — with
attention to **dynamics** (loud and soft), **phrasing** (musical sentences shaped like
breaths), and **ensemble** (many parts on one shared beat). They record a first take, listen
back, choose one specific revision, and perform again. Expression is reachable many ways —
voices, bodies, movement, and found sound — so no one needs a particular instrument or a
spoken voice to join.

## Objectives

- Perform a piece with attention to dynamics, phrasing, and ensemble, and revise after
  listening to a recording. _(D07.S2.07.01)_

## Connection

A choir doesn't just sing the notes; it decides *how loud, how shaped, and how together*. The
same song whispered feels secret; belted feels brave; with everyone landing exactly together
it feels powerful, and with one voice late it feels unsure. This is true of a drum circle, a
soccer chant, a call-and-response in a classroom, or a lullaby. Today you are the performer
who *chooses* those effects — then listens back to your own sound and makes it better, the
way musicians everywhere rehearse with their own recordings.

## Materials

- Performer-tools poster (dynamics / phrasing / ensemble)
- A short piece or chant to perform
- A recording device (or a listening partner)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the performer-tools poster.
- Choose or let groups choose a short, learnable piece — a call-and-response chant, a round,
  a rhythm pattern, or a simple melody — reachable by voice, body percussion, movement, or
  found sound.
- Set up one recording device per group, or assign each group a "human recorder" listener.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **dynamics, phrasing,
and ensemble are chosen, not accidental** — and that **a recording is a mirror for the ear**:
it lets a performer hear what an audience hears and revise. Model each tool with the worked
example before groups perform (explicit instruction for a novice skill — Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Keep performance **accessible to every body**: dynamics can be loud/soft *or*
big/small movement, fast/slow energy, or full/empty space; phrasing can be shaped with the
breath, a gesture, or a drawn arc; ensemble means matching a shared beat, which can be seen
or felt, not only heard. No learner is required to sing or speak — offer signed, movement,
and found-sound parts. Call-and-response and interlocking rhythms (e.g., Andean siku panpipes
or West African drumming) are living traditions; frame them with respect, and let learners
perform the piece *they* know or make, not an imitation of a tradition they do not belong to.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Last lesson you learned to *hear* how sound makes mood in
   media. Today you make the sound yourself — and listen back to your own.
2. **Meet the three tools (15 min).** Look at the poster. **Dynamics** is how loud or soft —
   from *p* (soft) to *f* (loud), and everything building in between. **Phrasing** is playing
   a musical sentence shaped like a breath: it rises, peaks, and settles, instead of a flat
   run of notes. **Ensemble** is many parts moving on one shared beat — when everyone lands
   together, the group sounds like one thing instead of many. Model each: clap a steady beat,
   then clap one phrase soft-to-loud, then have half the room clap while half steps, on the
   same beat.
3. **Learn the piece (10 min).** In your group, learn the short piece. Decide, together: where
   will it be soft, where loud (dynamics)? where does each phrase breathe (phrasing)? and what
   is the shared beat that keeps you together (ensemble)? Practice once.
4. **Record the first take (5 min).** Record (or have your listener attend to) one full take.
   This is not a test — it is a mirror.
5. **Listen and revise (10 min).** Listen back. Ask three questions: *Did the louds and softs
   show? Did the phrases breathe, or did it run flat? Did we land together, or drift apart?*
   Choose **one** thing to fix, say what it is, and rehearse just that part.
6. **Record the second take (5 min).** Perform again, recorded. Notice the difference — that
   is revision, made audible.
7. **Close (5 min).** You made a sound, heard it back, and made it better. That loop — do,
   listen, revise — is how every ensemble on Earth rehearses.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Limit the piece to one phrase and one dynamics change ("soft the first time,
  loud the second"), with the beat clapped by a partner.
- **Extension:** Mark the dynamics and phrase shapes on a written copy of the piece before
  performing, then check the recording against your marks and note any drift.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner demonstrate one dynamics choice, one
  phrase shape, and staying on the shared beat — and name one change they made after hearing
  the recording?
- **Artifact (performance):** The two takes (or a partner's written description of the
  change), showing a revision made after listening.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I point to the moment in the recording where I
  made my one revision, and say *why* it made the piece stronger?"

## Home connection

Record yourself (or have someone listen to you) doing a short rhythm, chant, or tune. Listen
back and name one thing you would change — then do it once more.
