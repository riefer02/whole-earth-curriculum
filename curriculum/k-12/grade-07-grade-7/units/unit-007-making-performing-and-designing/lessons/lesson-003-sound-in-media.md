# Lesson 3 — Sound in Media: How Film, Games, and Ads Shape Mood and Meaning

## Summary

Learners discover that **sound does invisible work** in media: the same image paired with
soft slow music, a sudden **sting**, or a bouncy rhythm reads as calm, fearful, or playful.
They learn the vocabulary — **soundtrack, sound effect, sting, underscore, leitmotif** —
analyze one film, game, or ad scene, and practice noticing *who chose the sound and what
they wanted us to feel*, including when sound is used to sell.

## Objectives

- Explain how sound is used in media such as film, games, and ads to shape mood and meaning.
  _(D07.S2.07.02)_

## Connection

Next time a movie makes your heart pound, notice what you are *hearing*, not just seeing: a
low drone creeping up, a sudden silence, then a sting. Sound is the reason a hallway feels
safe one moment and haunted the next — even though the picture never changed. Games do it
too: a soft loop means "explore, you're safe," and a rising beat means "get ready." Ads do it
most of all — a cheerful tune can make a drink feel like a party. Today you learn to *hear*
the mood being built, and to name who is building it and why.

## Materials

- Sound-mood poster (one picture, three sounds)
- Sound-mood worksheet
- Three short media clips (optional)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the sound-mood poster.
- Copy a sound-mood worksheet for each learner.
- If using clips, cue a calm scene, a tense scene, and an ad, each with clear sound design;
  have the low-tech voice-and-clap path ready if there are no devices.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **sound is a
deliberate, often hidden, meaning-maker**: a learner should be able to name the mood a sound
builds and connect it to a *choice* (tempo, volume, silence, a recurring theme). Model the
one-picture-three-sounds worked example fully (explicit instruction for a novice skill —
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the ethics and egalitarian lenses visible: ads and
trailers use sound to steer feeling, and the question "who benefits from this feeling?" is
always fair. Be aware that some learners cannot hear (or hear differently); sound analysis
must not exclude them — describe every sound in words, and offer visual alternatives such as
reading a caption or watching a silent scene and imagining its score. Sound traditions differ
across the world (film scores from Nollywood, Bollywood, Studio Ghibli, and video-game
composers in Japan and Korea), so keep examples global rather than one industry's default.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** In Lessons 1–2 you made images carry a message. Today you
   learn how *sound* carries mood — often without anyone noticing it working.
2. **Meet the one-picture-three-sounds idea (15 min).** Look at the poster: one drawing — a
   hallway with a half-open door. Now hear it three ways (hum, clap, or imagine): **soft,
   slow music** (gentle waves) → *calm, safe*; a **sudden loud sting** (a sharp spike) →
   *fear, alarm*; a **cheerful, bouncy rhythm** (skipping dots) → *playful, funny*. The
   picture never changed; the *sound* carried the meaning. The tools have names: the
   **soundtrack** (the music), **sound effects** (the door creak, the footstep), the **sting**
   (a sudden loud hit), the **underscore** (music under speech), and the **leitmotif** (a
   short tune that returns each time a character or idea appears). Say them once with a
   partner.
3. **Analyze one scene (15 min).** Watch or recall one short film, game, or ad scene. On your
   worksheet, fill three columns: the **scene**, the **sound** you hear (music? effects?
   silence?), and the **mood/meaning** it makes. Then ask the bigger question: *who chose this
   sound, and what did they want me to feel — and is that feeling true to the scene or a trick
   to sell me something?*
4. **Try it muted / imagined (10 min).** Watch the same scene with the sound off (or close
   your eyes and imagine the picture). What is lost? What did the sound alone add? Write one
   sentence.
5. **Share and compare (5 min).** With a partner, name one sound choice you each found and
   the mood it built. Notice how sound makes meaning even when we never see a word.
6. **Close (5 min).** You can now *hear the mood being made*. Next lesson you become the
   sound-maker yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a word bank for moods (calm, fearful, playful, sad, exciting, tense) and
  a sentence frame: "The ___ (music/sting/silence) makes the scene feel ___ because ___."
- **Extension:** Find or invent an example of a **leitmotif** — a tune that returns for one
  character or idea — and write how its return changes a scene's meaning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name one sound choice (music, effect,
  sting, or silence) and connect it to the mood or meaning it creates, in their own chosen
  example?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed sound-mood worksheet, kept for the unit portfolio.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I name a mood a sound built and say *which* sound
  choice made it — tempo, volume, silence, or a returning theme?"

## Home connection

Next time you watch or play something, pause once and ask: what would this exact moment feel
like with the sound off? Name what the sound was doing.
