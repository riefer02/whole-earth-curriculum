# Lesson 5 — Gesture, Posture, Spacing: Speaking Without Words

## Summary

Learners discover that **gesture, posture, and spacing** carry meaning without a single word —
open palms welcome, crossed arms close off, a lean forward shows interest, and nearness reads
as intimate while distance reads as formal. They learn that these cues differ across cultures,
that **sign languages are full languages** (not mere gestures), and that precise gesture
vocabularies such as **Bharatanatyam mudras** are living traditions. They then read and
perform meaning with their bodies alone.

## Objectives

- Explain how gesture, posture, and spacing communicate meaning without words, using examples
  from performance or daily life. _(D07.S3.07.02)_

## Connection

Think how much you already say without words. A friend opens their arms and you feel
welcomed before they speak; a classmate crosses theirs and you give them space; someone leans
in and you know they are really listening. Across a room, standing close feels like "us" and
standing far feels like "them." Bodies do this everywhere — in a market, a greeting, a
celebration, a quiet argument. Today you learn to *read* that silent language and to *speak*
it on purpose — and to remember that not every body reads it the same way.

## Materials

- Bodies-speak poster (five poses + spacing)
- Gesture-matching cards
- Meaning slips

## Preparation

- Print or draw the bodies-speak poster.
- Copy the gesture-matching cards and meaning slips.
- Recall Grade 6: learners told a story with movement, voice, and one more element. This
  lesson isolates the silent channel — gesture, posture, and spacing — and reads it precisely.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **the body is a
language, and like any language its meanings are learned, not universal** — a learner should
be able to name a gesture, a posture, and a spacing choice and the meaning each carries, and
to note that the same cue can mean different things to different people. Model the five poses
and the spacing strip (explicit instruction for a novice skill — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). Hold two careful distinctions: (1) **sign languages are complete languages** with
their own grammar, not "just gestures," and are the first language of millions of people —
never reduce them to pantomime; (2) **gesture meanings differ across cultures** — a
thumbs-up or a head-shake is not the same everywhere, and eye contact, personal space, and
bowing all vary — so keep the stance "we ask, we don't assume" (global/anthropological lens).
Performance here is open to every body: meaning can be shown from a seated position, with one
hand, or with the eyes, and no learner must stand, leap, or move in a way their body does not
do.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** You have made images speak and sound make mood. Now the
   body itself — the instrument you carry everywhere — becomes the message.
2. **Meet the silent language (15 min).** Look at the poster. **Gesture** — open palms up
   says *welcome, offering*; arms crossed says *closed, unsure*. **Posture** — leaning
   forward with a lifted face says *interested, listening*; standing tall with chin up says
   *confident, proud*; slouched with head down says *tired, low*. **Spacing** — standing
   close says *together, intimate*; standing far says *distant, formal*. Read each with a
   partner, then freeze in one pose and let a partner guess it.
3. **Name the limits (10 min).** Two honest notes. First, **sign languages** — American Sign
   Language, Lengua de Señas Mexicana, Nicaraguan Sign Language, and hundreds more — are
   complete languages with their own grammar, not "just gestures"; millions of people use them as a first language. Second,
   these cues are **not universal**: in some cultures a thumbs-up is rude, eye contact means
   different things, and comfortable personal space differs. And in Bharatanatyam, a classical
   dance of South India, a dancer's precise hand gestures (**mudras**) each carry a named
   meaning — a whole vocabulary in the fingers. When you meet a meaning you are not sure of,
   you *ask*.
4. **Read the room (10 min).** With your pair, match each gesture card to its meaning. Then
   trade one real example: a time a body told you something the words did not — a friend's
   shoulders, a stranger's step back, a crowd's hush.
5. **Speak silently (10 min).** Draw a meaning slip and show it with **body only** — gesture,
   posture, and where you stand — while a partner guesses. Then switch.
6. **Close (5 min).** You now read and speak a language everyone uses and few people name.
   Next lesson you put it together with timing and a group into a real performance.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a two-choice prompt ("does this read as welcome or closed?") and a
  sentence frame: "This body says ___ because the ___ (arms/lean/spacing) is ___."
- **Extension:** Choose one precise gesture system — a mudra or a sign — and write one
  sentence on how it differs from everyday body language.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner demonstrate one gesture, one posture, and
  one spacing choice, and name the meaning each carries — in a given example or one of their
  own?
- **Artifact (performance):** The silent-meaning guess-and-show, observed or self-noted.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I show a meaning with my body alone and say *which*
  channel — gesture, posture, or spacing — carries it, and note that it might read differently
  to someone else?"

## Home connection

Watch one quiet moment today — a greeting, a queue, a goodbye — and name one gesture, one
posture, and one spacing choice you saw, and what each said.
