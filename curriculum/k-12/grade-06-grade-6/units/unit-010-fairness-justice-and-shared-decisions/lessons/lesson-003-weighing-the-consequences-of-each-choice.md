# Lesson 3 — Weighing the Consequences of Each Choice

## Summary

Learners practice **weighing the consequences of each choice** in a moral dilemma —
asking *who is helped* and *who is harmed* by each choice, and *how big* each consequence
is — before reaching a considered decision. They weigh a worked example, then weigh
dilemma cards in pairs and record a decision of their own.

## Objectives

- Describe a moral dilemma (two right-seeming choices in conflict) and weigh the
  consequences of each. _(D09.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Last time you held a dilemma — two right-seeming choices, pulling opposite ways. Now the
next move: before you decide, *weigh* each choice. What happens **to whom** if you keep
the secret? What happens **to whom** if you tell? Consequences have weight — some are
heavy, some light — and they fall on different people. Weighing them is not finding a
perfect answer; it is deciding *with your eyes open*, aware of who is helped and who is
harmed.

## Materials

- Consequence-weighing poster
- Weighing cards
- Weighing page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the consequence-weighing poster (two branches, who is helped/harmed under
  each, and a balance line to compare).
- Copy weighing cards for each pair and a weighing page for each learner.
- Prepare one dilemma (from Lesson 2) to weigh together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is a **consequence
weighing routine**: for *each* choice, name *who is helped* and *who is harmed*, then
compare *how big* each consequence is before deciding. Model it once as a worked example
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The egalitarian heart is the question *who else is
affected?* — not only the chooser. Do **not** mark a learner's considered decision as
"wrong"; ask instead whether they *weighed both choices* and noticed who each helped and
harmed. Distinguish **evidence from values**: *what the consequences are and who they
fall on* can be reasoned and observed; *which consequence matters most* is a value
judgment — and different people weigh the same facts differently. Hold it open: the unit
treats these as questions, never settled as dogma. If a dilemma involves real risk of
harm, the lesson's safety move — bringing in a trusted adult — is always a legitimate
consequence to weigh. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You can now describe a dilemma: two right-seeming choices in
   conflict. Try to recall one dilemma from last time and its two choices. (Pause.) Today
   you weigh them.
2. **Meet the weighing (10 min).** Look at the poster. For **each** choice, ask two
   questions: *Who is helped?* and *Who is harmed?* Then compare: *how big* is each
   consequence, and on *whom* does it fall? Consequences are not all equal — some are
   heavy, some light. Weighing means putting them on the balance *before* you decide.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Weigh the dilemma together. "Keep the secret, or tell a
   trusted adult?" Under Choice A write: helped — the friend (trust kept); harmed — the
   person at risk. Under Choice B: helped — the person at risk; harmed — the friend
   (trust broken). Now compare: which consequences are heavier, and on whom? Say your
   considered decision and the reason.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a weighing card. For each choice, name who
   is helped and who is harmed. Then weigh them — which is heavier, and on whom — and
   decide together, with a reason: "We chose ___ because, on balance, ___."
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, take a dilemma of your own, list the
   consequences of each choice (who is helped, who is harmed), and write your considered
   decision. There is no single right answer — the point is that you *weighed* both sides
   first.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn two-branch page with one branch's consequences filled
  in; the learner completes the other branch and then decides.
- **Extension:** Weigh consequences for *several* people, not just one, and explain how
  considering more people changes the call.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name, for each choice, at least one person
  helped and one harmed, and state a decision that follows from the weighing?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I weigh *both* choices before deciding? Did I
  notice who else was affected, not just myself?"

## Home connection

Next time a choice at home affects more than one person, name who is helped and who is
harmed by each option — then decide.
