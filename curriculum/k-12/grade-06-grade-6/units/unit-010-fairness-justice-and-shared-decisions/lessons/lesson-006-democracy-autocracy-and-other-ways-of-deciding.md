# Lesson 6 — Democracy, Autocracy & Other Ways of Deciding

## Summary

Learners compare **democracy** (many people decide, often by vote), **autocracy** (one
person or a small group decides), and **other ways of deciding** such as **consensus**
(talk until everyone agrees) — holding each with *strengths and limits*, and never
assuming one is "the best." They match scenarios to methods, then record one strength
and one limit of each.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between a democracy, an autocracy, and other ways of making
  decisions, and their strengths and limits. _(D09.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

A group of people must make one decision. How? Three ways, and you have lived all three:
sometimes the **many** decide (a vote — the class raises hands); sometimes **one** decides
(a cook says "we close the kitchen now" during a fire); sometimes everyone talks until
**everyone agrees** (a family naming a shared garden, a council that will not stop until
all consent). Each way has *strengths* and each has *limits*. None is perfect everywhere —
and that is the honest thing to notice.

## Materials

- Three-ways poster
- Decision-method cards
- Compare page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-ways poster (three panels, each with a "strength" and a "limit"
  line).
- Copy decision-method cards for each group and a compare page for each learner.
- Prepare one small decision the group will actually make three ways (see the low-tech
  path) if you want the felt experience.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: a **democracy** is a
way of deciding where the *many* decide (often by vote); an **autocracy** is a way where
*one person or a small group* holds the power to decide; and there are **other ways**,
such as **consensus** (talk until everyone agrees), used by many peoples and communities
around the world. Teach each **with strengths AND limits — never as "the good one vs the
bad one."** This is the unit's no-single-model commitment: democracy is *not* assumed
superior, autocracy is *not* cartoon villainy, and consensus is *not* the hidden "right"
answer — each fits some situations and strains in others (a vote includes many voices but
can overlook a minority; one-person rule is fast but crowds out other voices; consensus
honors everyone but can be slow). Model the compare as a worked example (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006). Distinguish **evidence from values**: *that* these ways differ
and have these tendencies is descriptive and observable across societies; *which* to
prefer is a value question — and this curriculum's own egalitarian value (that every
person's voice deserves to matter) is a value, not a fact, so it is named as one. For the
"other ways" panel, name **consensus** honestly and with a real example — the Haudenosaunee
(Iroquois) Confederacy and its Great Law of Peace use councils that deliberate until
agreement, a living tradition, not a museum piece. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have seen how systems can leave people out. Now: how do groups
   *decide* in the first place? Try to recall a time a group you were in decided something
   — how did it happen? (Pause.) Today you compare the main ways.
2. **Meet the three ways (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Democracy:** the *many* decide,
   often by vote — everyone gets a say, and leaders can be changed. **Autocracy:** *one
   person or a small group* decides — fast and clear, but one voice rules. **Other ways:**
   many groups use **consensus** — talking until *everyone* agrees, so no one is
   overruled. Each way has a **strength** and a **limit**; none is perfect everywhere.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Compare together. *Democracy* — strength: many voices are
   heard; limit: the majority can overlook a minority. *Autocracy* — strength: fast, clear
   decisions; limit: others may not be heard, and power can be misused. *Consensus* —
   strength: everyone agrees; limit: it can take a long time. Say each pair aloud.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take decision-method cards. Match each scenario
   to a way of deciding, and for each name one **strength** and one **limit**. Notice:
   the *same* decision can be made more than one way — the question is which trade-offs
   you accept.
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, write one strength and one limit of each
   way — democracy, autocracy, consensus — and one real situation where you think each
   might fit, with a reason. There is no single right ranking; defend your reasoning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank (vote, one person, everyone agrees, fast, slow, many
  voices, one voice) and a three-column chart with one cell filled.
- **Extension:** Weigh two methods for the *same* decision and defend which fits that
  situation better, naming the trade-off you accept.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe democracy, autocracy, and
  consensus, and give one strength and one limit of each — without ranking one as simply
  "best"?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name a strength *and* a limit for each way —
  not just a strength for my favorite and a limit for the others?"

## Home connection

Notice how a decision gets made at home or nearby this week — by one person, by a vote,
or by talking until everyone agrees — and name one strength and one limit of that way.
