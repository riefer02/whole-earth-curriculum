# Lesson 2 — A Moral Dilemma: Two Right-Seeming Choices

## Summary

Learners learn what a **moral dilemma** is — two *right-seeming* choices in conflict, so
neither is simply "the good one." They meet a worked dilemma, describe the two
right-seeming choices in dilemma cards, and write a dilemma of their own.

## Objectives

- Describe a moral dilemma (two right-seeming choices in conflict). _(D09.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

You promised to keep a friend's secret — but the secret means someone could get hurt.
Keep the promise, or speak up? Both choices *look* right: loyalty says "keep it,"
protection says "tell someone." A **moral dilemma** is exactly this — two right-seeming
choices pulling in opposite directions. There is no clean "good" button. Learning to
*see* the dilemma clearly — without rushing to pick — is the first step of thinking
well about it.

## Materials

- Dilemma poster
- Dilemma cards
- Dilemma record page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the dilemma poster (one situation, two right-seeming branches, each with
  a "why it feels right" note).
- Copy dilemma cards for each pair and a record page for each learner.
- Prepare one told dilemma for the opening.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is precise: a **moral
dilemma** is *two right-seeming choices in conflict* — neither is obviously "the good
one," and that is what makes it hard. Model one dilemma as a worked example before
pairs try their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep it **non-shaming and
open**: the point is to *describe* the dilemma, not to settle it — the weighing comes in
the next lesson, and the unit holds questions as questions, never answered as dogma.
Choose dilemmas that are age-appropriate and emotionally safe: keep a promise vs help
someone in need, tell the truth vs protect a friend, share scarce food vs save it for
family. Avoid dilemmas that force a child to choose between loyalties they cannot
safely resolve alone — if a dilemma involves real risk of harm, the move is to bring in
a trusted adult, and that is a right-seeming choice worth naming. Distinguish **evidence
from values**: *that* two choices both seem right is observable; *which* to choose is a
value question for the next lesson. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you sorted facts, beliefs, and values. Today a new
   kind of hard thing: a **moral dilemma**. Try to recall: what does "right" and "wrong"
   usually mean in a simple choice? (Pause.) A dilemma is when it is *not* that simple.
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** Look at the poster. A situation sits at the top. Two
   choices branch down — **Choice A** and **Choice B**. The key: *both* are
   **right-seeming**. Each one has a good reason behind it. A **moral dilemma** is two
   right-seeming choices in conflict — not "good vs bad," but "good vs good," pulling
   opposite ways.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Build one together. *Situation:* "You promised to keep
   your friend's secret, but the secret means someone could get hurt." Choice A: keep
   the secret (right-seeming because of loyalty). Choice B: tell a trusted adult
   (right-seeming because of protecting someone from harm). Name both, and say why each
   feels right. That tension — both right — *is* the dilemma.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a dilemma card. Name the situation, then
   the two right-seeming choices, then why each feels right. Say it in one sentence each:
   "Choice A feels right because ___; Choice B feels right because ___." If one choice
   feels *wrong*, keep looking — a real dilemma has two that seem right.
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, write a dilemma of your own (real or
   invented), name its two right-seeming choices, and say why each feels right. Do not
   decide yet — deciding is the next lesson.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a dilemma card with both choices already named; the learner adds
  why each feels right, using a sentence starter.
- **Extension:** Write a dilemma where the two choices are *almost equally* right-seeming,
  and explain in one line what makes it genuinely hard.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a situation and two right-seeming
  choices, and say why each feels right?
- **Peer check:** Partners check each other: "Are *both* of your choices right-seeming —
  or is one actually just wrong?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they faced two right-seeming choices, and name the two
reasons that pulled opposite ways.
