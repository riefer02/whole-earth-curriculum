# Lesson 10 — Restorative Justice vs. Punishment

## Summary

In the unit capstone, learners **explain the aims and limits of restorative justice and
compare it with punishment**, and revisit the unit's threads — fact/belief/value, fairness
vs justice, and conflict analysis. They compare a worked example, then response cards, and
record a considered comparison, holding the question open rather than settling it as
dogma.

## Objectives

- Explain the aims and limits of restorative justice, and compare it with punishment.
  _(D09.S4.06.02)_
- Revisit and apply the unit's threads — fact vs belief vs value, fairness vs justice, and
  conflict analysis — in one connected capstone task. _(D09.S1.06.01, D09.S2.06.01,
  D09.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Someone breaks a shared water jar, and everyone looks at what to do. One answer: a
**punishment** — the person loses a turn or a privilege. Another answer: **restorative
justice** — the person meets the one harmed, hears what the loss meant, takes
responsibility, and helps repair it. Both are *responses to harm*, and both have **aims**
and **limits**. Neither is magic, and neither is always right. Understanding both — and
when each helps — is the last skill of this unit, and it draws on everything before it.

## Materials

- Restorative-vs-punishment poster
- Response cards
- Comparison page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the restorative-vs-punishment poster (two columns, each with an "aim" and
  a "limit" line).
- Copy response cards for each group and a comparison page for each learner.
- Prepare one harm scenario to compare together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **restorative justice**
aims to *repair harm, meet the needs of the person harmed, and have the person who harmed
take responsibility* — its limits include that it *takes time and everyone's willingness*,
and may not fit every harm. **Punishment** aims at *a clear consequence for the wrong and
accountability* — its limits include that *the harm and the harmed person's needs may go
unrepaired*, and it may not teach how to do better. Compare them **non-shamingly and
honestly**: neither is "good" and the other "bad"; each has aims and limits, and the unit
holds the question — *which response fits this harm?* — as a question, never answered as
dogma. This is a **capstone**, so build in **spaced retrieval** of the unit's threads: a
quick recall of fact/belief/value, fairness vs justice, and conflict analysis, interleaved
with the new comparison (see `docs/contexts.md`; Dunlosky et al. note practice testing and
distributed practice as high-utility). Model the comparison once (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Distinguish **evidence from values**: *what each approach aims at and where
its limits are* is observable in real practices; *that repair and dignity matter* is a
value this curriculum holds and names as such. Keep restorative examples **non-shaming** —
the focus is repair and dignity, never humiliation. If a learner brings up serious harm,
hold the boundary: serious harm needs adults and proper systems, and this lesson is about
understanding the *ideas*, not resolving real cases. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — retrieve the unit (5 min).** You have come a long way. Without looking back,
   try to recall: what is the difference between a **fact**, a **belief**, and a **value**?
   Between **fairness** and **justice**? What do you name when you analyze a conflict?
   (Pause — spaced retrieval across the unit makes these stick.)
2. **Meet the two responses (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Restorative justice** responds
   to harm by *repairing it*: the person harmed is heard, the person who harmed takes
   responsibility, and the harm is made right as far as it can be. **Punishment** responds
   by giving a *consequence* for the wrong. Each has **aims** — and each has **limits**.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Compare together. *Harm:* a shared water jar is broken.
   *Restorative response:* the person hears what the loss meant, apologizes, and helps
   mend or replace it. *Punishment response:* the person loses a turn. Now name an **aim**
   and a **limit** of each: restorative aims to repair harm and heal the relationship, but
   takes time and willingness; punishment aims at a clear consequence, but the harm — and
   the harmed person's needs — may go unrepaired.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a response card. For each harm, name a
   restorative response and a punishment response, and give one **aim** and one **limit**
   of each. Notice: the same harm can be answered more than one way — the question is which
   fits, and that question stays open.
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your comparison page, write the aims and limits of each
   approach, and a short considered view: *for the broken-jar harm, which response would
   you lean toward, and why?* Use the unit's tools — name facts vs values, check fairness,
   name both sides' needs — in your answer. This page is the unit's capstone portfolio
   piece.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a two-column chart with the aims already listed; the learner adds
  one limit to each and circles the response they would choose, with a sentence starter.
- **Extension:** Weigh a case where restorative justice reaches its limits (e.g., the harm
  is denied, or too big for a circle) and explain what a fair response might then require.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner state at least one aim and one
  limit of restorative justice and one of punishment, and give a reasoned comparison that
  uses the unit's tools (fact/value, fairness, both sides' needs)?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name an aim *and* a limit for *both* approaches
  — and did I hold the question open rather than declaring one always right?"

## Home connection

Talk with someone at home about how your family or community responds when harm happens —
repair or consequence — and name one aim and one limit of the way they usually use.
