# Lesson 8 — Seeing Both Sides of a Conflict

## Summary

Learners practice **analyzing a conflict to identify each side's needs and feelings**.
They learn to ask, for each side, *what does this side feel?* and *what does this side
need?* — on a worked example, then conflict cards in pairs, and record a two-sides
analysis. Proposing a fair resolution follows in the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Analyze a conflict to identify each side's needs and feelings. _(D09.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Two neighbors, one shared water tap, and not enough water for both on a dry day. One is
worried about the garden; the other is frustrated about cooking and washing. A conflict
is not one person right and the other wrong — it is two sets of **feelings** and **needs**
bumping into each other. Before anyone can fix it, someone has to *see* both sides
clearly. That seeing is what you will practice today.

## Materials

- Two-sides poster
- Conflict cards
- Needs-and-feelings page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-sides poster (conflict in the center, two side panels, each with
  a "feeling" and a "need" line).
- Copy conflict cards for each pair and a needs-and-feelings page for each learner.
- Prepare one told conflict for the opening.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is a **conflict-analysis
routine**: for *each* side, name the **feeling** (what this side is experiencing) and the
**need** (what this side needs or cares about underneath the feeling). Model it once as a
worked example (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep it **non-shaming and even-handed**:
no side is "the bad one" — the analysis is about *seeing*, not judging, and both sides'
needs are usually partly legitimate. The egalitarian move is that **every side gets
named**, including the quieter or less powerful one — a conflict where one side's voice is
missing cannot be analyzed fairly. Distinguish **evidence from values**: *what each side
feels and needs* is observable and inferable; *that both sides' needs deserve to be heard*
is a value this curriculum holds and offers as such. Draw a clear safety boundary: if a
conflict involves harm, threat, or real danger, the first move is to bring in a trusted
adult — analysis and resolution of serious harm is *not* a child's solo task. Resolution
comes next lesson; today is only *seeing*. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have studied fairness, justice, and change. Now a skill for
   real life: **seeing a conflict from both sides**. Try to recall a disagreement you saw
   — what did *each* side want? (Pause.) Today you practice naming it clearly.
2. **Meet the routine (10 min).** Look at the poster. A conflict sits in the center. For
   **each side**, ask two questions: *What does this side **feel**?* (the emotion — worried,
   frustrated, hurt) and *What does this side **need**?* (the thing underneath — water,
   safety, a turn, to be heard). Feelings are the surface; needs are what is underneath.
   Analyzing means naming **both**, for **both** sides.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Analyze together. *Conflict:* "Two neighbors, one shared
   water tap." Side 1: feeling — worried; need — water for the garden. Side 2: feeling —
   frustrated; need — water for cooking and washing. Name all four, and notice: both
   needs are real. That is what makes it a *conflict*, not a simple right-and-wrong.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a conflict card. For each side, name the
   **feeling** and the **need**. Then say it aloud: "Side 1 feels ___ and needs ___; Side 2
   feels ___ and needs ___." Check each other: did you name *both* sides, not just the one
   you agree with?
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, fill both panels for a conflict you choose —
   each side's feeling and need. Do not solve it yet; solving is the next lesson.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with one side's feeling and need filled in; the learner
  completes the other side, using a feeling word bank (worried, frustrated, hurt, left
  out) and a need word bank (safety, a turn, to be heard, fair share).
- **Extension:** Name what the two sides *share* (a need they have in common) — often the
  hidden bridge to a resolution.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name, for a given conflict, a feeling and a
  need for *each* side — not just one?
- **Peer check:** Partners check each other: "Did you name a feeling *and* a need for
  *both* sides?"

## Home connection

Next time you see a disagreement at home or nearby, quietly name each side's feeling and
need before saying who is right.
