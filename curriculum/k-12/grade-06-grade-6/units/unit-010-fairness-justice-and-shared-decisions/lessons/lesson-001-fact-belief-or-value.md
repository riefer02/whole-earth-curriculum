# Lesson 1 — Fact, Belief, or Value?

## Summary

Learners learn to tell the difference between a **fact** (a claim about how things are,
that evidence can check), a **belief** (a claim a person holds but cannot fully check
now), and a **value** (a claim about what *ought* to be). They sort a worked example,
then sort statement cards in pairs and record one fact, one belief, and one value from
their own life.

## Objectives

- Identify the difference between a fact, a belief, and a value in a short text or
  conversation. _(D09.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Three sentences, side by side. "The river floods every year." "There is life on other
worlds." "People should be kind." They sound similar — but they are three *different
kinds* of claim. The first can be checked with evidence; the second is held but cannot
be proven today; the third says what *ought* to be. Telling them apart is how you know
what to ask next: "Is that true? How do you know? Do you mean 'should'?" — at a market
stall, in a comment section, or anywhere people talk.

## Materials

- Fact–belief–value poster
- Statement cards
- Sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fact–belief–value poster (three columns, one worked example each,
  and the "how do I tell?" question under each).
- Copy statement cards for each pair and a sorting page for each learner.
- Prepare three spoken statements (one of each kind) for the opening.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is a precise,
three-way distinction: a **fact** is a claim about how things are that *evidence can
check* (true or false); a **belief** is a claim a person *holds* but cannot fully check
right now (it might be true or false, but we cannot settle it with what we have); a
**value** is a claim about what *ought* to be (it is neither true nor false in the
factual sense — it is about what matters). Teach the sort as a worked example before
independent practice (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Do **not** rank beliefs or
values against each other — the point is to *tell the kinds apart*, not to say which is
better. Distinguish **evidence from values** (`docs/philosophy.md` §5): a fact is
settled by evidence; a belief is held when evidence is not yet enough; a value is a
"should," not a "is." Keep the question open: this lesson teaches *classification*,
not a verdict on anyone's beliefs or values. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You already know what a value is — something you care about that
   guides your choices. Without looking back, try to recall it in your own words. (Pause
   — retrieval helps learning stick.) Today you meet its two companions: **fact** and
   **belief**.
2. **Meet the three kinds (10 min).** Look at the poster. A **fact** is a claim about
   how things are, and evidence can check it — like "the river floods every year." A
   **belief** is a claim a person holds but cannot fully check *now* — like "there is
   life on other worlds." It might be true or false, but we cannot settle it today. A
   **value** is a claim about what *ought* to be — like "people should be kind." It is
   not true or false the way a fact is; it is about what matters.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Build the sort together. Take a statement card. Ask the
   three "how do I tell?" questions: *Can evidence check it now?* (then it is a fact.)
   *Is it held, but not provable now?* (then it is a belief.) *Is it about what should
   be?* (then it is a value.) Sort three cards together, one of each kind, and say the
   reason for each.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a set of statement cards. Sort each one
   into **fact**, **belief**, or **value**, and for each, say the reason aloud: "This is
   a ___ because ___." If a card could go two ways, say which reading makes it which —
   that is the interesting part.
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, write one **fact** you can check, one
   **belief** you hold but cannot prove, and one **value** you care about — each with a
   one-line reason for why it belongs in that column.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a smaller card set with very clear examples and the three
  "how do I tell?" questions printed as a checklist.
- **Extension:** Take one tricky statement that could be read two ways and explain which
  reading makes it a fact, a belief, or a value — and how you would check the fact.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a given statement into fact, belief,
  or value, and give the "how do I tell?" reason?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their three examples and asks, "Would evidence settle
  my fact? Is my belief genuinely not provable now? Is my value really about what ought
  to be?"

## Home connection

Listen for three statements at home or nearby — one fact, one belief, one value — and
tell someone how you could tell which was which.
