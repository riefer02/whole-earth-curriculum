# Lesson 9 — Proposing a Fair Resolution

## Summary

Learners practice **proposing a fair resolution** to a conflict — one that honors both
sides' needs and that *both sides accept*. Building on Lesson 8, they name both needs,
brainstorm two or three ideas, test each for fairness, and agree on one, then resolve
scenarios in pairs and record a resolution of their own.

## Objectives

- Analyze a conflict to identify each side's needs and feelings, and propose a fair
  resolution. _(D09.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Last time you saw a conflict clearly — two neighbors, one tap, two real needs. Seeing is
the first half; the second half is a **fair resolution**: not one side winning, but a way
both sides can accept. A shared schedule — garden water in the cool morning, household
water at midday — meets *both* needs. A fair resolution is a bridge both people can walk
across together. Today you build bridges.

## Materials

- Fair-resolution poster
- Resolution scenarios
- Resolution page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fair-resolution poster (two sides' needs → a middle resolution, with
  the steps: name both needs, brainstorm, test for fairness, agree).
- Copy resolution scenarios for each pair and a resolution page for each learner.
- Prepare one conflict (from Lesson 8) to resolve together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is a **fair-resolution
routine**: (1) restate both sides' needs; (2) **brainstorm** two or three possible
resolutions; (3) **test each for fairness** — does it honor both needs, or only one?; (4)
**choose the one both sides accept**. This is a skill, so **model the full routine once**
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep it **non-shaming and genuinely mutual**: a fair
resolution must be accepted by *both* sides — not imposed, not "majority rules," and not
the quieter side giving in. The egalitarian move is that a resolution that only the louder
side accepts is *not* fair, however reasonable it sounds. Distinguish **evidence from
values**: *whether a resolution meets both sides' stated needs* can be checked; *that
fairness requires both sides' acceptance* is a value this curriculum holds and names as
such. Draw a clear safety boundary: if a conflict involves harm, threat, or real danger,
the first move is a trusted adult — a peer never resolves serious harm alone. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You can now name each side's feelings and needs in a conflict. Try
   to recall the two-neighbors conflict: what did each side need? (Pause — retrieval.)
   Today you turn seeing into a **fair resolution**.
2. **Meet the steps (10 min).** Look at the poster. Four moves: **name both needs** (say
   them aloud, both of them); **brainstorm** two or three possible resolutions; **test
   each for fairness** — does it honor *both* needs, or only one?; **choose the one both
   sides accept**. A resolution only one side accepts is not a fair resolution.
3. **Worked example (10 min).** Build one together. *Conflict:* the shared tap. Name both
   needs: garden water; cooking and washing water. Brainstorm: (a) only one side gets it;
   (b) a shared schedule. Test: (a) honors only one need — not fair; (b) honors both — a
   shared schedule: garden in the cool morning, household at midday. Choose (b), and check:
   do *both* accept? Yes.
4. **Practice with a partner (10 min).** Take a resolution scenario. Name both needs,
   brainstorm two or three ideas, test each for fairness, and choose the one **both sides
   accept**. If the first idea fails the fairness test, brainstorm again. Remember: if
   there is harm, threat, or danger, bring in a trusted adult — never resolve serious harm
   alone.
5. **Record your own (10 min).** On your page, write each side's needs, your two or three
   ideas, which one you chose, and why both sides accept it. This page pairs with
   Lesson 8's analysis as a portfolio piece.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a scenario with the two needs already named and two ready-made
  resolutions to test; the learner picks the fair one and says why both accept it.
- **Extension:** Resolve a conflict where the two needs partly conflict, and explain why
  your resolution is fair to *both* — not just a compromise that satisfies neither.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/observation):** Can the learner propose a resolution that
  honors both sides' stated needs, and can both sides accept it?
- **Peer check:** Partners check each other: "Does your resolution meet *both* needs, and
  would *both* sides accept it — not just the louder one?"

## Home connection

Next time a disagreement comes up at home, name both needs and offer two ideas, then pick
the one both accept.
