# Lesson 2 — The Same Sun, Shown Differently: Compare Two Cultures and Times

## Summary

Learners compare how the same subject — the sun — is shown differently across two
cultures and time periods, and **offer a reason** for the difference. A worked example
reads an ancient Egyptian winged sun disk (divine protection, gold on temple stone)
against a Japanese Edo-period woodblock red sun disc (flat, printed, part of the
landscape), then learners compare further suns — an Inca Inti gold face, a modern child's
crayon sun — always as **living and significant images to honor, never to rank**.

## Objectives

- Compare how the same subject is shown differently across two cultures or time periods
  and offer a reason. _(D07.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

The sun rises on everyone, everywhere — over a desert temple, a fishing harbor, a
mountain farm, a city street. Yet when people draw it, they draw it *differently*: one
community makes it a god's face in gold, another a flat red circle over the sea, another
a child's yellow circle with lines for rays. The difference is not a mistake, and not a
"better" or "worse" picture. Each image is a choice shaped by where and when it was made —
what materials the place gives, what the people believe the sun *is* and *does*. Reading
those choices is how we meet another time and place with respect and wonder.

## Materials

- Sun comparison cards
- Compare-and-reason chart

## Preparation

- Prepare the sun cards, each labeled with its place and period.
- Copy a compare-and-reason chart for each learner.
- Recall Grade 5: learners explained how an artwork reflects its culture's place,
  materials, or beliefs, citing what they observe; Grade 4: they named subject, materials,
  and likely purpose, and asked one respectful question.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **non-ranking
comparison with a reason**: we look closely at what each image is *made of* and *shows*,
connect those observations to a place, time, material, or belief, and offer a *reason*
for the difference — never to call one "more advanced." These are **living and significant
images**; the Egyptian winged disk and the Japanese rising-sun motif are meaningful to
people today, and the Inca Inti belongs to Andean communities — present them with
reverence, not as curiosities. Note for sensitivity: the Japanese rising-sun *motif*
predates and is distinct from the modern flag's contested uses; keep the lesson on the
artwork, not the flag. If a learner (or their family) belongs to a tradition shown here,
invite them to be the authority on its meaning and never correct them about their own
culture. See `docs/facilitation.md` on honoring living traditions and `docs/contexts.md`
on cultural power dynamics.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you used line, shape, color, balance, and contrast on
   purpose. Today you use those same eyes to compare one subject shown many ways: the
   **sun** — and offer a reason for each difference.
2. **Meet the comparison (15 min).** Look at the first two cards. *Ancient Egypt:* a
   winged sun disk — a circle with outstretched wings and rays, carved and painted in
   gold and pigment on temple stone. What I observe: a circle *with wings*. What it tells
   me: the sun is a **divine power** that protects — gold is the metal that never tarnishes,
   fitting a god, and it stands on temple stone to be honored. *Japan, Edo period:* a
   woodblock print of a flat red disc low over waves and a mountain. What I observe: a
   plain, *flat* disc, printed in ink and color on paper. What it tells me: the sun is
   part of the **land** — simple and symbolic — and printing lets many homes hold the
   image. Same subject, two very different pictures. The reason lies in *materials*
   (gold on stone vs ink on paper), *belief* (a god to honor vs a symbol of the land), and
   *purpose* (a temple wall vs a printed page for many people).
3. **Compare two more (15 min).** Choose two more suns — an **Inca Inti** gold sun face
   (the sun god of the Andes, a face surrounded by rays in worked gold) and a **modern
   child's crayon sun** (a circle with radiating lines, drawn for play). Fill your chart
   for each: what you observe, its place and time, its materials and purpose, and your
   reason for the difference.
4. **Share a reason (10 min).** In a small group, share one comparison and your reason.
   Listen for the difference between *what you see* (the observation) and *why it differs*
   (the reason). Notice that every image makes sense in its own place and time.
5. **Close (5 min).** The same sun has a thousand faces, and every one of them is true to
   someone, somewhere, sometime. To compare is not to judge; it is to understand. That is
   how we will meet art, music, and dance from around the world all through this unit.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sentence frame — "In ___ the sun is ___ (observation), but in ___ it
  is ___; the reason is ___" — and allow a single observation pair per learner.
- **Extension:** Bring a fifth sun from a tradition or image of your choice (with a
  source), complete a chart row, and offer a reason for how it differs from the others.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name at least one observable difference
  between two sun images and offer a reason — connected to place, time, material, belief,
  or purpose — without ranking one tradition against another?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name something I actually saw, and did I give a
  reason for the difference — speaking about living people and their images with respect?"

## Home connection

Find two pictures of the same subject (a home, a tree, an animal, a person) from two
different times or places, and name one difference and one reason for it.
