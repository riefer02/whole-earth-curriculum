# Lesson 3 — Compose and Notate a Melody or Rhythm

## Summary

Learners **compose** a short melody or rhythmic composition and **notate it so others can
follow it**. A worked example builds notation step by step — first a **beat grid** with
note symbols (ta, ti-ti), then a **pitch ladder** (do re mi sol la) that turns the rhythm
into a readable melody. Each learner notates their own two-bar piece, then trades with a
partner who must perform it exactly — the real test of whether the notation works.

## Objectives

- Create a melody or rhythmic composition and notate it so others can follow it.
  _(D07.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

A recipe written down lets someone you've never met cook your dish. A map lets a stranger
find the well. A musical score is the same idea in sound: marks on a page that tell
another person exactly what to sing or play — which sounds, and how long each one lasts.
When you **notate**, you hand your music to someone else, and it becomes *ours* instead of
only yours. Today you compose a short melody and write it down well enough that a partner
can play it back without hearing you first.

## Materials

- Notation poster
- Beat-grid and pitch-ladder page
- Sound makers

## Preparation

- Print or draw the notation poster, including the worked-example melody.
- Copy a beat-grid and pitch-ladder page for each learner.
- Recall Grade 4: learners read and played simple melodies and rhythms from basic
  notation; Grade 5: they composed a piece with a beginning, middle, and end.
- Arrange pairs so learners can trade and perform each other's notation.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **notation as a shared
language**: marks must be consistent enough that another person can follow them. Model the
worked example fully before learners compose (explicit instruction for a novice skill —
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Keep the notation concrete and simple: a beat grid
(one box per beat) with "ta" (one sound) and "ti-ti" (two sounds), plus a five-step pitch
ladder (do re mi sol la, the pentatonic scale) so melodies sound pleasant without wrong
notes. Do not demand staff notation; letter names or solfege are welcome for learners
ready to extend. Mention, as a global lens, that cultures notate music in many ways —
staff notation, Indian sargam syllables, Chinese gongche characters, Japanese koto
tablature, and graphic scores — all valid; we use one simple shared system today. The
partner performance is the feedback moment: if a partner cannot follow, that is
information for revision, never a verdict. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you compared how one subject is shown differently
   across cultures. Today you become the composer: you make a short melody and **write it
   down** so someone else can play it.
2. **Meet the beat grid (10 min).** Look at the poster. A beat is a steady pulse — tap it
   with your foot. We write it in **boxes**, one box per beat. One sound on a beat is a
   filled note, said "**ta**." Two quick sounds sharing a beat are two joined notes, said
   "**ti-ti**." Worked example: say and clap this four-beat pattern together — *ta, ta,
   ti-ti, ta* — and point to each box as you go.
3. **Add the pitch ladder (10 min).** Melody is rhythm *plus pitch* — high and low. We
   write pitch on a **ladder** of five steps: *do re mi sol la* (a pentatonic scale — no
   "wrong" notes, everything sounds friendly). Worked example: put your four beats onto
   the ladder — *do, do, re-mi, sol* — sing or hum it, then trace the noteheads on the
   poster as you sing. Now you have a **melody anyone can read**: which box (when) and
   which step (how high).
4. **Compose and notate yours (15 min).** On your page, fill two bars — eight beat boxes.
   Choose your rhythm (ta or ti-ti in each box) and your pitches (which step of the
   ladder). Make a short melody you like: it could climb, fall, or stay. Notate every
   sound clearly.
5. **Trade and perform (10 min).** Swap pages with a partner — no singing it first. Read
   their notation and perform it back. Then switch. Check: did it sound like what they
   meant? If something was unclear, fix the notation together — that is **feedback**, not
   failure.
6. **Close (5 min).** You just did what every composer from every tradition does: turned
   sound into marks that carry it to another person. Keep your notated melody — later in
   this unit you will build a performance on top of what you can write down.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Notate rhythm only (beat boxes with ta/ti-ti) first; add pitches only when
  rhythm is clear. Provide a pre-made rhythm to place on the ladder.
- **Support (non-visual and fine-motor options):** Notation need not be drawn or seen. A
  learner may notate aloud by speaking the sequence (solfege or letter names) while a
  partner repeats it back or scribes it, or may hum and clap the pattern while a partner
  writes it. The test is the same: can another person follow it?
- **Extension:** Notate with letter names (C D E G A) or staff notation, add a third bar,
  and write one sentence on what your melody expresses.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** Can a partner perform the learner's notation back
  accurately, showing the notation is followable — and can the learner fix an unclear mark
  when a partner stumbles?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The notated melody, kept for the unit portfolio.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could someone who never heard my melody still play it
  from my marks — and are my 'when' (beat) and 'how high' (pitch) both written down?"

## Home connection

Write down a short rhythm or melody you hum around the house — even just ta and ti-ti in
boxes — and ask someone to clap or sing it back from the page.
