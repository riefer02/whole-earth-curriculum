# Lesson 6 — Good Against Evil, Told Two Ways: Compare Two Dance-Dramas

## Summary

Learners compare how two **living dance-drama traditions** express one shared theme — the
struggle of **good against evil** — differently, and **offer a reason** for the difference.
A worked example reads **Kathakali** of Kerala (mudra hand gestures, navarasa facial
expressions, epic storytelling) against the Balinese **Barong** dance (masked lion and
witch, gamelan and chorus, good and evil held in balance), then learners compare an
alternative pairing, always meeting these traditions as living arts to honor, never as
specimens to rank.

## Objectives

- Compare how two traditions use movement or drama to express a shared theme, and offer a
  reason for the difference. _(D07.S3.06.02)_

## Connection

Every culture tells the same great story in its own body: the struggle between what
builds and what destroys, between good and evil. One theater tells it through a single
performer's hands and eyes — a flick of a finger means a king, a widening of the eyes
means wonder. Another tells it through masks and a whole village chanting, where neither
side wins forever because the point is *balance*, not victory. Neither is "better"; each
grew in a place, with its people's beliefs about how good and evil relate. Watching the
*difference* — the gesture, the mask, the chorus — is how we honor each tradition truly.

## Materials

- Dance-drama comparison cards
- Compare chart

## Preparation

- Prepare the comparison cards, each labeled with its place and features.
- Copy a compare chart for each learner.
- Cue short, respectful clips of Kathakali and Barong if available; otherwise prepare
  spoken and gestured descriptions.
- Recall Grade 5: learners explained how dance or drama reflects the place, history, or
  community that made it, citing what they observe.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **comparison with a
reason, never ranking**: we name observable features (mudras, navarasas, masks, chorus,
gamelan) and connect them to how each tradition expresses good vs evil — and to *why* the
difference exists (different occasions, beliefs, and the role of one performer vs the
whole community). Both **Kathakali** and the **Barong** dance are living traditions made
by living people; present them with reverence. A meaningful nuance to honor: in Kathakali,
the hero's dharma triumphs; in Barong, good and evil are held in *balance* — neither
wins outright — which reflects Balinese ideas of cosmic balance. This is a difference to
understand, not a contest to judge. If a learner belongs to one of these traditions, invite
them to be the authority and never correct them about their own culture. See
`docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md` on honoring living traditions.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you told a story with movement, voice, and one more
   element. Today you watch how two whole traditions tell the same great story — **good
   against evil** — and offer a reason for the difference.
2. **Meet the comparison (15 min).** Look at the first card, **Kathakali** of Kerala,
   India. What I observe: one dancer in elaborate makeup and a tall crown, telling an epic
   through **mudras** — codified hand gestures, each a word — and **navarasas** — nine
   facial expressions, from wonder to fury. How it tells good vs evil: the hero's dharma
   *triumphs* over the demon, one gesture and one expression at a time, over a long
   evening. Now **Barong**, of Bali. What I observe: a **masked** lion-like protector
   facing the witch **Rangda**, moving to **gamelan** and a chanting chorus of men, often
   in a village ritual. How it tells good vs evil: the two forces clash, but neither wins
   forever — the dance holds them in **balance**, because in Balinese thought good and
   evil both exist and must be kept in harmony. Same theme — good against evil — two very
   different tellings. The reason lies in *who performs* (one virtuoso vs a whole
   community), *what they wear* (makeup vs masks), and *what they believe the ending
   means* (triumph vs balance).
3. **Compare a second pairing (15 min).** Choose the alternative pairing — *honoring the
   departed*: the Yoruba **Egungun** masquerade (masked figures return as ancestors) vs
   **Día de los Muertos** processions (calavera figures honoring the dead). Meet both with
   reverence; these are sacred to living communities. Fill your chart: how each moves,
   how it expresses its theme, and your reason for the difference.
4. **Share a reason (10 min).** In a small group, share one comparison. Name what you
   *observed* and *why* the two differ. Notice that every tradition makes sense in its own
   place and belief.
5. **Close (5 min).** The great stories belong to everyone, and each culture tells them in
   its own body. To compare is to understand, never to rank. You will carry this respectful
   eye into the design work that closes this unit.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sentence frame — "In ___ the struggle is told with ___ (gestures,
  masks, chorus), so good and evil ___ (triumph / balance); in ___ it is told with ___,
   so ___." Copy one gesture or still shape from each.
- **Extension:** Research a third tradition that stages good vs evil (with a source),
  complete a chart row, and write one sentence on how its movement carries its meaning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name at least one observable feature
  of each tradition, connect it to how that tradition expresses the theme, and offer a
  reason for the difference — without ranking one tradition against another?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name something I actually observed, and did I
  offer a reason for the difference — speaking about living people and their traditions
  with respect?"

## Home connection

Find two versions of one familiar story or theme (a hero's victory, a farewell, a
welcome) told in two different ways — a film, a dance, a play, a game — and name one
difference and one reason for it.
