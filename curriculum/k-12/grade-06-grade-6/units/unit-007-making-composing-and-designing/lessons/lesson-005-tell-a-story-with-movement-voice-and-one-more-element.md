# Lesson 5 — Tell a Story with Movement, Voice, and One More Element

## Summary

Learners create a short performance that combines **movement, voice, and one other
element** — music, a prop, or shadow — to tell a story. A worked example performs a
three-part story ("a seed becomes a tree that gives shade") showing each part's movement,
voice, and chosen element, then small groups devise and perform their own short story,
using a storyboard to keep the **beginning, middle, and end** clear.

## Objectives

- Create a short performance that combines movement, voice, and one other element such as
  music or a prop to tell a story. _(D07.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

You already tell stories with more than words. You lean in and lower your voice to tell a
secret; you throw your arms wide to show how big the fish was; you tap a rhythm on the
table to set the mood. A performance just gathers these together on purpose: your
**movement** shows what happens, your **voice** says or sounds the feeling, and **one more
element** — a prop, a beat, a shadow — carries the story somewhere words and bodies alone
cannot. Today you put all three together to tell one small story from beginning to end.

## Materials

- Performance recipe card
- Prop and sound basket
- Storyboard page

## Preparation

- Copy the performance recipe card and storyboard page for each group.
- Gather the prop and sound basket.
- Recall Grade 5: learners choreographed a group dance or scene with a clear idea,
  working cooperatively; Grade 4: they created a character with voice, body, and a simple
  prop or costume.
- Arrange the room into small groups with space to move safely.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **integration with
clarity**: a performance works when movement, voice, and one more element all serve the
*same story* and the story has a clear beginning, middle, and end. Model the seed-to-tree
worked example fully first (explicit instruction for a novice skill — Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Keep the stories small and safe — a seed, a journey, a message, a rescue —
and invite only stories learners feel good sharing; performance can feel vulnerable at
this age (identity and self-consciousness spike in grades 6–8, see `docs/development.md`),
so make the audience kind, applause warm, and participation a choice. Celebrate every
completed story; clarity, not virtuosity, is the goal. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/contexts.md` on cooperative group work and emotional safety.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you heard how two traditions celebrate differently.
   Today you become the performers: you tell a short story using **movement, voice, and
   one more element**.
2. **Meet the recipe (10 min).** Look at the recipe card: **Story = Movement + Voice +
   One more element.** Here is a worked example, "a seed becomes a tree that gives
   shade." *Beginning:* you curl small on the ground and whisper "seed… seed…" — movement
   (small and curled) + voice (a quiet whisper). *Middle:* you rise slowly, arms reaching
   up, and make a rising "whoosh" while a partner taps a soft beat — movement + voice +
   **music** (the extra element). *End:* you stand tall, arms spread like branches, and say
   calmly, "shade for everyone." Three parts, one story, three tools working together.
3. **Choose your story (5 min).** With your group, pick a small story with a clear
   beginning, middle, and end — a journey, a message carried through a storm, a rescue, a
   gift, a change. Sketch or write it in your storyboard's three boxes.
4. **Devise it (15 min).** Decide, for each part: what does my **movement** show? what
   does my **voice** say or sound? what **one more element** — a prop, music, or shadow —
   carries it further? Rehearse once through, softly.
5. **Perform for an audience (15 min).** Take turns performing for another group, then
   switch. Watch for the story: can we follow the beginning, middle, and end? Applaud
   warmly after every performance.
6. **Close (5 min).** You just told a story the way humans always have — with your body,
   your voice, and one more thing that lifts it into the air. Keep your storyboard; next
   lesson you will meet how two whole traditions tell their stories through movement and
   drama.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-made story (the seed, or "carry a message across a stream")
  and let each learner hold one job — mover, voice, or extra element.
- **Support (movement, voice, and motor alternatives):** Movement does not have to be
  full-body — it can be a hand, an eyebrow, a turn of the head, or moving a prop, puppet,
  or object in place of your own body. Voice does not have to be spoken words — it can be
  a clap, a tap, a hum, a sign, a written or typed line, or a partner speaking your
  words. A learner may instead direct or keep the storyboard, shaping the story while
  others perform it. The story — not the able-bodiedness of its teller — is the learning.
- **Extension:** Add a second extra element (music *and* a prop) or a twist in the middle,
  and write one sentence on how the added element sharpens the story.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** Does the performance combine movement, voice, and one
  other element, and does its story have a followable beginning, middle, and end?
- **Self-check:** The group asks, "Did all three tools serve the same story, and could the
  audience follow our beginning, middle, and end?"

## Home connection

Tell a tiny story at home with just your body, your voice, and one object nearby — and ask
a listener what they understood the story to be.
