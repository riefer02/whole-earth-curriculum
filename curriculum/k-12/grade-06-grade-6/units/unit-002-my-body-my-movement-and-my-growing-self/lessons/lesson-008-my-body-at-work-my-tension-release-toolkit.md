# Lesson 8 — My Body at Work: My Tension-Release Toolkit

## Summary

Learners close the unit by building a one-page **"my body toolkit"** — a **portfolio
artifact** that gathers everything they learned: where stress shows up as tension, one named
way to release it, the movement skill they built through practice and feedback, and a teamwork
reflection. This is **retrieval and synthesis**: the unit's learning, carried forward for life.

## Objectives

- Explain how emotions and body sensations connect, such as stress and tension, and name one
  way to release tension. _(D03.S1.06.02)_
- Learn a new movement skill, such as a dance step or sport skill, through practice, feedback,
  and correction. _(D03.S2.06.01)_
- Work cooperatively with teammates, sharing roles and encouraging others regardless of skill
  level. _(D03.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

A craftsperson carries a small kit — the few tools that do the most work. This unit has been
building *your* kit, and today you lay it out so you can carry it with you. It has four tools:
a **map** of where stress shows up in your body, a **release** for when it does, a **skill** you
know how to build through practice and feedback, and a **team** habit of encouraging every
person. You do not need any of these tools to be perfect; you only need to know they are yours,
and where to reach for them.

## Materials

- My body toolkit page
- Practice log and earlier pages
- Pencils and colors

## Preparation

- Copy a "my body toolkit" page for each learner (four labeled sections).
- Have learners gather their own running log and body outline from earlier lessons.
- Arrange a quiet space for individual writing and a circle for sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **the unit's learning
is now the learner's own toolkit**. This is a **summative portfolio artifact** and an act of
**retrieval** — the learner recalls each piece rather than re-reading it. Keep it **dignified
and joyful**: no two toolkits will match, and that is the point — every body works in its own
way, and every one is honored. If a learner struggles to recall a part, point them to their own
earlier pages (their log, their body outline) rather than telling them the answer. The
reflection should include *what helped them improve and who encouraged them*, never a ranking.
See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have learned a lot: how stress shows up as tension, one way to
   release it, how to build a movement skill, and how to lift up a team. Today you gather it
   all into a **toolkit** you can carry with you.
2. **Meet the toolkit (5 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of the four
   sections: **① my tension map** (a body with one shaded spot); **② my release technique**
   (an open hand — "squeeze, then let go"); **③ my skill log** (tallies and footprints);
   **④ my teamwork reflection** (four figures in a circle). Your toolkit will be your own.
3. **Build your toolkit (30 min).** Fill each section, *recalling* before you check your notes:
   - **My tension map:** where does stress tighten your body? Shade it and name the feeling.
   - **My release technique:** write or draw the four steps — breathe in, squeeze, hold, breathe
     out and let go — and name it in your own words.
   - **My skill log:** write the four counts of your traveling step and one correction a
     partner gave you that helped.
   - **My teamwork reflection:** one sentence about a role you shared and one encouraging word
     you gave or received.
4. **Share one tool (10 min).** In a small circle, each person shares **one** section and
   listens to the others. Notice how different — and how complete — the toolkits are.
5. **Close (5 min).** This kit is yours now. Wherever you go, you can notice your body, release
   its tension, build a skill, and help a team belong. That is a body at work — and a self in
   good care.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a partially filled toolkit with pictures and a word bank for each section;
   scribe as needed; allow one picture per section instead of sentences.
- **Extension:** Connect the four parts in one paragraph about how noticing, releasing, moving,
   and belonging work together — and name one new situation where the toolkit will help.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** Does the toolkit gather all four pieces — a tension map, a named
  release technique, the skill with a correction, and a teamwork reflection — in the learner's
  own words or drawings?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their toolkit back and asks, "If stress arrives tonight,
  would I know where to reach — and could I teach one of these to a friend?"

## Home connection

Show your toolkit to someone at home and teach them one tool from it — the release, the step,
or the encouraging word.

## Resources

- Retrieval practice — recalling what was learned rather than re-reading it — strengthens
  long-term memory (see the learning-science literature and Roediger & Karpicke's work on
  testing effects). Gathering the unit into one portfolio artifact supports synthesis and
  metacognition, and honors the developmental turn toward identity at ages 11–14 (see
  `docs/development.md`). For SEL framing of self-awareness and self-management, see CASEL:
  <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
