# Lesson 4 — Feedback and Correction Refine My Skill

## Summary

Learners discover that **feedback and correction** — not just repetition — refine a new skill.
They meet a **feedback-correction loop**, learn a two-part starter ("I noticed ___. Try
___."), and practice the **traveling step** while a partner gives one specific, kind tip about
the *action*; then they correct and try again. This is the heart of the standard: *practice,
feedback, and correction*.

## Objectives

- Learn a new movement skill, such as a dance step or sport skill, through practice, feedback,
  and correction. _(D03.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

A mirror shows you what you cannot see from inside — and a good teammate is a mirror for your
movement. When you are stepping, you cannot watch your own feet; a partner who says "**I
noticed your cross came in front — try crossing behind**" gives you information you could not
get alone. Feedback names what is real, kindly, so you can **correct** it. Whether in a dance
circle, a workshop, or a field, the people who help us grow are the ones who tell us the truth
about one small thing at a time — and believe we can improve.

## Materials

- Feedback-correction loop poster
- Feedback sentence-starter card
- Practice log page (continued)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the feedback-correction loop poster (practice → observe → specific tip →
  correct → practice again).
- Copy a feedback sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Continue the running practice log with a new "correction tried" column.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **feedback about the
action, not the person** — specific, kind, and usable — and that **correction is how skill
grows**. Model the difference vividly and stop any feedback about the person the moment it
appears, redirecting to the action. Feedback is among the strongest influences on learning
(Hattie, *Visible Learning*), but only when it is clear and kind. Honor every body's pace:
feedback is about a *next step*, never about ranking. Offer seated/adapted versions of the step
for learners who need them. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you started the traveling step. Today you add the part that
   turns practice into *skill*: **feedback and correction**.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of a cycle:
   **practice** the step → a partner **observes** → they give a **specific tip** → you
   **correct** and **try again**. Notice: correction is not failure; it is the point. A step
   corrected once is worth ten uncorrected repeats.
3. **Learn the starter (5 min).** Read the two-part starter: "**I noticed** ___ . **Try**
   ___ ." Practice it once aloud about the step, using kind words. It names *one thing* and
   one *next try*.
4. **Give real feedback (15 min).** Partner up. One partner steps the four counts; the other
   **observes quietly**, then gives one specific, kind tip using the starter — about the
   *step*, never the person. The stepper **corrects** and tries again. Then swap. Log each
   correction you tried.
5. **Close (5 min).** A skill grows fastest when practice meets a kind, specific word. You can
   be that help for someone — and ask for it for yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the starter printed large with blanks and model one full example before
   partners begin; allow a tip about one single, visible thing.
- **Extension:** Distinguish feedback about the *action* from feedback about the *person*, and
   rewrite two hurtful comments into helpful corrections.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/performance):** Does the learner give feedback that is specific, kind, and
   about the action, and can their partner correct and try again with the tip in mind?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Was my tip about the action, not the person, and was it
   kind enough that I would welcome hearing it myself?"

## Home connection

Give one specific, kind piece of feedback to someone at home today — and notice whether it
helps them more than a general "good job."

## Resources

- Feedback is among the strongest influences on learning (Hattie, *Visible Learning*); it
  works best when it is specific, timely, and about the work, not the person. Corrective
  feedback — a cue plus a next try — is a well-established part of motor-skill learning. For
  classroom SEL framing of giving and receiving feedback, see CASEL's relationship-skills
  competency: <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
