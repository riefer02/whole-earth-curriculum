# Lesson 2 — One Way to Release Tension

## Summary

Learners name and practice one concrete way to release tension: **squeeze-and-release**
(a form of progressive muscle relaxation) paired with **slow breathing**. They meet a
**worked example**, follow a four-step card, and run the technique on their own hands and
shoulders, noticing which spot lets go. This is a *named, evidence-grounded* release skill —
one they can use anywhere, for the rest of their lives.

## Objectives

- Explain how emotions and body sensations connect, such as stress and tension, and name one
  way to release tension. _(D03.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever noticed that after a hard, stressful day your shoulders stay up near your ears
even when the work is done? The body can forget to let go. There is a simple way to teach it
again: **tighten on purpose, then release**. A potter squeezes clay and then opens her hands;
a breath held and then let out is the same shape. **Squeeze-and-release** works the same way —
by tightening a muscle fully, then letting it go, you remind your body what *soft* feels like,
wherever you are.

## Materials

- Squeeze-and-release poster
- Tension-release card
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the squeeze-and-release poster (a fist labeled "squeeze" and an open hand
  labeled "release," with a slow-breath arrow between).
- Copy a tension-release card for each learner.
- Arrange a circle with room to sit or stand comfortably.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **one named, usable way to
release tension: squeeze-and-release with slow breathing**. Teach it as a *skill* with explicit
steps and a worked example (this is a novice technique, not a discovery). Keep it gentle and
**never forceful** — a learner should squeeze to comfortable firmness, never to pain, and any
muscle group may be skipped. Slow breathing engages the body's calming (parasympathetic)
response; present it as a tool, not a cure, and as one of *many* ways different people and
cultures release tension — not the only right one. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned that stress shows up as tension — tight
   shoulders, a clenched jaw, a knot in the stomach. Today you learn to **release** it, with
   one simple technique you can use anywhere.
2. **Meet the technique (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example**. Two states of
   the same hand. First: a **tight fist**, shoulders up — *squeeze*. Second: an **open, soft
   hand**, shoulders down — *release*. Between them, a slow **breath out**. This is
   **squeeze-and-release**: breathe in, squeeze tight and hold for a few counts, then breathe
   out slowly and let the muscle go completely soft. Tightening first makes the letting-go
   easier to feel.
3. **Learn the four steps (5 min).** Read your card: **① breathe in; ② squeeze tight; ③ hold
   for three slow counts; ④ breathe out and let go.** Try it once with your hands: make a
   gentle fist, squeeze, hold, then open your fingers wide and soft.
4. **Practice together (15 min).** Run the sequence slowly through three spots: your **hands**,
   then your **shoulders** (lift them toward your ears, squeeze, then drop them), then your
   **jaw** (press your teeth gently together, then let your jaw hang loose). After each
   release, notice: where did you feel the most "letting go"? Draw or circle it on your card.
5. **Close (5 min).** Tension is not something to fight; it is something to *notice and
   release*. You now hold one named technique for it — squeeze, then let go, breathing slowly.
   It works because you already practiced it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only one muscle group (hands) and count the breaths aloud together;
   scribe the drawing.
- **Extension:** Compare squeeze-and-release with slow breathing alone, and write one sentence
   about when each might help more (before a test, at night, after a game).

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance):** Can the learner follow the four steps — breathe in, squeeze,
   hold, breathe out and let go — and name the technique in their own words?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I actually feel a spot let go? Which one, and could I
   do this again without help?"

## Home connection

Teach someone at home the four steps — squeeze, hold, breathe out, let go — and try it
together once before bed.

## Resources

- Squeeze-and-release is a form of progressive muscle relaxation, developed by Edmund Jacobson
  and still widely used to lower physical tension. Slow, extended exhaling engages the body's
  calming (parasympathetic) response — a well-documented mind–body link. Release techniques
  exist in many traditions (breath work, stretching, rest), and no single one is required;
  this lesson teaches one evidence-grounded, portable option. For classroom SEL framing, see
  CASEL's self-management competency: <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
