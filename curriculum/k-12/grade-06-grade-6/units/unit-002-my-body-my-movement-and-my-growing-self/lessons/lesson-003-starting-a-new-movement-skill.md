# Lesson 3 — Starting a New Movement Skill

## Summary

Learners begin a **new movement skill**: the **traveling step** — a four-count side step
(*side, cross behind, side, tap*) that appears, by many names, in dances and games across many
regions. They meet a **worked example** of the four counts, practice slowly in short repeated
rounds, and start a **practice log** they will carry through Lessons 4 and 7. Because this is
a *new skill*, the lesson uses **explicit steps and guided practice** before independent work
(see `docs/philosophy.md` §13).

## Objectives

- Learn a new movement skill, such as a dance step or sport skill, through practice, feedback,
  and correction. _(D03.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of the first time you learned a clapping game, a song with hand movements, or a footwork
pattern — at first it felt impossible, and then, try after try, your body just *knew* it.
Everywhere in the world, people teach movement the same way: one small step at a time, repeated
until it becomes smooth. The **traveling step** you will learn today is one such step — a cousin
of the grapevine in some line dances, the *chassé* in ballet, and side-stepping steps in folk
dances from many lands. Wherever people gather to dance or play, they build skill one counted
step at a time.

## Materials

- Traveling-step poster
- Practice log page
- Open floor space or a clear line

## Preparation

- Print or draw the traveling-step poster (four footprints labeled counts 1–4: side, cross
  behind, side, tap).
- Copy a practice log page for each learner (this log runs through Lesson 7).
- Clear space for pairs to step side to side; set a line or provide a chair back for adapted
  versions.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a new skill begins
with small, counted steps and grows through repetition**. Teach it with **explicit steps and a
worked example** (novice skill, not discovery). Frame the step as one of *many* movement
traditions — do not present it as the "correct" dance of any one people, but as a shared human
pattern with many names. Keep it **non-competitive**: the learner compares their own first
round to their own last round. Offer **seated/adapted versions** — tap the four counts with the
feet while seated, or trace the pattern with a hand on a table or knee — so every body can
practice; a seated step is a full step. Honor every body's pace. See `docs/contexts.md` and
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You know how stress shows up in your body and one way to release it.
   Now the joy of **movement**: today you begin a brand-new movement skill, the **traveling
   step**.
2. **Meet the skill (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of four counts:
   **① step to the side** with your lead foot; **② cross** the other foot **behind** it;
   **③ step to the side** again; **④ tap** the feet together. Then reverse, leading with the
   other foot. Watch a slow demonstration, or trace the footprints with your finger: *side,
   cross behind, side, tap*.
3. **Guided practice (10 min).** In pairs, step the four counts slowly, saying each count
   aloud, ten times. A partner watches and counts with you. Each round, **tally** it on your
   practice log.
4. **Repeat and steady (15 min).** Do three more rounds of ten. After each round, notice: do
   your feet know the order without your voice? Does your last round feel smoother than your
   first? Log it. Compare **your own** early tries to your own later tries — not to your
   partner's.
5. **Close (5 min).** A skill is not a gift you either have or do not have — it is something
   you **build**, one counted step at a time. Your log is the proof, and it will grow with you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Break the step into two parts — side-tap first, then add the cross — and use a
   chair back or a partner's hand for balance; offer a seated foot-tap version; scribe the log.
- **Extension:** Add a quarter turn on the tap, or reverse direction mid-step, and note in the
   log what each change did to the step's steadiness.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance):** Can the learner follow the four counts of the step, tally their
   rounds, and describe how their later tries improved over their earlier ones?
- **Self-check:** The learner compares their first and last logged round and says in one
   sentence what changed — steadiness, smoothness, or confidence.

## Home connection

Teach the four counts — side, cross behind, side, tap — to someone at home and practice it
together for one minute.

## Resources

- Repeated, structured practice builds a motor skill, reflecting well-established research on
  motor learning and deliberate practice; for novice skills, explicit instruction with worked
  examples and guided practice beats discovery alone (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The
  side-traveling step appears across many folk and social dance traditions; the curriculum
  presents it as a shared human pattern rather than the property of one culture. For children's
  movement guidance, see the World Health Organization:
