# Lesson 5 — Sharing Roles on a Team

## Summary

Learners discover what makes a team work: **every person holds a role, and roles are shared
and rotated** so no one is stuck doing only one thing. They meet four roles — **stepper,
counter, encourager, director** — and practice the traveling step in small teams while rotating
roles each round. The aim is **cooperative, not competitive**: every contribution matters, and
no role outranks another.

## Objectives

- Work cooperatively with teammates, sharing roles and encouraging others regardless of skill
  level. _(D03.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of preparing a meal together, building a shelter, or running a market stall: one person
minds the fire, another chops, another counts the money, another welcomes people. If one person
tried to do it all, the work would stall; if the same person always got the easiest job, the
team would sour. A team works when **everyone holds a role and the roles are shared fairly**.
Whether it is a fishing crew, a dance troupe, or a study group, the same truth holds everywhere:
each person contributes, and the roles turn so everyone learns everything.

## Materials

- Sharing-roles poster
- Role cards
- Open floor space

## Preparation

- Print or draw the sharing-roles poster (four roles around a circle, with rotating arrows).
- Copy role cards — stepper, counter, encourager, director — one set per team.
- Clear space for teams of four to practice together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **teamwork = shared,
rotated roles**, and that **no role outranks another**. The roles are deliberately designed so
that skill is not what qualifies a learner: the *encourager* and *director* are as essential as
the *stepper*. Rotate roles so every learner tries each one — this is the egalitarian core, and
it prevents one learner from being fixed as "the skilled one." Offer seated/adapted versions of
every role (a seated stepper taps the counts; the director can direct with a hand; the counter
counts for any pace). See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have practiced a new skill and learned to give feedback. Today you
   learn what makes a **team** work: **shared roles**.
2. **Meet the roles (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of a team of four:
   the **stepper** shows the step; the **counter** counts the four beats aloud; the
   **encourager** cheers and names what is going well; the **director** watches and calls the
   next move. Notice the arrows: the roles **rotate**, so each person tries each one. No role
   is the "best" — the team needs all four.
3. **Read the cards (5 min).** Take a role card and read its job. Say it to your team so
   everyone knows what each role does.
4. **Practice with rotating roles (20 min).** In teams of four, practice the traveling step
   together while each person holds their role. After each round, **rotate** the cards to the
   left and try the new role. Go around until everyone has tried all four. Notice: does the
   team feel different when *you* are the encourager, or the counter?
5. **Close (5 min).** A team is not one star and three helpers; it is **four contributors**,
   each role turning so everyone grows. That is how cooperation beats competition.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only two roles at first (stepper and encourager), then add the others;
   provide a picture cue on each role card; allow a seated version of every role.
- **Extension:** Propose a fair rotation rule for a larger team and explain, in one sentence,
   why rotating roles is fairer than always letting the strongest person lead.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Does the learner hold a role, do its job, and rotate to a
   new role when it is their turn — treating every role as important?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I do my role fully, and did I treat the next person's
   role as equal to my own?"

## Home connection

At home, try rotating roles for one shared job (setting a table, tidying up) so each person
does a different part today than yesterday.

## Resources

- Cooperative learning that assigns roles and holds each member accountable is a well-evidenced
  classroom structure (see the cooperative-learning literature, e.g., Slavin; Johnson &
  Johnson). Rotating roles so every member tries each one supports equity — no learner is fixed
  by skill or background. For classroom SEL framing of teamwork, see CASEL's relationship-skills
  competency: <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
