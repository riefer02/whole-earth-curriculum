# Lesson 1 — Stress, Tension, and My Body

## Summary

Learners return to the **mind–body connection** with new precision: emotions such as
**stress** show up in the body as **tension** — tight shoulders, a clenched jaw, a knotted
stomach. They meet a **worked example** of stress flowing into tension, scan their own
bodies with curiosity, and shade where tension lives for them on a **body outline**. The
skill is *noticing* the signal, never judging it — the first step toward releasing it later
in this unit.

## Objectives

- Explain how emotions and body sensations connect, such as stress and tension, and name one
  way to release tension. _(D03.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of a moment this week when a lot was on your mind — a test, a promise to keep, a
journey to a new place. Before you even said a word, maybe your **shoulders were up by your
ears**, or your **jaw was clenched**, or your **stomach felt tight**. That is your body
answering your mind: the feeling we call **stress** can show up as the sensation we call
**tension**. Whether you live in a busy city, a fishing village, or a mountain farm, and
whether the stress is small or large, your body keeps its own record — in the places that
tighten.

## Materials

- Stress-to-tension poster
- Blank body outline page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the stress-to-tension poster (a thought bubble flowing into a body outline
  with shaded, labeled shoulders, jaw, and stomach).
- Copy a blank body outline page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and a quiet space for individual work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **emotions and
body sensations connect — stress shows up as tension** — and that noticing this is a skill,
not a problem to be ashamed of. Keep examples **ordinary and safe**; a learner may carry
heavy or ongoing stress (family strain, illness, displacement), so invite only small, safe
moments and never require anyone to reveal more than they choose. Present tension as
*information*, never as weakness, and honor every body — tension can live in different spots
for different people. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned that a worry can make a stomach feel tight — the
   mind–body connection. This year you sharpen that idea: **stress** in the mind often shows
   up as **tension** in the body. Today you learn to *notice* where.
2. **Meet the connection (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example**. A thought —
   "so much to do, and a test tomorrow" — flows down into a body, and three spots tighten:
   **shoulders up by the ears, a clenched jaw, a tight stomach**. Those tight sensations are
   tension — the body's answer to stress. Stress is the *feeling*; tension is the *body
   sensation* it produces. They always travel together.
3. **Scan your body (10 min).** Think of one **small, safe** stressful moment from your week.
   Soften your gaze. Where does your body hold it? Tight shoulders? A furrowed brow? Clenched
   hands? A churning stomach? A stiff neck? Name the spot without judging it — this is
   *information* about you, not a flaw in you.
4. **Shade your own (15 min).** On your body outline page, **shade the spot** where tension
   lives for you, and write the feeling's name beside it. Check with a partner: does your
   friend's tension live in the same place or a different one? Every body speaks in its own
   way — there is no wrong map.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your body is not separate from your feelings; it is where they live.
   Noticing where stress tightens you is the first, kind step — soon you will learn to
   *release* it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a word bank of feelings and a pre-labeled body outline to shade instead
  of write; scribe the feeling name.
- **Extension:** Trace two different emotions (stress *and* excitement) and compare where each
  one shows up, then name which signal helps them know what they feel sooner.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name an emotion, point to a body spot where it
  shows up as tension, and explain the stress-to-tension connection in their own words?
- **Self-check:** The learner points to their shaded spot and asks, "Is this truly where and
  how tension shows up for me?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Where in your body do you feel stress or tension?" and share one body
signal you noticed in yourself today.

## Resources

- The connection between emotion and body sensation is central to embodied emotion research
  and to cognitive-behavioral approaches (see the work of Aaron Beck on the thought–feeling
  loop). Naming where tension lives is a self-awareness skill as framed by CASEL:
  <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>. The stress-to-tension link reflects the body's
  stress response, which is the same across cultures even while the situations that trigger
  it differ.
