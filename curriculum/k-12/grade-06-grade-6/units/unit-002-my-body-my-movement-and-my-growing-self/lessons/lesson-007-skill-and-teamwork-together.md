# Lesson 7 — Skill and Teamwork Together

## Summary

Learners put the unit together: they build a shared **eight-count sequence** — the traveling
step plus one move from each teammate — and perform it as a **cooperative game** whose goal is
collective, not competitive. Every person contributes, roles rotate, and everyone is
encouraged. This lesson carries both movement standards at once: the *refined skill* and *true
teamwork*.

## Objectives

- Learn a new movement skill, such as a dance step or sport skill, through practice, feedback,
  and correction. _(D03.S2.06.01)_
- Work cooperatively with teammates, sharing roles and encouraging others regardless of skill
  level. _(D03.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

A single voice singing a line is lovely, but many voices weaving one song is something more —
each adds its part, and no part outranks another. That is what a **cooperative game** is: not
one team beating another, but a group building something together that no one could make
alone. Today you do exactly that with movement — your traveling step becomes four counts of a
shared sequence, and each teammate adds one move of their own. The win is the *together*, not
the score.

## Materials

- Team step-sequence poster
- Sequence planner page
- Practice log page (final)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the team step-sequence poster (four figures, eight labeled counts).
- Copy a sequence planner page for each team.
- Clear space for teams to rehearse and perform; prepare a steady beat if available.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **skill and teamwork
come together in a cooperative goal**. Keep the game **collective, never competitive** — there
is no winner and no loser, only a shared performance to celebrate. Ensure every learner
contributes a move, and that the move may be *any* movement — seated, small, slow, or large —
so every body is included. Rotate the counting/directing roles so no one is fixed. The
performance is for encouragement, not judgment; another team responds with the encouraging
words from Lesson 6. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have a skill — the traveling step — and you know how to share roles
   and encourage every teammate. Today you put it all into one **cooperative game**.
2. **Meet the sequence (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of a team's
   eight-count sequence: counts **1–4** are the traveling step (*side, cross behind, side,
   tap*), then counts **5–8** are one move added by each of four teammates — a clap, a turn, a
   reach, a hop (or a seated tap, a nod, a wave). Every teammate's move is counted in. No
   move outranks another.
3. **Build your sequence (10 min).** On your planner page, write your eight counts. Decide
   together: who adds which move, and who counts or directs. Make sure **every person has a
   move and a role**. Practice slowly until the whole team can step and move as one.
4. **Perform and encourage (20 min).** Perform your sequence for another team, then watch
   theirs. Respond with the encouraging words you practiced — cheer their **effort and
   progress**, never a rank. Log your team's goal and one thing that went well together.
5. **Close (5 min).** A cooperative game is won the moment everyone contributes and everyone
   is cheered. Today you made something together that no one could make alone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only the four-count step plus one team move at first, then add more; allow
   seated moves for any count; scribe the planner page.
- **Extension:** Design a second, mirror-image sequence or lead the team's count, and reflect
   in one sentence on how sharing roles shaped the final performance.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer/portfolio):** Does the sequence combine the refined step with
   one contribution from every teammate, performed together, with roles shared and everyone
   encouraged?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did every teammate contribute, did I hold and rotate my
   role, and did I encourage rather than rank?"

## Home connection

Perform your eight-count sequence for someone at home, teaching them one count so they can
join you for a few steps.

## Resources

- Cooperative (rather than competitive) goal structures support belonging and mutual aid —
  see the cooperative-learning literature (e.g., Slavin; Johnson & Johnson). A shared, built
  sequence also illustrates transfer and retrieval: performing the step in a new context
  strengthens the skill. For SEL framing of teamwork and cooperation, see CASEL's
  relationship-skills competency: <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
