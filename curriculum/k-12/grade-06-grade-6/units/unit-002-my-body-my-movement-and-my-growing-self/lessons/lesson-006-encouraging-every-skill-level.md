# Lesson 6 — Encouraging Every Skill Level

## Summary

Learners learn to **encourage teammates regardless of skill level** — cheering **effort and
progress**, never ranking anyone. They meet a contrast of encouragement and ranking, practice
sentence starters that honor effort, and encourage one another as teammates practice the
traveling step at their own pace. This is the egalitarian heart of the standard: **every body
belongs**.

## Objectives

- Work cooperatively with teammates, sharing roles and encouraging others regardless of skill
  level. _(D03.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

Every team has someone still learning — and that someone is *you*, some of the time. Which
helps more when a teammate is struggling with the cross-step: "**you're the slowest**" or "**I
saw your cross today — you're getting it!**"? The first ranks people like runners in a race; the
second sees *progress* and keeps the door open. Encouragement is not pretending everyone is the
same; it is noticing each person's next step and cheering it. Whether in a game, a kitchen, or
a classroom, the teams where everyone *belongs* are the ones where no one is ranked and
everyone is cheered on.

## Materials

- Encourage-every-skill poster
- Encouragement sentence-starter card
- Practice log page (continued)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the encourage-every-skill poster (encouraging vs. ranking comment, the ranking
  one crossed out).
- Copy an encouragement sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Continue the running practice log with a line for one encouragement given and one received.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **encourage effort and
progress, never rank**. Ranking by skill — even "friendly" — teaches some learners they do not
belong; this unit's standard explicitly says "regardless of skill level." Model the difference
vividly and stop any ranking comment the moment it appears, redirecting to effort ("what did
you see them get better at?"). Honor every body and pace: a seated step, a slow step, and a
fast step are all full steps. See `docs/contexts.md` and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you learned to share roles. Today you learn the deeper part
   of teamwork: **encouraging every teammate, whatever their skill level**.
2. **Meet encouragement (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two comments about the same learner.
   *Ranking:* "You're the slowest." Crossed out — it sorts people, and it helps nothing.
   *Encouraging:* "I saw your cross today — you're getting it!" It names **effort and
   progress**, not rank, and it keeps the learner on the team.
3. **Learn the starters (5 min).** Read the starters: "**I saw you** ___ . That's **better than
   before**." and "**Keep going — you're getting it.**" They cheer the *trying*, not the
   *winning*. Practice one aloud with a partner.
4. **Encourage for real (15 min).** In pairs or small teams, take turns practicing the step at
   **your own pace**. While one person tries, the others give one true, encouraging word about
   effort or progress. Log one encouragement you gave and one you received.
5. **Close (5 min).** A team is not a ranking; it is a place where everyone is **cheered on
   from where they are**. You can make a team feel like that — and you deserve it too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the starters printed large with blanks and model one full example before
   partners begin; allow a single kind word instead of a full sentence.
- **Extension:** Distinguish praising *effort* from praising *rank*, and rewrite two ranking
   comments into encouragements that honor progress.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/self):** Does the learner give encouragement that names effort or progress
   rather than ranking anyone — and can their teammate feel welcome to keep trying?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did my words cheer their effort, and did I leave out every
   hint of rank?"

## Home connection

Give one true, encouraging word about effort to someone at home today — and notice whether it
changes the room.

## Resources

- Encouraging effort and progress (a "growth" stance) rather than fixed rank supports belonging
  and persistence; praise of effort over ability is well evidenced in motivation research (see
  the work of Carol Dweck on mindsets, and Hattie's synthesis on feedback and classroom climate).
  Ranking by skill is deliberately excluded from this standard — every skill level belongs. For
  SEL framing, see CASEL's relationship-skills competency: <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>
