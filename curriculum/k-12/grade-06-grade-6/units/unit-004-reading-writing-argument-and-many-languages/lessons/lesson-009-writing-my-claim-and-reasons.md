# Lesson 9 — Writing My Claim and Reasons

## Summary

Learners begin their own argument by writing a **claim** and the **reasons** that support it.
They meet a full worked example — one claim joined to three reasons by "**because**" — learn
the "**does it support the claim?**" test, and draft their own claim and reasons on a real
topic. This is step one of three toward a complete argument (evidence and counterargument
follow).

## Objectives

- Write a claim and support it with reasons. _(D04.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

You want something to change — a garden, a rest spot, a water tap, a reading corner. You could
just say "I want it," but that convinces no one. The moment you say **why** — "because there is
nowhere cool to sit, because children play in the hot sun" — you have begun an **argument**: a
**claim** (what you believe should happen) plus **reasons** (why). This is not about winning or
talking the loudest. It is about giving another person honest reasons they can weigh — the
same move you made when you disagreed respectfully, now written down, in any language.

## Materials

- Claim-and-reasons poster
- Claim-and-reasons frame
- Topic chooser cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the claim-and-reasons poster (claim on top, reasons below, joined by
  "because").
- Copy the frame for each learner and the topic cards for each group.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **an argument is a
claim plus reasons** — and a reason is only a reason if it *supports* the claim, not just
restates it or wanders. Use the worked example fully and slowly; this is a foundational
writing skill, so model it step-by-step before independent drafting (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Retrieval: connect back to Lesson 1's claim/detail/example tree — writing an
argument is making your own tree. Hold the ethical frame firmly: argument is *reasoned claim*,
never winning or rhetoric (`docs/philosophy.md` §4, §7). Honor every home language: draft in
the strongest language. The modeled example — planting shade and fruit trees — is adaptable;
invite learners to argue for what *their* community actually needs.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one change you genuinely want in your school or neighborhood.
   Today you start an **argument** for it — not by shouting, but by writing a **claim** and
   **reasons**.
2. **Meet the worked example (10 min).** Look at the poster. The **claim**: "our neighborhood
   should plant more shade and fruit trees." The **reasons**, each joined by "**because**":
   "because trees give cool shade in hot weather," "because fruit trees give free, healthy
   food," "because trees give birds and bees a home." Notice: each reason *supports* the claim.
   A reason that doesn't support it — "because I like green" — is not a reason for *this*
   claim.
3. **Learn the test (5 min).** For each reason, ask: "**Does it support the claim?**" If you
   can say "this is *why* I believe the claim," it's a reason. If not, cut it or fix it.
4. **Draft your claim and reasons (20 min).** Choose a topic from the cards or your own. Write
   your **claim** in one clear sentence. Then write **three reasons**, each starting with
   "because." Run each through the test with a partner: does every reason support your claim?
5. **Close (5 min).** You now have the spine of an argument: a claim you believe, and reasons
   that say why. Next lesson you will strengthen it with **evidence** — and answer the person
   who disagrees.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the claim already written and two reasons to choose between; ask the
  learner to add one reason of their own and run the test.
- **Extension:** Write four reasons, then order them strongest to weakest and explain why you
  would lead with the strongest.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/portfolio):** Is the claim a clear, arguable position, and does each
  reason actually support it (not restate it)?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "For each 'because' sentence, could a reader say 'yes,
  that's why you believe this'?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one change you want, and give them two honest reasons — each starting
with "because" — and see how they respond.

## Resources

- Argument writing as claim + reasons (Toulmin's claim/data/warrant) is standard; for classroom
  guidance see Graham & Perin, "Writing Next" (Carnegie Corporation, 2007) and the IES
  "Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively" guide. On explicit instruction and worked
  examples for novice writers, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006). The "reasoned claim, not
  winning" stance is a values commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4, §7).
