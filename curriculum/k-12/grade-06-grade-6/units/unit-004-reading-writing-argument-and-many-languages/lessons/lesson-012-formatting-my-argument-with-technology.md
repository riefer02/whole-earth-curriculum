# Lesson 12 — Formatting My Argument with Technology

## Summary

Learners **use technology to draft, revise, and publish** writing with **basic formatting**.
They learn what formatting is *for* — headings, paragraphs, and intentional bold or italic
help a reader — apply a formatting checklist to their argument, and save their work with a
clear name. A low-tech "paper as a screen" path keeps the same decisions available without a
device.

## Objectives

- Use technology to draft, revise, and publish writing, including basic formatting of digital
  text. _(D04.S3.06.02)_

## Connection

You have read pages that were one solid wall of words, and pages where a **title** stood at the
top, **paragraphs** were separated, and a few important words were **bold** or *italic*. The
second was easier to read — not because the words were better, but because the page was
**formatted** to help your eye find its way. Formatting is not decoration; it is a small act
of care for your reader. A **heading** says "this is what it's about"; a **paragraph break**
says "one idea ends here"; a **bold** word says "notice this." Using a device to write is just
this care, done on a screen — and done so your work can be saved, shared, and read far beyond
one room.

## Materials

- Formatting poster
- Formatting checklist
- A device with a writing tool (or paper modeled as a screen)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the formatting poster (a labeled document: title, paragraphs, bold, italic,
  save).
- Copy the formatting checklist for each learner.
- Prepare devices with a text editor if available; otherwise prepare paper-as-screen sheets.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **formatting is
reader-care, not decoration** — headings, paragraphs, and intentional bold/italic make text
easier to enter and navigate. Teach each move with a demonstration (a novice skill; model then
guide — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). This is the technology lens in action, held
ethically and egalitarianly: a device is a *tool*, not a mark of worth; no learner is behind
for lacking one, and the "paper as a screen" path teaches the exact same decisions
(`docs/philosophy.md` §4). Also name access honestly: not everyone has a device or power or
connectivity, and a tool only serves if it is accessible — a font someone can read, a size
that works for everyone. Retrieval: connect back to the published argument from Lesson 11 —
now you are giving it a readable digital shape. Honor every language and script; formatting
supports any of them.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a page that was easy to read and one that was a wall of words.
   Today you learn the small moves that make the difference: **formatting** with technology.
2. **Meet the formatted page (10 min).** Look at the poster. A document has a **title** set
   large and bold — a **heading** that says what it's about. It has **paragraphs** separated by
   blank lines — one idea each. One word is **bold** ("notice this") and one is *italic* (a key
   term). At the bottom, a **Save** button — the habit that keeps your work from vanishing.
3. **Learn the checklist (5 min).** Four items: (1) title as a **heading**; (2) **paragraphs**
   separated; (3) **one bold or italic** used *on purpose*; (4) **saved** with a clear name.
   Formatting with a reason beats formatting everywhere — one bold word says more than ten.
4. **Format your argument (20 min).** Open your writing tool (or your paper-as-screen). Type or
   write your published argument. Then run the checklist: set the title as a heading, separate
   the paragraphs, bold or italicize one word on purpose, and **save** it with a name you'll
   recognize. Re-open it once to check it saved.
5. **Close (5 min).** Technology can carry your words far — but only if you use it with care.
   A heading, a paragraph break, a saved file: these are small acts of respect for the person
   who will read what you wrote.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the argument already typed; ask the learner to do two moves — make the
  title a heading and separate the paragraphs — then save it.
- **Extension:** Add a second formatting choice (a bullet list for the reasons, italics for a
  key term) and write one sentence explaining why each choice helps the reader.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/self):** Does the piece show a heading, separated paragraphs, one
  intentional bold or italic, and a saved file (or a paper-as-screen with the same four
  choices)?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If someone opened my document, could they find the title,
  follow the paragraphs, and see the one thing I wanted them to notice?"

## Home connection

Format one real piece of writing — a note, a list, a message — on any device or on paper,
giving it a title and paragraphs, and notice whether it is easier for someone else to read.
