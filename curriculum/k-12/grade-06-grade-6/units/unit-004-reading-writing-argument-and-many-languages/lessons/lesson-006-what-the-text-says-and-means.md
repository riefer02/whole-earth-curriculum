# Lesson 6 — What the Text Says and Means

## Summary

Learners learn to separate **what a text says** (the literal words) from **what it means** (the
inference the clues point to). They meet a worked example, hunt for the **clue words** that
bridge the two columns, and write their own "the text says / this means" pair with the clue
named — the second step of citing text evidence.

## Objectives

- Cite text evidence to support an interpretation of what a text says and means.
  _(D04.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

A story says: "the wind died and the birds stopped singing, and everyone began rolling up the
mats." It never once says the word "storm." But every reader knows: a storm is coming. The
difference is between **what the text says** — the exact words — and **what it means** — the
idea the words point to. Skilled readers live in both places at once: they can quote the words
*and* name the meaning, and they can point to the **clues** that got them from one to the
other. This is how you read what is on the page *and* what is between the lines — in a story,
a message, or the news.

## Materials

- Says-versus-means poster
- A short practice passage
- Says-and-means recording page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the says-versus-means poster (quote → inference, joined by "because of the
  clues").
- Copy the practice passage for each pair and the recording page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **inference is not
guessing — it is evidence-based**: what a text *means* must be bridged to what it *says* by
real clue words. Retrieval: open by recalling Lesson 5's "because the text says" frame, then
extend it to "and the clues are ___." Model the says-versus-means pair slowly (a novice skill;
worked example first — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Guard against over-reading: an
inference is only as good as the clues that support it, and two readers can defensibly differ
— then the text, not the louder voice, decides (`docs/philosophy.md` §5). Honor every home
language; inferring in one's strongest language is the real skill, and translating an
inference is advanced work. Keep the passage short and global.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall last lesson's frame: "I think ___ because the text says ___."
   Today you add the middle step — the **clues** that let a text *mean* more than it *says*.
2. **Meet the two columns (10 min).** Look at the poster. Left: "**the text says** — 'the wind
   died and the birds stopped singing, and everyone began rolling up the mats.'" Right: "**this
   means** — a storm is coming." The arrow between them is labeled "**because of the clues**."
   The text never says "storm"; the clues — *wind died, birds stopped, rolling up mats* — point
   there. That is **inference**: a meaning built from evidence, not a guess.
3. **Learn to name the clues (5 min).** For any inference, ask: "**Which exact words are my
   clues?**" List them. If you can't name clues, you don't have an inference yet — you have a
   feeling. A feeling can start an inference; it can't finish one.
4. **Hunt the clues (20 min).** Read the practice passage with a partner. Find one thing the
   text *means* without *saying*. On your page, fill both columns — the **quote** on the left,
   the **meaning** on the right — and on the clue line, list the **exact words** that bridge
   them. Check each clue against the text: is it really there?
5. **Close (5 min).** Reading is not just collecting words; it is building meaning from them.
   When you can say what the text *says*, what it *means*, and which *clues* connect the two,
   you are reading the way thoughtful people everywhere read — carefully, and on evidence.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the quote and the meaning already paired, and ask the learner to find
  and underline the clue words that connect them.
- **Extension:** Find a passage where two meanings are both possible, and argue in one
  paragraph which one the clues support better, citing both sets of evidence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/self):** Does the learner's meaning rest on real clue words from the text,
  and can they name the clues that bridge what it says to what it means?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If someone asked 'how do you know?' could I point to the
  exact clue words — not just say 'it feels that way'?"

## Home connection

Read a short message or story and tell someone one thing it *says* and one thing it *means*,
naming the clue words that got you from one to the other.

## Resources

  Adolescent Literacy." The evidence-vs-belief distinction is a core value (`docs/philosophy.md`
  §5). On explicit instruction for novice skills, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
