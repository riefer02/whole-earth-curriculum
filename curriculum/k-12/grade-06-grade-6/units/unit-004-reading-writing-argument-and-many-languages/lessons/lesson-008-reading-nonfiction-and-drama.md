# Lesson 8 — Reading Nonfiction and Drama

## Summary

Learners read and understand **nonfiction and drama**, two more genres in a well-read life.
They learn what to look for in each — **headings and checkable facts** in nonfiction;
**characters, dialogue, and stage directions** in drama — read a short original example of
each (in their strongest language where possible), and map what they find.

## Objectives

- Read and understand nonfiction and drama in at least one language. _(D04.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

Some writing is there to tell you what is *true*: a page headed "How Rain Reaches the River,"
with facts you could check. Other writing is there to be *performed*: a page where two people
speak and the words in brackets say "she sets the basket down slowly." The first is
**nonfiction** — information you can verify; the second is **drama** — a play meant to be
acted. A well-read person can move between them without confusion: they know that a fact and a
spoken line are judged by different standards — a fact by *whether it is true*, a line by
*what it shows about the character*. Both are everywhere, in every language, from a newspaper
to a festival stage.

## Materials

- Nonfiction-and-drama poster
- A short nonfiction passage
- A short play scene
- Genre notes page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the nonfiction-and-drama poster (information page and play page, each with its
  "what to look for" list).
- Copy the nonfiction passage and the play scene for each pair, and a genre notes page per
  learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **nonfiction and drama
have different shapes and different standards**: nonfiction is organized with headings and
built from facts you can check; drama is built from characters, dialogue, and stage directions
and is meant to be performed. Retrieval: recall Lesson 7's genre shapes, and recall the
fact/opinion distinction from Grade 5. Keep the examples original and short; read the scene
aloud with parts — drama is for the voice and body. Honor home languages above all: read and
map in the strongest language, and never rank genres or languages. Nonfiction and drama exist
in every human culture; they are not any one region's invention.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of something true you learned today, and a moment of acting you
   have seen or done. Today you read **nonfiction** and **drama**.
2. **Meet the two pages (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the left, **nonfiction**: look for
   a **heading** that names the topic, and **facts** — statements you could check. On the
   right, **drama**: look for **characters** (who speaks), **dialogue** (their words), and
   **stage directions** (bracketed instructions for how they move and speak). Nonfiction asks
   "is it true?"; drama asks "what does this show?"
3. **Read the nonfiction (15 min).** With a partner, read the short passage. Map it: what is
   the heading? what facts does it give? for one fact, say how you could check it. Summarize
   the passage in two sentences.
4. **Read the drama (15 min).** Read the short scene aloud, taking the parts. Map it: who are
   the characters? what does the dialogue reveal about them? what do the stage directions tell
   the actors to do? Act it once, following the stage directions.
5. **Close (5 min).** You have now read four of the great genres — fiction, poetry, nonfiction,
   and drama. Each has its own shape and its own way of being true. Knowing the shape is how
   you read anything, anywhere, with confidence.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read the passage and scene aloud together; provide a simple frame (heading +
   facts; characters + one stage direction) and a single "how could we check it?" prompt.
- **Extension:** Explain in one paragraph why a fact and a line of dialogue must be judged by
  different standards — truth versus what it reveals — and give an example of each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner identify a nonfiction text's heading and
  checkable facts, and a drama's characters, dialogue, and stage directions, in at least one
  language?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I point to each thing I claim — the heading, the
  fact, the line, the stage direction — in the actual text?"

## Home connection

Find one piece of nonfiction (a label, a message, a notice) and one moment of drama (a story
acted out, a conversation) around you, and name what each is made of.

## Resources

- Genre knowledge (how informational and dramatic texts are structured) supports
  comprehension; see Duke & Pearson's synthesis and the IES "Improving Adolescent Literacy"
  guide. Distinguishing a checkable fact from a spoken line is part of the evidence-vs-belief
  skill (`docs/philosophy.md` §5). Reading plays aloud supports fluency and comprehension; see
  the National Reading Panel report (2000).
