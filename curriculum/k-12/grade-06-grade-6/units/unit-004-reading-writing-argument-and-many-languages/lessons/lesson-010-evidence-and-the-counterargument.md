# Lesson 10 — Evidence and the Counterargument

## Summary

Learners strengthen their argument by adding **evidence** under each reason and by answering a
**counterargument**. They see the full modeled argument — claim, reasons with evidence, a
counterargument, and a **concede-and-answer** response — then add both to their own drafts.
This is step two of three toward a complete argument.

## Objectives

- Write an argument with evidence and a response to a counterargument. _(D04.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

Your reasons say *why*; now someone asks *how do you know?* and *what about the person who
disagrees?* "Trees are nice," says your neighbor, "but they cost money and need care." If you
answer only with more reasons, you sound like you are repeating yourself. The strong move is
twofold: **evidence** for your reasons ("streets with no trees feel far hotter than shaded
streets") and a fair **response to the counterargument** ("true — trees do need care, but the
shade and fruit give back more each year, and we can share the watering"). Notice the response
*concedes first* — it admits the other side has a point — and *then* answers. That is what
makes an argument honest instead of just loud.

## Materials

- Full-argument poster
- Evidence sentence frames
- Counterargument frames

## Preparation

- Print or draw the full-argument poster (claim, reasons + evidence, counterargument,
  response).
- Copy the evidence frames and counterargument frames for each learner.
- Have learners bring their claim and reasons from Lesson 9.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The two ideas to land are **evidence** (each
reason needs support you can point to) and the **counterargument response** (concede what is
fair, then answer). Teach both with the worked example (a novice skill; model step-by-step —
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Retrieval: connect evidence here to the text evidence of
Lessons 5–6 (same idea, now for your own claim) and to Grade 5's fact/opinion work. The ethics
lens matters: a good argument *names its own weakest point* and answers it honestly — that is
intellectual honesty, not weakness (`docs/philosophy.md` §5). Honor every home language; write
in the strongest language. Watch that "evidence" stays checkable, not just a stronger opinion.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall your claim and reasons. Today you add the two things that make
   an argument *strong*: **evidence** and a **response to the counterargument**.
2. **Meet the full argument (10 min).** Look at the poster. The claim and reasons are joined
   now by **evidence**: under "shade" — "streets with no trees feel far hotter than shaded
   streets"; under "fruit" — "a single mango tree can give many baskets of fruit each year."
   Then the **counterargument**: "some people say trees cost money and need care." And the
   **response**: "true — trees do need care, but the shade and fruit give back more each year,
   and we can share the watering." The response **concedes** what is fair, then **answers**.
3. **Learn the frames (5 min).** Evidence: "**For example, ___**", "**The evidence is that
   ___**". Counterargument: "**Some people say ___, and they have a point, but ___**", "**It is
   true that ___, yet ___**". Practice one of each aloud.
4. **Strengthen your argument (20 min).** Under each of your reasons, write one piece of
   **evidence** you could check or point to. Then write the **counterargument** — the best
   honest objection someone could raise — and your **response**, conceding what is fair before
   you answer. Read it to a partner; ask them: "Is my evidence checkable? Did I answer the
   objection fairly?"
5. **Close (5 min).** You now hold a nearly complete argument: claim, reasons, evidence, and a
   fair answer to the other side. Next lesson you will **revise and publish** it so it is
   clear, complete, and ready to be read.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide one reason with its evidence already written and one counterargument to
  answer; ask the learner to write just the concede-and-answer response.
- **Extension:** Raise two counterarguments and answer both, then write one sentence on which
  response is stronger and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/portfolio):** Does each reason carry checkable evidence, and does the
  response concede what is fair before answering the counterargument?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to where each piece of evidence came from, and
  would the other side feel I heard them before I answered?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home your argument, ask them to raise the strongest objection they can, and
answer it — conceding first, then responding.

## Resources

- Argument writing with evidence and counterargument (Toulmin's claim/evidence/rebuttal) is
  standard; see Graham & Perin, "Writing Next" (Carnegie Corporation, 2007) and the IES
  "Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively" guide. Intellectual honesty in answering
  the strongest objection is an ethics commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5). On explicit
  instruction and worked examples, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
