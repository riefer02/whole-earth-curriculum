# Lesson 7 — Reading Fiction and Poetry

## Summary

Learners read and understand **fiction and poetry**, two genres that work on the imagination.
They learn what to look for in each — **characters, setting, and plot** in a story; **lines,
stanzas, and images** in a poem — read a short original example of each (in their strongest
language where possible), and map what they find.

## Objectives

- Read and understand fiction and poetry in at least one language. _(D04.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

A story carries you into another life: a girl crossing a flooded field, a boy trading at a
festival. A poem does something different: it hands you a picture made of words — "the moon, a
silver coin / dropped in the well of night." Both are **fiction and poetry** — writing that
works on the imagination, in every language and every corner of the world. Each has its own
shape, and knowing that shape is how you read them well: in a story you follow **who, where,
and what happens**; in a poem you listen for **the image** and the **rhythm**. Neither is
harder than the other; they are different tools for saying true things about being human.

## Materials

- Fiction-and-poetry poster
- A short fiction excerpt
- A short poem
- Genre notes page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fiction-and-poetry poster (story page and poem page, each with its "what
  to look for" list).
- Copy the fiction excerpt and the poem for each pair, and a genre notes page per learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **genres have
shapes**, and reading well means looking for what each genre is built from: **fiction** —
characters (who), setting (where and when), plot (what happens); **poetry** — lines, stanzas,
and images (pictures in words). Use original, short examples so no copyright is in question,
and read them aloud — poetry especially is meant for the ear. Honor home languages above all:
learners should read and map texts in their strongest language, and a poem or story in a home
language is a gift to the whole class, never a "translation problem." No genre is above
another; fiction and poetry are ways humans everywhere have always made meaning. This is the
first of two genre lessons; nonfiction and drama follow.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a story you love and a song or rhyme you remember. Today you
   read two kinds of imaginative writing: **fiction** and **poetry**.
2. **Meet the two pages (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the left, a **story**: look for
   **characters** (who), **setting** (where and when), and **plot** (what happens, in order).
   On the right, a **poem**: look for **lines** and **stanzas** (the poem's shape) and
   **images** (pictures made of words). A story tells what happens; a poem paints a moment.
3. **Read the fiction (15 min).** With a partner, read the short story. Map it: who are the
   characters? where and when is it set? what happens — the plot — in order? Tell the story's
   shape back in a few sentences.
4. **Read the poem (15 min).** Read the short poem aloud — twice, slowly. Then map it: what
   images do you see? what do the lines and stanzas do with rhythm and breath? Say which image
   stays with you and why.
5. **Close (5 min).** Fiction gives you another life to live for a little while; poetry gives
   you a moment to hold. When you know what each genre is made of, you can enter both — in any
   language — and come back with something worth keeping.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Read the story and poem aloud together; provide a simple three-part frame
  (who / where / what happens) and a single "favorite image" prompt.
- **Extension:** Compare how a story and a poem could treat the *same* idea — say, a storm —
  differently, and write one sentence on which form fits which effect and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner map a story's characters, setting, and
  plot, and name an image and the shape of a poem, in at least one language?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to each thing I claim — the character, the
  setting, the plot, the image — in the actual text?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a story or a poem they love — in any language — and name one thing
about its shape: who it is about, or one image it paints.

## Resources

  Literacy" guide. The claim that no genre or language is above another is a values commitment
  (`docs/philosophy.md` §4). Read-aloud supports fluency and enjoyment; see the work on
  read-alouds in the National Reading Panel report (2000).
