# Lesson 11 — Revising and Publishing My Argument

## Summary

Learners **revise and publish** their argument. They run a revision checklist — claim clear,
every reason supported, evidence checkable, counterargument answered, order strong — make at
least two revisions, and write a final published copy for real readers. This completes the
three-lesson argument arc.

## Objectives

- Write a well-developed argument with a claim, reasons, evidence, and a response to a
  counterargument. _(D04.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

The first version of anything is rarely the best version. A potter doesn't stop at the first
shape; she smooths it, checks it, and only then sets it out for others. Writing is the same:
the draft is the clay, and **revising** is the smoothing — checking that the claim is clear,
every reason is supported, the evidence is checkable, and the counterargument is answered —
before you **publish** it for readers. Publishing is not a contest; it is the moment you hand
your honest thinking to other people, in your own words, in your own language.

## Materials

- Revision-check poster
- Revision checklist
- Publishing page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the revision-check poster (checklist plus a before/after example).
- Copy the revision checklist for each learner (and peers).
- Copy the publishing page, and prepare the publication wall, booklet, or folder.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **revision is
re-seeing** — checking the *thinking* (claim, support, evidence, counterargument), while
editing checks the *surface* (spelling, punctuation). Model one revision on the board — take a
real draft sentence and improve it with the checklist (a novice skill; worked example first —
Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Retrieval: this lesson pulls together Lessons 9 and 10 and
revisits the listening and evidence skills of Lessons 1–6. Frame publishing cooperatively: the
goal is a strong, honest piece shared for real readers, never a ranking (`docs/philosophy.md`
§8). Honor every home language — publish in the learner's strongest language, and celebrate a
published piece in any language as complete and worthy. Keep peer feedback specific and kind:
one thing that works, one thing to try.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at your draft from the last two lessons. It is almost an argument.
   Today you **revise** it — make it clearer and stronger — and **publish** it.
2. **Meet the checklist (10 min).** Look at the poster. Five questions: Is my **claim** clear?
   Is every **reason** supported? Is my **evidence** checkable? Did I **answer the
   counterargument**? Is my **order** strong (strongest reason first)? The "before" page shows
   a muddled draft with an unsupported reason crossed out; the "after" page shows it fixed.
   That is revision: keeping the idea, improving the work.
3. **Run the checklist (15 min).** Go through your own draft with the checklist, question by
   question. Mark anything that needs fixing. Make **at least two revisions** — change the
   order, add or cut a reason, sharpen a sentence, make the evidence concrete. If you can, have
   a partner run the checklist too and tell you one thing that works and one thing to try.
4. **Publish (15 min).** On a clean page, write your final argument: a title, your claim, your
   reasons with evidence, and your response to the counterargument. Read it once aloud to
   yourself before you set it out — you will hear the last rough spots.
5. **Close (5 min).** You have done what writers everywhere do: thought, drafted, revised, and
   shared. Your argument now stands on reasons and evidence — and it answers, fairly, the
   person who sees things differently. That is a piece of writing to be proud of, in any
   language.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter checklist with only two items (claim clear; counterargument
  answered) and a sentence frame for one revision; allow a shorter published piece.
- **Extension:** Revise also for voice — the strongest first sentence and the clearest link
  between claim and reasons — and write one sentence explaining each change you made.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio/peer/self):** The published argument includes a clear claim, reasons
  with evidence, and a response to a counterargument, and the learner made at least two
  revisions shown by the checklist.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could a stranger read my published argument and name my
  claim, my reasons, my evidence, and how I answered the other side?"

## Home connection

Read your published argument to someone at home — in the language that feels strongest — and
ask them one question: "Did it make sense, and did I answer the other side fairly?"

## Resources

- Revision as re-seeing the thinking (distinct from editing the surface) and publishing for
  real readers support writing development; see Graham & Perin, "Writing Next" (Carnegie
  Corporation, 2007) and the IES "Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively" guide.
  Cooperative, non-ranked publication reflects `docs/philosophy.md` §8. On explicit instruction
  and worked examples, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
