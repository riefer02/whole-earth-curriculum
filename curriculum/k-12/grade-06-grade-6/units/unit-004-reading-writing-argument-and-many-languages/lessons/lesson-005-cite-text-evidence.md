# Lesson 5 — Cite Text Evidence

## Summary

Learners learn to **cite text evidence**: to point to the exact words in a text that support an
interpretation of what it means. They meet a worked example of an interpretation joined to its
quote by "**because the text says**," practice underlining supporting words, and write their
own interpretation with a cited quote.

## Objectives

- Cite text evidence to support an interpretation of what a text says and means.
  _(D04.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

Two people read the same story and come away with different ideas. "Amara is just watching the
river," says one. "Amara is worried about a flood," says the other. Which is right? You can't
settle it by feeling or by who says it louder — you settle it by going back to the **words**.
"The water crept a little higher" and "the village must move the rice sacks" are **evidence**.
An interpretation without evidence is a guess; an interpretation with **cited evidence** is an
argument you can check. Reading well is this back-and-forth: what do I think, and **where in
the text** does it say so?

## Materials

- Cite-the-evidence poster
- A short practice passage
- Evidence sentence frames

## Preparation

- Print or draw the cite-the-evidence poster (passage → highlighted quote → interpretation).
- Copy the practice passage for each pair and the sentence frames for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that an **interpretation
must be anchored to the text** — and the anchor is an exact quote, not a vague memory. Teach
the frame "**I think ___ because the text says '___'**" as a worked example (a novice skill;
model then guide — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). This is the ethical heart of reading
too: we are honest about *where* our idea came from, and we can change our mind when the text
disagrees (`docs/philosophy.md` §5 — evidence distinguished from belief). Honor every home
language: learners may read and cite a passage in their strongest language; citing in a second
language is advanced, valuable work. Keep the practice text short and global — a flood, a
garden, a journey — so it lives anywhere.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time two people read the same thing and understood it
   differently. Today you learn to settle such differences with **text evidence** — the exact
   words.
2. **Meet the poster (10 min).** Look at the poster. A passage tells of Amara watching the
   river rise. One sentence is highlighted: "each night the water crept a little higher up the
   bank." The interpretation is joined to it: "**I think Amara is worried about a flood,
   because the text says 'each night the water crept a little higher.'**" Another: "I think she
   is preparing, **because the text says 'the village must move the rice sacks to higher
   ground.'**" The idea points to the words; the words hold up the idea.
3. **Learn the frame (5 min).** Read the frames: "**I think ___ because the text says '___'.**"
   and "**The text says '___,' which shows ___.**" These are bridges between your idea and the
   page. Say one aloud.
4. **Underline and cite (20 min).** Read the practice passage with a partner. Underline three
   exact places that could support an interpretation. Then write one interpretation of your
   own, and under it, copy the **exact words** that support it, using a frame. Read it back and
   check: is that quote really in the text, word for word?
5. **Close (5 min).** A good reader is like a careful witness: they can always point to what
   they saw. Your idea is only as strong as the words it stands on — so keep them close.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a shorter passage and one interpretation with the quote already
  selected; ask the learner to match each interpretation to its quote.
- **Extension:** Cite two pieces of evidence for one interpretation, and write one sentence on
  which is stronger support and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/portfolio):** Does the learner's interpretation rest on an exact, copied
  quote from the text, and does the quote actually support the idea?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I point to the exact words in the text that my idea
  stands on — and are they really there?"

## Home connection

Read a short passage — a story, a message, a label — and tell someone one thing you think it
means, ending with "because the text says…" and the exact words.

## Resources

- Citing text evidence is a foundational comprehension skill; see Duke & Pearson's synthesis of
  comprehension strategy research and the IES practice guides (e.g., "Improving Adolescent
  Literacy"). The distinction between evidence and belief is a core value (`docs/philosophy.md`
  §5). On explicit instruction for novice skills, see Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
