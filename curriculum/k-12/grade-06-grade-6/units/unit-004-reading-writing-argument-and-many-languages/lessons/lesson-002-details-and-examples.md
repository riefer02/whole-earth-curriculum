# Lesson 2 — Details and Examples

## Summary

Learners sharpen the listening skill from Lesson 1 by separating **supporting details**
(general reasons) from **examples** (specific instances). They meet two test questions —
"**Does it apply broadly?**" versus "**Is it one specific case?**" — sort sentences into two
columns, and write a detail and an example of their own for one claim.

## Objectives

- Distinguish a speaker's supporting details from their examples. _(D04.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Someone arguing for a shaded rest stop says two different kinds of things. "**Shade keeps
people cool all day**" is a **detail** — a general reason that would be true for anyone,
anywhere. "**Yesterday the market seller napped in the one shady spot**" is an **example** — a
specific story about one person, one day. Both support the claim, but they work differently:
a detail gives you the *rule*, an example gives you the *picture*. Telling them apart is how
you know exactly what a speaker is offering you — a general reason to weigh, or a single case
to check.

## Materials

- Detail-versus-example poster
- Detail-or-example sentence strips
- Two-column sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the detail-versus-example poster (two columns with the two test questions).
- Copy a set of detail-or-example sentence strips for each pair.
- Copy the two-column sorting page.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **details are general
and examples are specific** — the difference is *scope*, not importance. Neither is "better";
each does a job in an argument, and a strong argument usually needs both. Retrieval practice:
open by having learners recall Lesson 1's trunk/branch/leaf image before refining it. Model
the two test questions on a close call; do not rush past sentences that feel like both — those
are the thinking. Honor every home language: learners sort and argue in their strongest
language. Keep sorting cooperative and let learners justify choices rather than racing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall last lesson's tree: trunk (claim), branches (details), leaves
   (examples). Today you look closer at the difference between a **branch** and a **leaf** —
   a **detail** and an **example**.
2. **Meet the two questions (10 min).** Look at the poster. A **supporting detail** is a
   **general reason** that applies broadly — "shade keeps people cool all day." An **example**
   is a **specific instance** — "yesterday the market seller napped in the one shady spot."
   Two questions sort them: **Does it apply broadly?** (detail) and **Is it one specific
   case?** (example).
3. **Sort the strips (15 min).** With a partner, read each sentence and place it in the
   "detail" or "example" column. For each one, say *why*: is it a broad rule or a single case?
   Slow down on the close ones and talk them through.
4. **Write your own (10 min).** Choose one claim — "our class should start a reading corner,"
   or one of your own. Write **one detail** (a general reason) and **one example** (a specific
   case) to support it, and label each.
5. **Close (5 min).** A thoughtful listener hears not just *that* a speaker supports a claim,
   but *how* — with a general reason you can weigh, or a specific case you can check. Knowing
   the difference keeps you from being moved by a single vivid story when the general reason is
   weak — and from missing a strong reason hidden under one small example.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentences that differ sharply (one clearly general, one clearly a named
  person and day), and a word bank ("applies to everyone / one person / one day").
- **Extension:** Take a general detail and expand it into a specific example, and take an
  example and generalize it into a detail; then say which is more convincing for one audience
  and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/self):** Can the learner sort sentences into detail and example and state
  whether each applies broadly or names a specific case?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "For each sentence, could I say 'this is true for anyone'
  or 'this is about one specific person or moment'?"

## Home connection

Listen for one general reason and one specific example in a conversation today, and tell
someone which is which and how you knew.
