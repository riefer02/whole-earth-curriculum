# Lesson 13 — The Same Idea in Many Languages

## Summary

Learners compare how the **same idea is expressed in two or more languages** and notice what
is **gained or lost** in each. They meet a worked example — a greeting of peace across Arabic,
Hebrew, and English — compare idea cards, and record one idea in two languages with a note on
what each carries that the other does not.

## Objectives

- Compare how the same idea is expressed in two or more languages, noting what is gained or
  lost. _(D04.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

One person says "**السلام عليكم**" (*as-salaamu alaykum*), another says "**שלום**" (*shalom*),
a third says "**peace be with you.**" All three greet with peace, yet none says exactly what
the others say. The Arabic *wishes a blessing on the other person*; the Hebrew *shalom* also
means *wholeness* and works as hello and goodbye; the English says the idea plainly and lets
the blessing and the wholeness go. No language is "better" — but **each keeps something the
others let go, and lets go of something the others keep**. Comparing languages this way is how
you discover that meaning is never quite carried whole from one tongue to another — and that
this is a richness, not a flaw.

## Materials

- One-idea-three-languages poster
- Idea comparison cards
- Gained-and-lost page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the one-idea-three-languages poster (peace greeting in three languages with
  gained/lost notes).
- Copy idea comparison cards for each group and a gained-and-lost page per learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **translation always
gains and loses**, and this is a *wealth*, not a defect — no language is "better," and no
language says everything (`docs/philosophy.md` §4). Use accurate examples and check them: the
Arabic *as-salaamu alaykum* ("peace be upon you") is a blessing; Hebrew *shalom* means peace,
wholeness, hello, and goodbye, sharing a Semitic root (*s-l-m*) with the Arabic *salaam*. Be
careful and humble with languages you do not speak — prefer examples learners or their
families actually know, and let those who speak a language be its experts. Invite learners to
share their own languages as gifts. The linguistic facts are evidence; the claim that all
languages are equally worthy is a value we hold (`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5). Retrieval: connect
back to Grade 5's writing-systems and language-and-culture lessons.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a greeting, a thanks, or a word for home you know in more
   than one language. Today you ask: what does each version **keep** that the others **let
   go**?
2. **Meet the three greetings (10 min).** Look at the poster. Three bubbles, one idea — peace.
   "**السلام عليكم**" wishes a blessing *on the other person*. "**שלום**" carries peace *and
   wholeness*, and is also hello and goodbye. "Peace be with you" says it plainly and drops
   the blessing and the wholeness. Each is complete; none is complete in the same way. That is
   **gained and lost**.
3. **Compare the cards (15 min).** With your group, take the idea cards. For each pair of
   versions, ask two questions: "**What does this one keep that the other lets go?**" and
   "**What does it let go?**" If someone in the group speaks the language, let them be the
   expert.
4. **Record your own (15 min).** On your page, write one idea in **two languages you know or
   can learn** — a greeting, a thanks, a word for home, a proverb. Under each, note **one
   thing it expresses that the other does not**. Check with someone who knows both if you can.
5. **Close (5 min).** The world's languages are not copies of each other; they are many
   different ways of holding the world. Noticing what is gained and lost between them is how
   you meet each language — and each speaker — with respect and wonder.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one pair of greetings the learner already knows, and ask only "what does
  your language's greeting carry that the other doesn't?"
- **Extension:** Compare a proverb or a nuanced word across three languages, and write one
  sentence on why no single translation can be "the same" as the original.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner give one idea in two languages and name at
  least one thing each expresses that the other does not?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "For my two versions, could I say what each one keeps and
  what it lets go — without calling either one better?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one idea — a greeting, a thanks, a proverb — in a language they know,
and find one thing it carries that your own language's version does not.

## Resources

- Translation as always gaining and losing is a core insight of translation studies and
  linguistic anthropology; see Daniel L. Everett, *Language: The Cultural Tool*, and the work
  on untranslatability and linguistic relativity (Sapir, Whorf, and later linguists). The
  shared Semitic root of *salaam/shalom* is standard in historical linguistics. The claim that
  all languages are equally valid is a values commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
