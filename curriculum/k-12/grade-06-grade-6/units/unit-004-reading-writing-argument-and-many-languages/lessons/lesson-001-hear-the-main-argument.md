# Lesson 1 — Hear the Main Argument

## Summary

Learners learn to **hear a speaker's main argument** and separate it from the **supporting
details** and **examples**. They meet a worked example of one spoken argument drawn as a tree
(trunk = claim, branches = details, leaves = examples), practice a "**listen for the trunk**"
routine, and sort sentences into three buckets with a partner. This is the first of two
listening skills; telling details from examples follows in the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Listen for and distinguish a speaker's main argument from supporting details and examples.
  _(D04.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

A neighbor stands up at a gathering and says, "We should build a shaded rest stop by the
field." Then she talks for a long time: people work there all day, there is no shade, the old
woman carries water, the children play at noon, the market seller naps on his cart. If you
walk away only remembering "she talked a lot," you missed the one thing she was really
arguing for. **Listening for the main argument** is the skill of finding that one sentence —
the **claim** — and seeing how everything else hangs off it as **details** and **examples**.
Whether the gathering is a village meeting or a city council, a strong listener can say back:
"Her main point is ___, and the rest was support."

## Materials

- Argument-tree poster
- Argument sentence strips
- Three-bucket sorting page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the argument-tree poster (trunk = claim, branches = details, leaves = examples).
- Copy a set of argument sentence strips for each pair.
- Copy the three-bucket sorting page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that an **argument has a
spine**: one main claim, supported by details (general reasons) and examples (specific
instances). The skill is *separating* them when a speaker mixes them together — not judging
whether the argument is good yet. Model the tree slowly and aloud; because this is a novice
skill, use a worked example and guided practice before independent sorting (Kirschner, Sweller
& Clark, 2006). Honor every home language: learners may listen to and sort arguments in their
strongest language. Use real, local arguments as examples wherever you can, and keep the
"whose claim is it?" question alive — a claim is a *person's* position, not a fact that needs
no support (`docs/philosophy.md` §5).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time someone asked you for something and you had to find the
   one real point under all their words. Today you learn to **hear the main argument**.
2. **Meet the tree (10 min).** Look at the poster. One argument is drawn as a tree. The
   **trunk** is the **main claim**: "We should build a shaded rest stop by the field." The
   **branches** are **supporting details** — general reasons like "people work there all day"
   and "there is no shade." The **leaves** are **examples** — specific cases like "the old
   woman who carries water" and "the children who play at noon." A claim *holds* the
   argument; details and examples *hold up* the claim.
3. **Listen for the trunk (10 min).** With a partner, one of you says a short argument aloud —
   for something small and real (where to sit, which game to play, where to put the new water
   tap). The other listens and names the **trunk**: "Your main claim is ___." Then check:
   did they find the one sentence the whole argument rests on? Swap roles.
4. **Sort the strips (15 min).** Take the sentence strips. Read each and place it in the right
   bucket: **main claim**, **supporting detail**, or **example**. For each one, say *why* — is
   it the one big sentence, a general reason, or a specific case?
5. **Close (5 min).** A speaker gives you many sentences but one spine. Hearing the **claim**
   first, then the **details** and **examples** that support it, is the beginning of every
   good discussion — and the first step toward making arguments of your own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start with an argument that has one short claim and two very different
  sentences; provide a starter sentence ("The main claim is ___ because…") and hand signals.
- **Extension:** Listen to a two-sided argument and separate *each side's* claim from its own
  details and examples, then note where the two claims actually differ.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer/observation):** Can the learner state a speaker's main claim and sort
  supporting sentences into details versus examples, giving a reason for each placement?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If the speaker heard me name the trunk, would they nod
  and say 'yes, that's what I'm arguing'?"

## Home connection

Listen to someone at home make a request or a case today; afterward, say back their one main
claim, then one detail and one example they used.

## Resources
