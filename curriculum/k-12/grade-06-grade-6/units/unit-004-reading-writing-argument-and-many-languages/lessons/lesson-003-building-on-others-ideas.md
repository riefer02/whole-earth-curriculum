# Lesson 3 — Building on Others' Ideas

## Summary

Learners practice the heart of good discussion: **building on others' ideas** instead of just
waiting to speak. They meet **sentence starters** that connect a new idea to the one before
it, watch a worked example of a "build-on" chain, and hold a short discussion where every turn
visibly rests on the last idea.

## Objectives

- Participate in a group discussion by building on others' ideas. _(D04.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of a real conversation that went somewhere good — someone said one thing, and the next
person's words made it *bigger*, not different. "We should start a class garden." "**Building
on that** — a garden gives us food to share." "**That connects to health** — we'd eat what we
grow." Each idea rested on the one before it, like blocks in a tower. The opposite is a
conversation where everyone only waits for their turn and nobody's words touch anyone else's.
**Building on others' ideas** is the skill that turns a group of people talking into one
*mind thinking together* — in a classroom, a family, or a community meeting.

## Materials

- Build-on sentence starters
- A discussion question card
- A "building blocks" poster

## Preparation

- Print or draw the building-blocks poster (a tower of speech bubbles, each resting on the one
  below).
- Copy the build-on sentence starters for each pair.
- Prepare one discussion question the group genuinely cares about.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **discussion is a
shared construction, not a sequence of separate speeches** — a good turn *connects* to the last
one. Teach the sentence starters explicitly (a novice skill; model then guide — Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006), and hold the discussion lightly: the goal is *connection*, not
coverage or correctness. Honor every home language — learners may build on ideas in their
strongest language, and code-switching between languages in one discussion is welcome, not a
mistake. Watch for quiet learners and invite them with a gentle, genuine question; "build on"
starters give everyone a way in. Cooperation over competition (`docs/philosophy.md` §8).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time a conversation grew because each person added to the
   last idea. Today you practice doing that on purpose: **building on others' ideas**.
2. **Meet the tower (10 min).** Look at the poster. Four bubbles stack into a tower. The first
   says, "We should start a class garden." The next three each *build on* the one below:
   "Building on that — a garden gives us food to share." "That connects to health — we'd eat
   what we grow." "I want to add — we could give extra food to families." Nobody erases or
   wins; the tower just gets taller.
3. **Learn the starters (10 min).** Read the starter card: "**Building on what ___ said…**",
   "**That connects to…**", "**I want to add…**", "**Another reason is…**". Practice saying
   one starter aloud once. Notice: each begins by *pointing back* to the idea before it.
4. **Hold a discussion (20 min).** In a small group, take up your question. The rule: **every
   turn must start by connecting to the last idea** — with a starter, or in your own words. If
   someone forgets, gently pass the talking stone back and ask, "How does that connect?"
   Build the tallest tower you can in ten minutes, then share one built idea with the whole
   group.
5. **Close (5 min).** A discussion is not a list of speeches; it is one idea that many hands
   lift together. When you build on others, you make everyone's thinking stronger — including
   your own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the starter sentence to copy and a single question with a shared word
  bank; allow a two-person "build one step" turn before the full circle.
- **Extension:** After the discussion, map the tower — who built on whose idea, and *how*
  (added, connected, gave a new reason, asked a follow-up) — and name which moves made the
  thinking deepest.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Does each learner's turn visibly connect to the previous
  idea, using a starter or their own link, rather than starting an unrelated point?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If someone pointed at my sentence, could they draw a line
  from it to the idea right before it?"

## Home connection

In a conversation at home, try one "building on" move — "building on what you said…" — and
notice how the other person responds.

## Resources

- Cooperative discussion and "building on" talk moves are well-supported structures; see the
  "talk moves" of accountable talk (Michaels & O'Connor) and cooperative-learning research
  (Johnson & Johnson). On cooperation over competition and explicit instruction for novice
  skills, see `docs/philosophy.md` and Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006).
