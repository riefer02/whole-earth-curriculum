# Lesson 14 — Across a Language Difference

## Summary

Learners cap the unit by **communicating across a language difference**. They meet four
bridges — **gestures, drawings, translation tools, and patience** — act out real cross-language
scenarios, and reflect on what works, what needs care, and why every language and speaker
deserves patience and respect. The lesson revisits the unit's argument, evidence, discussion,
and language-comparison skills.

## Objectives

- Communicate across a language difference using gestures, drawings, translation tools, and
  patience. _(D04.S4.06.02)_
- Compare how the same idea is expressed in two or more languages, noting what is gained or
  lost. _(D04.S4.06.01)_
- Participate in a group discussion, building on others' ideas and disagreeing respectfully.
  _(D04.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

A traveler reaches a village where no one speaks her language. She is thirsty. She doesn't
need perfect words to be understood — she can **gesture** a cup to her mouth, **draw** a cup in
the dirt, try a **translation tool** on a phone if she has one, and above all bring
**patience** while the two of them find the way. Communication across a language difference is
not about speaking the "right" language; it is about **meeting the other person halfway**,
using every tool you have and treating their language — and yours — as equal. This is the
skill you have been building all unit: listening, arguing with reasons, and honoring many
languages, now put to use with a real human being across from you.

## Materials

- Bridge-the-difference poster
- Communication scenario cards
- Bridge toolkit page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the bridge-the-difference poster (two people, four labeled bridges).
- Copy scenario cards for each group and a bridge toolkit page per learner.
- If devices are available, prepare one translation tool for a trial.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **communication across
a language difference is possible and human**, and that the four bridges — gestures, drawings,
translation tools, and patience — are tools, not magic. Hold the anti-deficit heart firmly:
not sharing a language is *ordinary*, never a *failure*, and multilingualism is wealth
(`docs/philosophy.md` §4). Be honest about translation tools — they help and they flatten
(nuance, tone, a proverb), which connects back to Lesson 13's "gained and lost." This is the
capstone, so build in retrieval: learners draw on the discussion moves (Lessons 3–4), the
argument and evidence skills (Lessons 9–11), and language comparison (Lesson 13) as they
reflect. Keep scenarios safe and everyday; celebrate every successful bridge, however small.
Honor every home language and accent in the room.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you and someone else did not share a language — and you
   still understood each other. Today you practice the bridges that make that possible.
2. **Meet the four bridges (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two people speak different
   languages, but four bridges join them: **gestures** (a hand shapes a cup), **drawings** (a
   cup in the dirt), a **translation tool** (a phone arrow between the words), and **patience**
   (a heart — the bridge that holds the others up). No single bridge is enough alone; together,
   and with patience, they carry meaning across.
3. **Act it out (20 min).** With your group, take a scenario card — asking for water, giving
   directions, welcoming a newcomer, helping someone find a place. One of you plays a person
   who does not share the others' language. Use **gestures, drawings, and patience** to
   accomplish the task; if you have a translation tool, try it once and notice what it helps
   and what it misses. Swap roles so each of you tries a bridge.
4. **Reflect on the bridges (10 min).** On your page, record for your scenario: which bridges
   carried the most meaning, what could have gone wrong, and one way patience mattered. Then,
   in your group, **discuss**: build on each other's ideas and, if you differ, disagree
   respectfully — using the moves from earlier in the unit.
5. **Close (5 min).** You began this unit learning to hear an argument; you end it learning to
   reach a person. Listening, reasoning, honoring many languages, and meeting another human
   with patience — that is what it means to communicate in a many-voiced world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one scenario with a single clear need (thirst) and practice one bridge (a
  gesture or a drawing) before combining them.
- **Extension:** Reflect in writing on when a translation tool helps and when it fails — nuance,
  tone, a proverb — and why a patient human being is still the most important bridge.

## Assessment

- **Summative (performance/observation/self):** Can the learner use at least two bridges to
  accomplish a cross-language task, and reflect on what worked, what needed care, and where
  patience mattered — treating both languages as equal?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I meet the other person halfway — using my tools,
  honoring their language, and staying patient — rather than expecting them to do all the
  work?"

## Home connection

Try one bridge with someone at home or nearby who knows a different language — a gesture, a
drawing, or one new word — and notice what carries meaning and what needs patience.

## Resources

- Strategies for cross-language communication (gesture, drawing, translation tools, patience)
  are grounded in multilingual and intercultural communication research; see UNESCO guidance on
  multilingual education and the literature on lingua franca and plurilingual competence
  (Council of Europe's CEFR companion). Translation tools have real limits — see the critical
  literature on machine translation. The principle that no language is above another and that
  multilingualism is wealth is a values commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
