# Lesson 4 — Disagreeing Respectfully

## Summary

Learners practice **disagreeing with an idea without harming the person who holds it**. They
meet the "**idea vs. person**" distinction, learn **sentence starters that acknowledge before
they disagree**, and practice respectful disagreement on real topics — the second half of good
discussion, after building on others' ideas.

## Objectives

- Participate in a group discussion by disagreeing respectfully. _(D04.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Two friends disagree about which path to take home. One says, "You're always wrong — you have
no sense of direction." The other says, "I hear you want the quick way, and I see it
differently because the short path floods in the rain." Same disagreement, two very different
conversations. The first attacks the *person*; the second disagrees with the *idea* — and
still reaches the person. Disagreement is not the enemy of good discussion; it is how groups
avoid going the wrong way together. The skill is to disagree in a way that **keeps the person
while challenging the idea** — in a friendship, a family, or a whole community deciding
something important.

## Materials

- Disagree-respectfully poster
- Disagreement sentence starters
- Disagreement practice cards

## Preparation

- Print or draw the disagree-respectfully poster (the idea between two people, with respectful
  speech bubbles).
- Copy the sentence-starter card for each pair.
- Copy the disagreement practice cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **disagreement is
healthy and necessary; disrespect is the problem, not the disagreement**. The core move is
"**acknowledge before you disagree**" — name the other person's point fairly first, then state
your own with a reason. Model a respectful and a hurtful version of the *same* disagreement and
label the difference (a novice skill; worked example first — Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).
This is a values-heavy lesson: we treat *every* person as worthy of respect even when their
idea is wrong, and we never rank people by who agrees with us (`docs/philosophy.md` §4).
Retrieval: connect back to Lesson 3's "build on" moves — disagreeing well often starts by
acknowledging, which is itself a form of building. Honor every home language and every accent;
respectful disagreement sounds different across cultures, and that variety is itself
instructive. Hold the space gently — disagreement can feel scary, and the goal is safety with
honesty.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you disagreed with someone and it went badly — or went
   well. Today you learn to disagree so that **the idea is challenged, and the person is
   kept**.
2. **Meet the two paths (10 min).** Look at the poster. Two people face each other, and the
   **idea sits between them** — not inside either one. One says, "I hear you, and I see it
   differently because the path floods in rain." The other says, "That's a fair point about
   the path; I wonder about the climb." Both disagree with the *idea*, never the *person*. The
   hurtful version — "you're always wrong" — aims at the person and helps no one.
3. **Learn the starters (10 min).** Read the card: "**I hear you, and I see it differently
   because…**", "**That's a fair point, though…**", "**I understand ___, but I think ___
   because…**". Each one **acknowledges first**, then disagrees, then gives a **reason**.
   Practice one aloud.
4. **Practice the moves (20 min).** With a partner, take a practice card — two positions on a
   real topic. One of you states your position; the other **disagrees respectfully**: name
   their point fairly, state your own, give a reason. Swap. After each round, check together:
   did we challenge the idea, and did we keep the person?
5. **Close (5 min).** Good discussion needs both halves — **building on** ideas you share and
   **disagreeing respectfully** with ideas you don't. A group that can do both thinks better
   than any one person alone, and stays a group while it thinks.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the full starter to copy and a single clear topic; practice just the
  acknowledgment ("I hear you") before adding a reason.
- **Extension:** Disagree with a *respected* idea — a class rule, an author's claim, a
  tradition — using the same moves, and reflect in one sentence on why disagreeing with someone
  you respect is hardest and most important.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Does the learner acknowledge the other's point before
  disagreeing, aim at the idea (not the person), and give a reason?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "If I put my disagreement between us as an idea, could the
  other person still feel respected — even while we differ?"

## Home connection

Next time you disagree with someone at home, try "I hear you, and I see it differently
because…" and notice whether the conversation goes further than it usually does.

## Resources

- Respectful disagreement and "acknowledge before you disagree" are core to civil discourse
