# Lesson 1 — Patterns in My Emotional Responses

## Summary

Learners discover that feelings do not happen at random: the **same feeling tends to
show up again and again** in similar situations, forming a **pattern**. Using a
situation-to-feeling chart, they notice a repeated feeling from their own week and
begin to *predict* it. This is the first step toward caring for a feeling — you can
prepare for what you can foresee.

## Objectives

- Identify patterns in their own emotional responses and name what tends to trigger
  them. _(D01.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of the weather where you live: the same season tends to bring the same kind of
sky — a dry wind, a heavy rain, a cool evening — year after year. Your feelings have a
kind of weather too. One person feels **worried** again and again before being seen or
judged; another feels **peaceful** again and again near water or quiet; another feels
**charged** after a good game. Whether you live in a busy city or on a mountain farm,
the feeling that keeps returning in the same kind of moment is your *pattern* — and
once you can see a pattern, you can prepare for it.

## Materials

- Feeling-pattern poster
- My week's feelings page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the feeling-pattern poster (three situations → one repeated feeling,
  with a banner reading "a pattern").
- Copy a "my week's feelings" page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and a quiet space for individual work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **feelings
repeat in recognizable patterns**, not that a learner's feelings are "predictable" in a
way that boxes them in. Present the pattern as *useful knowledge*, never as a label or a
verdict ("you're always worried"). A pattern is information about *what tends to happen*,
not a rule about *who the learner is*. Some learners carry heavy or private patterns
(loss, illness, home tension); invite only small, safe examples and never require a
learner to reveal a painful one. The skill is *noticing*, with gentleness. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you learned that feelings have a strength and can be
   shaped by thoughts, memories, and body sensations. Today you look for something new:
   the **patterns** — the times the same feeling keeps showing up.
2. **Meet the pattern (10 min).** Look at the poster. Here is a worked example: before a
   test → **worried**; speaking in front of the class → **worried**; meeting a new group
   → **worried**. Three different situations, the *same* feeling. That repeating shape —
   similar situation, similar feeling, again and again — is a **pattern**. A pattern is
   not a rule about you; it is a signal you can learn to read.
3. **Find your own pattern (15 min).** Think of one small, safe feeling from your week —
   worried, excited, annoyed, proud, peaceful. On your page, list a few situations where
   it showed up. Do you see a repeating shape? What tends to come *right before* the
   feeling? Check with a partner: does your partner see the same shape in your list?
4. **Read the signal (10 min).** Pick your clearest pattern. If you know this feeling
   tends to arrive in this kind of moment, what could you do next time to greet it —
   a breath, a friend, a plan? Write one small idea.
5. **Close (5 min).** You are not a mystery to yourself. Your feelings have patterns,
   and reading them is the first step to caring for them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a picture-and-word bank of situations and feelings to sort into
  patterns; scribe the learner's words.
- **Extension:** Notice a second pattern — a comfortable one and a hard one — and name
  what is different about how each arrives.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a feeling that repeats for them and
  two or three situations where it shows up?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their page back and asks, "Is this shape really
  repeating, or is it just one hard day?" and adjusts if needed.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a feeling that shows up for *them* again and again — and share
one pattern you noticed in yourself today.

## Resources

- The idea that emotional responses follow recognizable, learnable patterns is central
  to cognitive-behavioral approaches (see the work of Aaron Beck on recurring thought
  and feeling patterns) and to self-awareness as framed by CASEL:
