# Lesson 7 — Peers and Media Shape My Self-Image

## Summary

Learners examine two forces that shape **self-image** — **peers** and **media** — and learn
to *notice* their influence without fear or shame. They see that peer opinions and media
images are like mirrors, and that **some mirrors are warped**: edited, curated, partial.
Noticing the influence — and asking the egalitarian question, *who is left out of this
image?* — turns a passive feeling of "less-than" into an active, critical skill.

## Objectives

- Examine how peers and media can influence self-image, and describe a healthy response
  to negative comparison. _(D01.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

You scroll through a feed of perfect, bright photos — and suddenly your own day feels a
little smaller. A friend shrugs and says "that's not cool anymore" — and you doubt the
thing you quietly loved. Neither your peers nor the screen is evil; they are just
**mirrors**, and some mirrors are **warped**. Media shows *curated highlight reels* — the
best two seconds, the best angle, often edited — not a whole life. Peers show their own
narrow idea of what is "in." Learning to notice the mirror — and to ask what it is leaving
out — is how you keep your own picture of yourself clear.

## Materials

- Two-forces poster
- Notice the influence page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-forces poster (center "how I see myself" ← peers and media).
- Copy a "notice the influence" page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for discussion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **notice the
influence, without fear or shame** — media and peers are not villains to be feared, but
forces to be read critically. Do not imply that using media is bad or that liking what
peers like is weak; belonging is a real human need. The power move is *awareness*: knowing
a feed is curated, that an ad has a goal, and that a peer's "in" opinion is one opinion.
Carry the **egalitarian** lens lightly but clearly: media often shows a narrow idea of a
"good" life (certain bodies, wealth, certain places) and quietly leaves everyone else out;
asking *who is left out?* and *who benefits?* is how learners stay clear-eyed. Keep it
hopeful: always end on agency — you can curate your own feed, choose your company, and
remember that feeds are edited, not real lives. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/philosophy.md` §4–5.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have mapped your many parts and seen them shift. Today you
   look at two outside forces that shape the picture you hold of yourself — **peers** and
   **media**.
2. **Meet the two mirrors (10 min).** Look at the poster. Your **self-image** is the
   picture you hold of yourself. Two mirrors keep reflecting into it. **Peers:** what
   friends value, compare, and say ("everyone has one"). **Media:** screens, ads, and
   feeds showing **curated highlight reels** — the best moments, often edited. Worked
   example: a feed of perfect photos can make you feel "less"; a friend's "that's not
   cool" can make you doubt what you like. Neither mirror is evil — but some are warped.
3. **Notice your own mirrors (15 min).** On your page, fill two columns: what *peers* make
   you notice about yourself, and what *media* makes you notice. Be gentle and honest —
   this is noticing, not judging. Check with a partner: did they notice something you
   didn't?
4. **Ask who is left out (10 min).** Pick one image or opinion from your columns. Ask the
   critical questions: **who is left out of this picture? who benefits from me feeling
   "not enough"?** Discuss one answer with your partner. Then write one thing you know is
   real about you that the mirror missed.
5. **Close (5 min).** You do not have to break the mirror — just notice it. A person who
   can read a warped mirror is free of it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide picture prompts (an ad, a group opinion) and sentence starters
  ("This makes me notice ___; the mirror might be leaving out ___").
- **Extension:** Write the egalitarian question in your own words and give one example of
  a group of people a certain kind of media image tends to leave out.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one peer influence and one media
  influence on self-image, and one thing the "mirror" leaves out?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their page and asks, "Did I notice *both* mirrors,
  and did I name something real the mirror missed?" and adds to it if not.

## Home connection

This week, when a feed or a friend's opinion makes you notice yourself, pause and ask:
what is this mirror leaving out? Share what you find with someone you trust.

## Resources

- Research on media and peer influence on self-image and body image (e.g., Common Sense
  Media's reports on social media and adolescent well-being; the social comparison work of
  Leon Festinger) supports the framing that curated, edited images can distort
  self-perception. For classroom SEL framing, see CASEL's self-awareness competency:
