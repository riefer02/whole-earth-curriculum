# Lesson 4 — Guessing the Feeling Behind a Behavior

## Summary

Learners practice reading the feeling behind a behavior the way a **fair detective**
would: a behavior is a **clue, not a label**. They guess *several* possible feelings
behind it, then — crucially — they **check** with the person instead of assuming. This
builds empathy and fairness: guessing generously and checking treats the other person as
the expert on their own feelings.

## Objectives

- Distinguish what a person feels from what a person shows, and guess the feeling behind
  a behavior. _(D01.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

A classmate slams a book and turns away. Is she **angry**? Maybe. Or maybe she is
**hurt** by a word she overheard, or **embarrassed** about a mistake, or simply tired
after a sleepless night. The behavior is the same; the feeling behind it could be many
things. If you *assume* you know, you might treat her wrong. If you *guess and then
check* — "hey, you seem off — everything okay?" — you treat her as the expert on her own
feelings. That is fairer, everywhere people live together.

## Materials

- Behavior detective poster
- Guess-then-check cards
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the behavior detective poster (a behavior → several possible feelings →
  a "check" step).
- Prepare guess-then-check scenario cards.
- Arrange a circle and space for paired role-play.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **guess, then check —
never assume**. This is the unit's clearest *ethics* moment: assuming a feeling can misjudge
and hurt a person; checking treats them with respect and fairness. Use **invented
scenarios or story characters only** — never real classmates — so no learner is analyzed or
labeled in front of peers. Emphasize that a behavior is a *clue*, not a verdict, and that
the person themselves is the best source. This aligns with the egalitarian lens: checking
is how we treat every person as their own expert. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding
difficult topics safely and `docs/contexts.md` on cultural power dynamics (in some settings,
asking directly may need to be gentle and private).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you saw that what a person shows can differ from what
   they feel. Today you become a **feeling detective**: you read the clue *and* you check
   your guess.
2. **Meet the detective's rule (10 min).** Look at the poster. A behavior — slamming a
   book, turning away — is a **clue**, not a label. A fair detective makes **more than one
   guess**: hurt? embarrassed? angry? tired? Then the detective **checks** — kindly asks or
   notices more — instead of deciding. Worked example: "She slammed the book. My guesses:
   hurt, embarrassed, or just having a rough morning. My check: 'You seem upset — want to
   talk, or do you need a minute?'"
3. **Practice guess-then-check (15 min).** With a partner, take two scenario cards. For
   each, name the behavior, offer **two or three** possible feelings behind it, and write
   or say a **kind check question**. Swap roles and notice: how does checking feel
   different from assuming?
4. **Guess vs assume (10 min).** Discuss: what is the difference between a guess (I think
   it might be this, let me check) and an assumption (I *know* it is this)? Why is
   checking fairer to the other person — and why is it also fairer to *you*?
5. **Close (5 min).** A clue is not a verdict. Guessing with more than one possibility and
   then checking is how we treat each person with respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank of feelings and pre-written check questions to choose
  from and read aloud.
- **Extension:** Write why checking is fairer to *both* people, and describe a time
  (real or invented) when a checked guess would have helped.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance):** In paired work, can the learner name a behavior, offer two
  or three possible feelings behind it, and give a kind check question?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their check question and asks, "Is this kind, and
  does it leave room for the person's real answer?" and revises if not.

## Home connection

This week, when someone's behavior puzzles you, try the detective's rule: make two guesses
about the feeling behind it, then check kindly instead of assuming.
