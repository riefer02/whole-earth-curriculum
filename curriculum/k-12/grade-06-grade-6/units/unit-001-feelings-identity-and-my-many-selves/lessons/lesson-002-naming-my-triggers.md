# Lesson 2 — Naming My Triggers

## Summary

Learners name what tends to **set their feelings off** — a **trigger**. They learn that a
trigger can be a sound, a time, a place, a word, or a tired body, and that **naming a
trigger works like a warning light**: it turns a feeling that surprises them into a
feeling they can prepare for. This deepens Lesson 1's pattern: the *trigger* is the
"situation" side of the repeating shape.

## Objectives

- Identify patterns in their own emotional responses and name what tends to trigger
  them. _(D01.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

A storm does not come from nowhere — there is a darkening sky, a drop in the air, a
first gust. The moment you learn to read those signs, you can close the window before
the rain. Feelings are like that. A crowded market might reliably make one person feel
**overwhelmed**; a raised voice might make another feel **tense**; being left out of a
group might make a third feel **hurt**. Those "first gusts" are **triggers** — and naming
them gives you a chance to act *before* the storm, not only after.

## Materials

- Trigger-to-feeling poster
- My trigger map page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the trigger-to-feeling poster (three triggers → the feelings they set
  off).
- Copy a "my trigger map" page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle and a quiet space for individual work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **a trigger is
information, and naming it is power — never an excuse and never a verdict**. Be careful
not to imply that a trigger "makes" a person behave badly; a trigger explains *a feeling*,
not a choice (the choice comes later, in Lesson 10). Invite only small, safe triggers;
never press a learner to name a trigger tied to trauma or a painful home situation, and
allow "I'd rather keep mine private." If a learner names something heavy, receive it with
calm care and help them name one *small* warning signal, not the whole story. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you found a pattern — a feeling that repeats. Today you
   name the part of the pattern that comes *first*: the **trigger**, the thing that tends
   to set the feeling off.
2. **Meet the trigger (10 min).** Look at the poster. A trigger is not the feeling — it is
   what comes *right before* it. Worked example: a crowded, noisy hallway → **overwhelmed**;
   a raised voice near me → **tense**; a friend walking past without saying hi → **hurt**.
   The trigger is the "before"; the feeling is the "after." A trigger can be a sound, a
   time of day, a place, a word, or even a tired, hungry body.
3. **Map your own triggers (15 min).** On your page, list two or three small, safe
   triggers of yours and the feeling each tends to set off. Which comes first — the
   trigger or the feeling? Check with a partner that you wrote them in the right order.
4. **Build a warning light (10 min).** For your clearest trigger, design your own early
   warning: what would you want to notice, say, or do the moment you sense that trigger
   coming? A breath? A step outside? A word to a friend? Write it or sketch it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Naming a trigger does not make you weak — it makes you *ready*. A
   warning light is a kindness you give yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide picture cards of common triggers to sort, and sentence starters
  ("When ___ happens, I tend to feel ___").
- **Extension:** Sort your triggers into *body* triggers (tired, hungry, too much screen)
  and *situation* triggers (a sound, a word, a time of day), and notice which kind is
  easier to plan for.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a trigger and the feeling it tends to
  set off, in the correct "before → after" order?
- **Self-check:** The learner reads their map back and asks, "Is this really the *before*,
  or is it the feeling itself?" and corrects the order if not.

## Home connection

Notice one trigger of your own at home this week — a time, a sound, a place — and name
what it tends to set off. Share it with someone if you like.
