# Lesson 3 — What I Feel vs What I Show

## Summary

Learners discover that what a person **shows on the outside** and what they **feel on the
inside** can be different — sometimes on purpose, sometimes not. They learn that people
manage their face for many good reasons (to be brave, to be kind, to fit in, to avoid
worrying others), that **both sides are real**, and that noticing the difference is a
skill for understanding themselves and others — without judging either side as "fake."

## Objectives

- Distinguish what a person feels from what a person shows, and guess the feeling behind
  a behavior. _(D01.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

A dancer walks on stage looking calm and bright, while inside her chest is hammering. A
friend smiles when you wave, though something inside them is heavy. An athlete at the
starting line looks still, while inside there is a storm. The outside is what the world
sees; the inside is what the person lives. Both are true at the same time. Learning to
tell them apart helps you read a room, care for a friend, and understand your own face
too — in a city, on a farm, or anywhere people are together.

## Materials

- Feel-vs-show poster
- Inside/outside page
- Pencils and crayons

## Preparation

- Print or draw the feel-vs-show poster (a smiling outside beside a worried inside).
- Copy an inside/outside page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for discussion.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **the outside and
the inside can differ, and both are real** — not that "hiding feelings is lying." Managing
one's face is a normal, often kind and necessary skill in every culture. Normalize it
without shame, and name that different families and cultures have different habits about
how much feeling to show — none "correct." At the same time, gently flag the boundary a
learner should know: managing a face for a good reason is different from *hiding something
they need help with*. Invite only small, safe examples; never push a learner to reveal a
hidden pain. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/art-style.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you named what sets a feeling off. Today you learn that
   the feeling a person *has* and the face a person *shows* can be two different things.
2. **Meet the two sides (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the outside, a smile. On the
   inside, worried. The symbol between them means "not the same." Why might a person do
   this? Worked example: to be **brave** before a test, to be **kind** so a friend doesn't
   worry, to **fit in**, or simply because the face hasn't caught up with the feeling yet.
   Managing our face is a real human skill — and it is not the same as being fake.
3. **Fill your own two sides (15 min).** Think of one small, safe moment this week when
   your outside and inside were different. On your page, draw or write what you *showed*
   (outside) and what you *felt* (inside), and one sentence about *why*. Share with a
   partner if you are comfortable.
4. **The question that matters (10 min).** Discuss together: when is managing your face
   helpful or kind, and when might it be a sign you need to talk to someone you trust?
   Write down one thing that helps you tell the difference.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your outside and inside are both real. Noticing both is how you
   understand yourself — and how you begin to read the people around you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a word bank of inside feelings and outside faces, and sentence
  starters ("I showed ___, but inside I felt ___, because ___").
- **Extension:** Write two moments — one where managing your face helped someone, and one
  where sharing the inside would have helped you more.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state an outside and a different inside,
  and give a reason for the difference?
- **Self-check:** The learner rereads their page and checks that they described *both*
  sides (not just one) and that the reason makes sense.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they showed one face while feeling another — and share
one small example of your own if you like.

## Resources

- The distinction between felt emotion and outward display, and the social reasons people
  manage their expression, is studied in the psychology of emotion and emotion regulation
  (see the work of Paul Ekman on facial expression and James Gross on emotion regulation).
  For classroom framing, see CASEL's self-awareness and social-awareness competencies:
