# Lesson 10 — Capstone: Why Some Have More Than They Need

## Summary

In this **capstone** lesson, learners explain — in age-appropriate terms — **why some people
have more than they need while others lack**, and frame it as a **fairness and systems
question**, never as a verdict on anyone's worth. They sort reasons (**where you were born, a
family's work and pay, illness, displacement, unfair rules**) as **circumstances and systems**,
and name **fair actions — sharing, cooperating, changing a rule** — revisiting the unit's
assertion, belonging, and cooperation skills to produce a **fairness plan**.

## Objectives

- Explain why some people have more than they need while others lack, in age-appropriate
  terms. _(D02.S4.06.02)_
- Revisit the unit: analyze inclusion and exclusion (D02.S3.06.01) and compare cooperation
  and competition (D02.S4.06.01) while planning a fair action.

## Connection

Two families live on the same street. One pantry overflows; the other is often empty. Is it
because one family is *better* and the other *worse*? No — that is the trap. The difference
comes from **systems and circumstances**: where you were born, what work your family could
find and what it paid, whether the land or savings were passed down, whether illness or war
or displacement struck. These are not choices, and they are not measures of anyone's **worth**.
Understanding this is not blaming the family with more or shaming the family with less — it is
seeing how shares get shaped, so that **fairness — sharing, cooperating, changing a rule** —
can make sure more people have **enough**.

## Materials

- Systems-not-worth poster
- Reason cards
- Fairness plan page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the systems-not-worth poster (three households, arrows to the reasons, "never
  a person's worth").
- Copy reason cards per team and a fairness plan page per learner.
- Arrange teams and, if possible, objects for the low-tech pile-making variant.

## Facilitator note

This is the unit **capstone**, written to the learner ("you"). Handle it with care — this is
the lesson where a learner could feel shamed for having less or blamed for having more, and
neither may ever happen. The idea to land is that **unequal shares come from systems and
circumstances, never from a person's worth** — and that **fairness is something people build
together, not something some people deserve and others don't**. Keep the analysis at the level
of *systems* (work and pay, land and inheritance, health and disability, war and displacement,
unfair rules), never at the level of individual character. Never ask learners to disclose their
own family's resources. Always connect the analysis to **agency and hope** — a concrete fair
action — so the lesson lands as empowerment, not despair (`docs/facilitation.md`; `docs/
development.md` 6–8 band). Model one worked example first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).
The distinction "how things are vs what people deserve" is the ethical core; hold it firmly
and kindly.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** For nine lessons you have practiced honest words, assertion, empathy,
   perspective-taking, belonging, and cooperation. Today you bring them to the unit's biggest
   question: **why do some people have more than they need while others lack?**
2. **Meet the question — and the trap (10 min).** Look at the poster. Three households, three
   different amounts. The tempting answer — "one family is better" — is the trap. The truer
   answer: **systems and circumstances** shape the shares. Read the arrows: where you were
   born, the work a family could find and its pay, land or savings passed down, illness or
   disability, war or displacement, unfair rules. None of these is a measure of **worth**.
3. **Sort circumstances from worth (10 min).** In your team, take the reason cards and sort
   each one: does it explain *how shares got shaped* (a circumstance or system) or *what a
   person deserves* (worth)? Every card should land on "circumstance/system" — that is the
   point. Worked example: "her family was displaced by war" → circumstance, not worth.
4. **Plan a fair action (15 min).** On your page, write: (a) one reason shares differ, (b)
   one way that is unfair, and (c) one thing people can do — **share**, **cooperate**, or
   **change a rule** — so more have enough. Use your unit skills: choose **assertive, honest
   words** when you propose; notice who is **left out** and open the circle; and ask which
   mode — **cooperation or fair competition** — serves the goal.
5. **Close (5 min).** Nobody's worth is measured by how much they have. Unequal shares are
   made by systems — and systems can be remade by people who act fairly, together. That is the
   whole unit, in one question and one act.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two pre-sorted reasons and one fair action to choose from, and ask the
  learner to say in one sentence why the reason is *circumstance*, not *worth*.
- **Extension:** Distinguish *circumstance* (how things are) from *worth* (what a person
  deserves), and argue in one sentence why confusing them is the root of unfairness.

## Assessment

- **Summative (performance/portfolio):** Can the learner explain unequal shares through
  systems and circumstances (never worth), and propose one fair action — sharing, cooperating,
  or changing a rule — so more have enough?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I explain the *systems*, not blame or shame any
  person? Did I end with a real fair action, not just a problem?"

## Home connection

Talk with someone at home about one fair thing your family or neighbourhood could do so more
people have enough — and, if you can, do one small part of it.

## Resources

- Economic inequality is driven largely by structural factors — where one is born, access to
  work and pay, inherited wealth, health, and the effects of conflict — rather than individual
  worth (widely documented; e.g., World Bank and UN data on inequality and poverty). The
  statement that *no person's worth is measured by their resources* is a values commitment of
  this curriculum, not a scientific claim. For a developmentally careful classroom framing of
  fairness and inequality, see CASEL social awareness and the UNESCO/UN "equity" materials:
