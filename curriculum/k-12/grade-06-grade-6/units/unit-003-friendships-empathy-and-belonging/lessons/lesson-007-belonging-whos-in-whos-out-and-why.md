# Lesson 7 — Belonging: Who's In, Who's Out, and Why

## Summary

Learners analyze how **belonging can include some people and exclude others, and why**. They
meet the idea of **in-group and out-group**, examine invented scenes of everyday exclusion —
a saved seat, a repeated pick, a left-out chat — and ask two questions: **why does it happen,
and what would open the circle?** They produce an **inclusion analysis**.

## Objectives

- Analyze how belonging can include some people and exclude others, and why that happens.
  _(D02.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

A lunch table has one empty seat, and someone keeps saving it "for a friend" who never
comes. A game is picked, and the same players are always chosen first, the same faces left
last. No one is shouting "you can't sit here" — and yet the circle quietly closes. Belonging
isn't only about loud walls; it is often about **quiet ones**: habits, unspoken rules, and
who we notice and who we don't. When we ask *why* someone is out, we can see the barrier —
and when we see it, we can open the circle. This is true of a classroom, a neighbourhood, and
every community humans build, everywhere.

## Materials

- Circle poster
- Belonging scenario cards
- Inclusion analysis page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the circle poster (some in, one out, the "why" and "what would open it").
- Copy scenario cards per pair and an inclusion analysis page per learner.
- Arrange pairs and, if possible, space for a physical circle.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **exclusion is often
quiet and unintentional** — habits, unspoken rules, and unnoticed absence — and that the first
step to inclusion is *noticing and asking why*. Use **invented scenarios**, never real
classmates, so no learner is named as the excluder or the excluded. This lesson is the
egalitarian heart of the unit: every person has an equal claim to belonging, and barriers are
usually built by *systems of habit*, not by "bad people." Hold both truths — exclusion hurts,
and it is usually not intended — so the analysis stays curious rather than blaming. If a
learner raises a real, painful exclusion, hold it privately and gently. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you felt *in* a group — and a time you felt *out*.
   What made the difference? Today you analyze **belonging**: who is in, who is out, and why.
2. **Meet the circle (10 min).** Look at the poster. Some figures are inside the circle; one
   is outside, looking in. Every group has an **inside** and an **outside** — an in-group and
   an out-group. The question is never "is there a circle?" but "**who is outside it, and
   why?**" Worked example: a lunch table with one empty seat saved "for a friend" who never
   comes. Who is out? The new learner with nowhere to sit. Why? An unspoken habit, not a
   spoken rule. What would open it? "Come sit with us — there's room."
3. **Learn the two questions (5 min).** Whenever you notice an outside, ask two things:
   **"Why are they out?"** (habit? a rule? a difference? fear?) and **"What would open the
   circle?"** (a word, a gesture, a change in the rule). The second question turns noticing
   into action.
4. **Analyze scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, take scenario cards — a team that always
   picks the same players, a group chat that leaves one person out, a game with a rule no one
   explained. For each, record on your page: who is in, who is out, why it happens, and one
   thing that could open the circle. Swap and try a second.
5. **Close (5 min).** Belonging is not automatic; it is *built* — or quietly *blocked* — by
   small habits. You now have two questions that can open any circle: *why are they out?* and
   *what would let them in?* Use them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a scenario with the in-group and out-group already labeled, and ask
  the learner to choose one opening move from a short list.
- **Extension:** Analyze how *unspoken rules* (who sits where, who gets picked first) exclude
  without anyone meaning to, and propose one counter-rule your own group could adopt.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self/portfolio):** Can the learner name who is in and who is out in a scene,
  give a reason *why* the exclusion happens, and propose one concrete way to open the circle?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I look for the *quiet* barrier, not just the loud
  one? Did I move from noticing to one real opening?"

## Home connection

Notice one quiet "circle" in your own life this week — a table, a team, a group chat — and
ask yourself who might be just outside it, and one way you could open it.
