# Lesson 6 — Why Did They Act That Way? Perspective-Taking

## Summary

Learners use **perspective-taking** to understand why a peer acted in a way that upset
them. They learn that a first guess is only **one angle**, practice generating **two or three
other possible reasons** behind an act, and write a **perspective note** — holding both
**understanding and accountability**, not one instead of the other.

## Objectives

- Use perspective-taking to understand why a peer acted in a way that upset them.
  _(D02.S2.06.02)_

## Connection

Your friend walked right past you without a word, and it stung. Your first thought: *they're
mad at me*. But what else might be true? Maybe they just got bad news, maybe they were lost
in worry, maybe they didn't even see you. The first guess is **one angle on a scene that has
many**. Stepping into someone else's shoes does not erase the hurt or excuse the act — it
*adds* the missing angles so you can respond to the whole picture, not just the part you
saw. That is perspective-taking, and it is how hurt becomes understanding, in a hallway or a
farmyard or a crowded bus anywhere in the world.

## Materials

- Two-angles poster
- Perspective scenario cards
- Perspective note page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the two-angles poster (one scene, two angles, "what else might be true?").
- Copy scenario cards per pair and a perspective note page per learner.
- Arrange pairs and space to move around a marked spot.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **perspective-taking
adds angles, it does not excuse harm** — understanding why and holding someone accountable
are both possible at once. This protects learners from two traps: assuming the worst about a
peer, and excusing real hurt with "they must have had a reason." Use **invented scenarios
only**; never analyze a real classmate's behavior in front of peers. Model the worked
example fully; this is a skill (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). This lesson builds
directly on Grade 5's "check my guess about feelings" and the unit's earlier lessons — make
the connection explicit so the spiral is felt. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time a peer's action upset you. What was your first guess
   about *why*? Today you learn to add the other angles.
2. **Meet two angles (10 min).** Look at the poster. From the front, a figure storms off —
   you see *angry*. From behind, you see they are carrying a heavy, hidden worry. Same act,
   two angles. The question that opens understanding: "**what else might be true?**" Worked
   example: "She snapped at me. My first guess: she's mad at me. Other possible reasons: she
   just failed a test, her pet is sick, she didn't sleep. Understanding these doesn't make
   snapping okay — but it changes how I respond."
3. **Learn the move (5 min).** The move has three steps: **notice the act and your feeling**,
   then **ask "what else might be true?"** and list two or three reasons, then **decide how
   to respond** to the whole picture, not just the first guess.
4. **Practice on scenarios (15 min).** With a partner, take scenario cards — a peer ignored
   you, said something sharp, took the last of something you needed. Do the three steps
   together, writing on your note page: the act, your first guess, two or three other possible
   reasons, and one line about what you understand now. Swap and try a second.
5. **Close (5 min).** Understanding why does not erase the hurt or excuse the act — it
   *widens* your view so you can respond fairly. When you feel stung, pause and ask: "What
   else might be true?"

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a scene with three possible reasons already written, and ask the
  learner to choose the one that best explains the act and say how it changes their response.
- **Extension:** Distinguish a *reason* (explains) from an *excuse* (lets harm continue), and
  write one sentence holding both: "I understand why, and it still wasn't okay that…"

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/portfolio):** Can the learner name the act, move past a first
  guess to two or three other possible reasons, and describe a fairer response — holding both
  understanding and accountability?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I add angles instead of stopping at my first guess?
  Did I keep both understanding *and* the truth that hurt still matters?"

## Home connection

Next time someone's action stings, pause and ask "what else might be true?" before you
respond — and name one other angle out loud or in writing.
