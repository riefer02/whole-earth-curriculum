# Lesson 4 — Choosing Assertion in Real Situations

## Summary

Learners practice *choosing* assertion: they learn a **three-part formula** — **say the need,
respect the other person, propose a fair next step** — and rehearse it on real situations,
including **making a request** and **saying no**. They name the **passive and aggressive traps**
they are avoiding and record one assertion, preparing to use it in the week ahead.

## Objectives

- Choose assertion in real situations. _(D02.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

A friend asks you to copy your answers in a test, or to join a dare you do not like. Saying
"whatever, fine" swallows your own limit; snapping "leave me alone!" breaks the friendship.
The **assertive** middle sounds like this: "I'm not comfortable with that, and I still want
to be your friend — so let's do something else after." Assertion is a **choice** you make in
the moment — and like any skill, it gets stronger with practice. In a schoolyard or a
workshop or a fishing boat, saying your need *and* honoring the other person's is how people
stay in relationship without losing themselves.

## Materials

- Assertion formula poster
- Situation cards
- Assertion record page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the assertion formula poster (say the need, respect the other, propose a fair
  next step) with a worked example.
- Copy situation cards per pair and an assertion record page per learner.
- Arrange pairs and space to stand and speak.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **assertion is a
choice, not a personality** — and it is built by rehearsal, not willpower. The three-part
formula keeps both people's needs on the table, which is the egalitarian heart of the skill.
Carry forward Lesson 3's caution: how a person states a need "assertively" looks different across
cultures — in some, indirect speech, deference, or quietness is the respectful way, not passivity.
Honor each learner's own cultural style; the skill is that needs get honored within the
relationship, not that everyone must speak in one "direct" register. Model a full worked example,
then do guided practice before independent rehearsal; this is a
skill (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Give special care to **refusals**: saying no to a
friend can feel like betrayal to an 11-year-old, so frame "no" as protecting the friendship,
not ending it. Use invented situations; never script a real classmate into a scenario. If a
learner's situation involves real pressure or harm, connect them to a trusted adult. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last time you met the three voices. Today you practice **choosing the
   assertive one** in real situations — including the hard ones, like saying no.
2. **Meet the formula (10 min).** Look at the poster. Assertion has three parts: **say the
   need** ("I feel ___ / I need ___"), **respect the other** ("I know you want ___ too"),
   and **propose a fair next step** ("so let's ___"). Worked example: "I need to finish this
   before I can play. I know you're ready to go. So give me ten minutes, then I'll join you."
3. **Learn the refusal (5 min).** Saying no is a kind of assertion. The frame: "**I'm not
   comfortable with that, and I still want to be friends — so let's ___.**" Practice it once
   aloud, calm and upright, not apologizing and not attacking.
4. **Rehearse real situations (15 min).** With a partner, take situation cards: asking to
   borrow something, asking for quiet, refusing an unfair dare, saying no to a plan. Name the
   **passive trap** (giving in, going silent) and the **aggressive trap** (blaming, snapping),
   then deliver your three-part assertion. Partner gives kind feedback: was it calm, honest,
   and fair to both? Swap and try a second situation. Record one on your page.
5. **Close (5 min).** Assertion is a muscle: the more you use it, the readier it is. Choose
   it once this week — a request or a kind no — and notice how it feels to hold both people's
   needs at once.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three-part frame with blanks and a bank of fair next steps to
  choose from; rehearse one situation as a script first.
- **Extension:** Add a *boundary* (a limit you will repeat if needed) to the refusal, and
  explain how tone — calm, not apologetic or angry — keeps "no" assertive rather than
  passive or aggressive.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/peer):** Can the learner state a need, acknowledge the other
  person's need, and propose a fair next step — in a request and in a refusal — without
  slipping into passivity or aggression?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I say my need, respect theirs, and propose a fair
  next step? Would a friend hear this as fair, not bossy and not weak?"

## Home connection

Use the three-part assertion once at home this week — a request or a kind refusal — and tell
someone how it went.
