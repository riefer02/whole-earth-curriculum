# Lesson 9 — Cooperation vs Competition: When Each Helps or Harms

## Summary

Learners compare **cooperation and competition** and explain **when each helps or harms a
group**. They meet the key distinction — a **shared goal** calls for cooperation; a **contest
of skill** can use fair competition — and analyze situations to choose the mode that serves
the **common good**, producing a cooperation-versus-competition note.

## Objectives

- Compare cooperation and competition and explain when each helps or harms a group.
  _(D02.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

A boat needs every pair of hands rowing together — **cooperation**, because the goal is
shared. A footrace has one finish line and many runners — **competition**, because the test
is who is fastest. Neither is good or bad by itself; each is a *tool*, and a tool helps when
it matches the task. Competition helps when it is fair and joyful and lifts everyone's play;
it harms when winning becomes the only thing that matters. Cooperation helps when a goal is
shared; it can stall when people hide their own best ideas. The skill is *choosing* the right
mode — in a crew, a classroom, a market, or a village sharing a harvest.

## Materials

- Cooperation vs competition poster
- Situation cards
- Cooperation-versus-competition note page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the cooperation-vs-competition poster (rowing together vs racing to a line).
- Copy situation cards per pair and a note page per learner.
- Arrange pairs and, if possible, space for a shared task and a short race.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **neither mode is
good or bad in itself — each is a tool that helps or harms depending on the goal and the
spirit**. The curriculum's stance (`docs/philosophy.md` §8) values cooperation as the default
for learning, while honoring fair play as a real and joyful kind of competition. Avoid
moralizing: don't cast competition as villain and cooperation as saint. Instead, ask the
*functional* question — what is the goal, and which mode serves it and every member of the
group? This is a concept with a strong worked-example need (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006); model the choice explicitly. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you worked *with* others toward one goal — and a time
   you *raced* against them. Today you compare the two and learn when each **helps** or
   **harms**.
2. **Meet the two modes (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Cooperation:** figures row one boat
   together — the goal is *shared*, so it helps. It can harm if people go quiet and hide good
   ideas. **Competition:** figures race to one line — it helps when it is *fair play* that
   lifts everyone; it harms when winning becomes everything and others are trampled or
   left out. Worked example: a group project (shared goal) → cooperate. A spelling match
   (test of skill, everyone wants to play) → compete, fairly.
3. **Learn the choosing question (5 min).** Before any task, ask: "**Is the goal shared, or
   is it a test of who is best?**" Shared → lean to cooperation. A fair contest → competition
   is fine, with fair play. And always ask: "**Does this mode help every member of the group,
   or only the winner?**"
4. **Sort situations (15 min).** With a partner, take situation cards — a group project, a
   sports match, a shared harvest, a race for one prize. For each, decide which mode fits, and
   write on your note page: the situation, the mode, when each would help, and when each would
   harm. Swap and try a second.
5. **Close (5 min).** Cooperation and competition are two tools in one box. The wise person
   picks the tool that fits the task and lifts the whole group — and keeps fair play in both.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two clear situations (one shared goal, one fair contest) with the mode
  already chosen, and ask the learner to say *why* each fits.
- **Extension:** Analyze a *zero-sum* situation (one prize, one winner) against a *common-good*
  one (a shared goal), and argue in one sentence when competition stays fair and when it turns
  harmful.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self/portfolio):** Can the learner name the two modes, choose the fitting one
  for a situation, and explain when each helps and when each harms the group?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I choose the mode that fits the goal *and* serves
  every member — and can I say when each mode would help and when it would harm?"

## Home connection

Notice a moment of cooperation and a moment of competition at home or in your neighbourhood
this week, and ask quietly which mode fit and whether it helped or harmed the group.
