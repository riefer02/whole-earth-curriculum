# Lesson 1 — A Friendship Changes

## Summary

Learners open the unit by **normalizing that friendship changes as people grow**. They
meet the idea that a friendship changing — growing closer, drifting apart, or shifting
roles — is **not a failure**, and practice naming a change and choosing a **healthy way to
navigate it**. They produce a **friendship-change reflection** as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Name a change in a friendship and choose a healthy way to navigate it. _(D02.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Two friends walked home together every single day. Then one joined a music group that
meets after school, and the walks stopped. The other felt left behind — *was our friendship
over?* Not over: **changing**. People grow, interests shift, families move, and friendships
bend with them. The question is not "how do I keep it exactly the same?" but "how do I
navigate the change kindly — for my friend and for me?" That is a skill you can learn,
everywhere people live side by side, in a village, a city, or a new school far from home.

## Materials

- Friendship-change poster
- Friendship weather card
- Reflection page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the friendship-change poster (a winding path, a bend, "change is normal").
- Copy a friendship weather card and a reflection page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle and pairs for sharing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **friendship change
is normal, not a failure** — and that "navigating" means naming the change and choosing a
next step, not "winning" the friendship back. Peers matter enormously at this age
(`docs/development.md`, 6–8 band), so loss and change feel big; honor that without
dramatizing it. Two traps to avoid: framing the person who drifts away as a villain, and
framing the person left behind as pitiable. Both people are simply **growing**, and both can
act with care. Allow invented or story examples for anyone who prefers not to share their own
life. This is a skill (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006), so model one worked example before
independent reflection. See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding feelings safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a friendship that changed — maybe you grew apart, or one of
   you moved, or you became closer. Notice the feeling. Today you learn that **friendship
   change is normal**, and how to **navigate** it.
2. **Meet the path (10 min).** Look at the poster. A friendship is a path with bends, not a
   straight line that never turns. At a bend, people pause. That pause is not the end — it is
   a chance to choose. Worked example: "My friend joined music group and our walks stopped. I
   felt left out. Healthy choices: tell her I miss our walks, ask to hang out another time, or
   make room for her new interest *and* keep our friendship in a new shape."
3. **Meet four kinds of change (10 min).** Read the friendship weather card: **growing
   closer**, **growing apart**, **shifting roles** (one becomes a leader, one a helper), and a
   **rupture** (a hurt that needs repair). For each, name one feeling it brings and one healthy
   thing you could do. Which kind is the change you thought of in step 1?
4. **Reflect (15 min).** On your reflection page, write or draw: (a) a friendship that
   changed (real or invented), (b) what you felt, and (c) one healthy thing you can do now.
   Share one part with a partner if you want to.
5. **Close (5 min).** Friendships change because people grow — and growing is good, even
   when it stings. The skill is not to freeze a friendship but to **navigate** it with
   honesty and care, for both people.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a sentence frame ("My friendship changed when ___. I felt ___. One
  thing I can do is ___.") and a short list of healthy choices to pick from.
- **Extension:** Distinguish *drift* from *rupture*, and write a one-step plan for each
  (a gentle reconnection for drift; a repair conversation for rupture).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self/portfolio):** Can the learner name a friendship change, a feeling that
  came with it, and one healthy next step?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I treat the change as normal rather than a
  failure, and did I choose a step that is kind to both people?"

## Home connection

Think of one friendship that has changed in your life, and tell someone at home one
healthy thing you could do about it — even if it is simply to accept the change.
