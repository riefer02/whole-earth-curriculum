# Lesson 3 — Passive, Aggressive, Assertive: Three Voices

## Summary

Learners meet the three communication voices — **passive, aggressive, and assertive** — and
learn to tell them apart by **words, tone, and body**. They sort real-sounding lines into the
three voices and record what each voice **sounds like, looks like, and does to a
relationship**, preparing them to *choose* assertion in the next lesson.

## Objectives

- Distinguish assertive, passive, and aggressive communication. _(D02.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Three people want the same thing: a little quiet to finish their work. One says, "Whatever
you want," and swallows the need — that is the **passive** voice. Another shouts, "Be quiet,
now!" and tramples yours — that is the **aggressive** voice. The third says, "I need quiet to
finish — can we talk after?" — that is the **assertive** voice: it states the need *and*
respects yours. Same situation, three voices, three very different friendships. Learning to
hear the difference is the first step toward choosing the one that builds, everywhere people
share space — a schoolroom, a market stall, a shared kitchen.

## Materials

- Three-voices poster
- Three-voices sorting cards
- Voice chart page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-voices poster (hunched/passive, pointing/aggressive,
  upright/assertive) with a sample line for each.
- Copy a set of sorting cards per pair and a voice chart page per learner.
- Arrange pairs and space to stand in three postures.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **assertion is the
voice that holds both people's dignity** — it states the need without trampling others
(aggression) or erasing yourself (passivity). Model each voice with tone and body, not just
words; the body carries the meaning and many learners read it faster than text. Be careful
not to mock the passive or aggressive voices or the people who use them — everyone uses all
three sometimes, and each voice is a *choice*, not a *character*. A note for global
classrooms: the three-voices model comes from a Western, individually-spoken tradition, and
what counts as *respectful* differs across cultures. In many cultures — across East Asia, much
of Africa, Indigenous communities, and elsewhere — indirect speech, quietness, deference to
elders, and saving face are the respectful ways to keep a relationship, and they are not
"passivity." Teach the three voices as a way to notice whether each person's needs get honored
*within* a relationship, never as a ranking of any culture's way of speaking as better or
worse. This is a foundational
skill, so use explicit instruction with worked examples before independent sorting
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). The egalitarian lens: assertion treats the other person
as an equal whose needs count; aggression and passivity both fail that test. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time you wanted something from another person — quiet, a
   turn, a favor. How did you ask? Today you meet the **three voices** we all can use.
2. **Meet the three voices (10 min).** Look at the poster. **Passive:** hunched, silent,
   "whatever you want" — the need gets swallowed. **Aggressive:** pointed, loud, "do it my way
   or else" — the other person gets trampled. **Assertive:** upright, calm, "I need quiet to
   finish — can we talk after?" — the need is stated *and* the other person is respected.
3. **Hear the difference (10 min).** Say the same line three ways aloud, using posture and
   tone: "I'd like a turn with the tool." Hunched and whispered; pointed and snapped; upright
   and calm. Notice how your body changes with each voice — and how the other person would
   feel hearing each one.
4. **Sort the voices (15 min).** With a partner, take the sorting cards and place each line
   in one of the three columns. For each, ask: does it swallow the need (passive), trample the
   other person (aggressive), or state the need and respect the person (assertive)? Record on
   your voice chart what each voice sounds like, looks like, and does to a relationship.
5. **Close (5 min).** Everyone uses all three voices sometimes — the question is which one you
   *choose* in the moment that matters. Tomorrow, you learn to choose assertion.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide three labeled boxes and a short list of lines to read and sort, with
  the body posture for each voice modeled first.
- **Extension:** Explain *why* passivity and aggression fail over time (silence breeds
  resentment; force breeds fear) while assertion builds trust, and give a real-life example.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner sort spoken lines into passive,
  aggressive, and assertive by words, tone, and body, and say what each voice does to a
  relationship?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I hear and see the difference? Which voice states the
  need *and* respects the other person, and why is it called assertive?"

## Home connection

Listen for the three voices today — in your home, on a show, in a game. Name quietly to
yourself which voice each speaker used.
