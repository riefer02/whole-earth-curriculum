# Lesson 12 — Soil Supports Life

## Summary

Learners close the unit at the place where every system meets: **soil**. They learn
that soil is made of **minerals** from broken-down rock (the rock cycle) and **humus**
from decayed life (cells and decomposers), that it cycles water and nutrients, and
that it feeds the plants that capture the Sun's energy. They revisit the unit's
through-line — collect data, check a claim, and separate **evidence** from
**inference** — to show the whole unit at work in one handful of earth.

## Objectives

- Explain how the rock cycle recycles Earth's materials and how soil supports life.
  _(D06.S4.06.02)_
- Revisit how matter cycles through an ecosystem _(D06.S2.06.02)_, how energy changes
  form _(D06.S3.06.01)_, and how data support or revise a claim _(D06.S1.06.01)_ to
  connect soil to the living, physical, and inquiry threads of this unit.

## Connection

Everything you have ever eaten began, in some way, in soil. The grain, the fruit, the
grass that fed the animal — all of it grew from a thin, living layer of earth that is
part broken rock and part the remains of old life. A farmer's field, a mountain
meadow, a river delta, a rooftop garden: all depend on the same handful of dark,
living ground. And people have been reading that ground for thousands of years — soil-
keepers who know a field by its color, its feel, and its smell, from the terraced
slopes of the Andes to Sahelian fields to the dark forest-garden earth of the Amazon.
That long observation is a science in its own right, held beside — never beneath — the
soil science you learn today. Today you learn what soil is made of, and why it is the
quiet engine under every meal you will ever have.

- Print or draw the soil diagram and a reflection page per learner.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This capstone has two jobs: teach the last standard
(rock cycle recycles materials; **soil supports life**) and close the unit's
through-line (data → claim → support/revise → evidence vs. inference). Two ideas to
land: **soil = minerals (broken rock) + humus (decayed life)**, and **soil is the
place rock, life, water, and energy meet**. Name the cross-links explicitly: the rock
cycle makes the mineral part (Lesson 11); cells and decomposers make the humus and
cycle nutrients (Lessons 5–6); energy flows from Sun to plant (Lessons 7–8); and a
soil investigation uses tables, graphs, and claims (Lessons 1–4). The ethics and
egalitarian lenses: healthy soil feeds everyone, and caring for soil is caring for
people — a value we hold, stated alongside the evidence. Keep "how do we know" alive:
we can *see* soil's parts and *test* water-holding, but we *infer* the deep rock
origins and the slow work of decomposers. Connect to agency — learners can protect
soil. See [`docs/facilitation.md`](../../../../../../docs/facilitation.md).

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Quick unit review: how do we organize data and
   check a claim? (tables, graphs, support or revise — Lessons 1–2). What is the unit
   of life? (the cell — Lesson 5). What flows and what cycles in an ecosystem?
   (energy flows; matter cycles — Lesson 6). What recycles rock? (the rock cycle —
   Lesson 11). Today's last question: what does all of that make possible?
2. **Meet soil (10 min).** Look at the diagram. **Soil** is a mix of **minerals** —
   broken-down rock from the rock cycle — and **humus** — the decayed remains of
   living things, broken down by **decomposers**. Together they hold water, air, and
   nutrients, and they are home to countless living things. Soil is where the rock
   cycle meets the living world.
3. **Look closely (10 min).** With a magnifier, examine your soil sample. Sort what
   you see into "from rock" (gritty grains, sand) and "from life" (dark bits, roots,
   tiny creatures). Write each in the right column.
4. **Soil supports life (15 min).** Trace the connections: rock weathers into mineral
   grains (rock cycle); dead plants and animals decay into humus (decomposers); soil
   holds water and nutrients that cycle (matter); plants grow in it and capture the
   Sun's energy (energy); and everything that eats depends on those plants. Draw the
   loop on your page.
5. **Separate evidence from inference (10 min).** Sort these: "I saw mineral grains
   in the soil" (evidence — you looked); "this soil took thousands of years to form
   from rock" (inference — you reason it); "we should care for soil so it can feed
   everyone" (a value — a choice). Which is which?
6. **Close (5 min).** One handful of soil is the whole unit in miniature: rock
   recycled, life decomposed, water and nutrients cycling, the Sun's energy caught by
   plants — and you, the scientist, holding it with open eyes, evidence, and care.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort soil parts into two labeled piles ("from rock" | "from life");
  use a two-word record and the diagram as reference.
- **Extension:** Run a small soil investigation (which soil holds water best?) with a
  data table, a bar graph, and a claim you support or revise.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The unit reflection page, showing what soil is made of,
  what it supports, the rock-and-life loop, and a "how do we know" sentence that
  separates evidence from inference.
- **Observation:** Can the learner connect rock cycle + decomposers + water/nutrient
  cycling + plant energy to explain how soil supports life?

## Home connection

Find a patch of soil near home and look closely. Tell someone what part came from
rock, what part came from life, and one thing you could do to protect it.
