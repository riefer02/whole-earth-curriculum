# Lesson 11 — The Rock Cycle Recycles Earth's Materials

## Summary

Learners meet the **rock cycle**: rock is made, broken down, and made again in a loop
that never uses Earth's materials up. They learn the three rock types — **igneous**
(made by cooling), **sedimentary** (made by pressing layers), and **metamorphic**
(changed by heat and pressure) — and the processes that turn one into another, then
trace one rock's long journey around the cycle.

## Objectives

- Explain how the rock cycle recycles Earth's materials. _(D06.S4.06.02)_

## Connection

The sand on a beach was once part of a mountain. The mountain, long before that, was
once molten rock, and the grains of sand may one day be pressed into new stone and
lifted again. Rock is not a thing that is used once and gone; it is **recycled**,
broken down and rebuilt over times so long we cannot watch them. Today you learn the
cycle that makes every mountain, beach, and pebble part of one slow, endless story.

## Materials

- Rock-cycle diagram
- Rock-type cards
- Cycle page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the rock-cycle diagram, cards, and cycle page.
- Recall Grade 5: weathering, erosion, and the slow changes to Earth's surface.
- Have clay, sand, and stones ready if using the hand model.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land: **rock is recycled** — made,
broken down, and made again, in a loop that has run for billions of years. Three rock
types, each tied to a process: **igneous** (cooled from magma or lava), **sedimentary**
(pressed from layers of sediment), **metamorphic** (changed by heat and pressure). The
cycle is driven by Earth's internal heat (melting) and surface processes (weathering,
erosion, deposition). Model the worked example with the clay/sand model and trace one
rock around the diagram aloud. The deep-time scale is evidence-based (radiometric
dating of rocks); the sense of wonder — that the ground under us is ancient and
recycled — can seed an environmental reflection about caring for what is never truly
"used up," but name it as a value. Keep the "how do we know" line: we cannot watch a
cycle that takes millions of years, so we *infer* it from the rocks we can see and
measure. See [`docs/facilitation.md`](../../../../../../docs/facilitation.md).

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Hold a pebble or a grain of sand. How old is it, and where has
   it been? Write a guess: could it have once been part of a mountain?
2. **Meet the cycle (10 min).** Look at the diagram. The **rock cycle** is a loop.
   **Magma** (melted rock) cools into **igneous** rock. Weathering and erosion break
   rock into **sediment**, which presses into **sedimentary** rock. Heat and pressure
   change rock into **metamorphic** rock, which can melt back into magma — and the
   loop begins again. Point to each step and say what happens.
3. **Arrange the cycle (10 min).** With your cards, lay out the three rock types and
   the processes between them in a circle: cooling, weathering/erosion, pressing,
   heat/pressure, melting. Check each arrow with a partner: does this process turn
   that rock into the next one?
4. **Trace one rock (15 min).** On your cycle page, draw the loop and trace one
   rock's journey: born from cooling magma, weathered into sand, pressed into
   sandstone, folded by pressure, and melted to begin again. Label each rock type as
   you go.
5. **Close (5 min).** Nothing about rock is used up and thrown away. It is broken
   down and rebuilt, over and over — the oldest recycling system on Earth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort rock samples or cards into the three types first; trace the cycle
  with a moving token and say each change aloud.
- **Extension:** Explain how one rock could become all three types over deep time,
  naming the process that drives each change (cooling, pressing, heat/pressure).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three rock types, the process
  that makes each, and state that the cycle recycles Earth's materials?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I start anywhere in the cycle and trace a
  rock all the way around, naming each rock type and change?"

## Home connection

Find a rock near home and decide which type it might be and how it likely formed.
Tell someone the cycle that rock has been part of — and will rejoin.
