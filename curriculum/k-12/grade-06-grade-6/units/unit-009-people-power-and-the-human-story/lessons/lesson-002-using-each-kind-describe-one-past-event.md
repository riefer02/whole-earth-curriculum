# Lesson 2 — Using Each Kind: Describe One Past Event

## Summary

Learners use a **primary source** and a **secondary source** together to describe one past
event: the Indian Ocean voyages of **Zheng He (1405–1433)**. A worked example pairs an
eyewitness account (Ma Huan) with a modern history book, then learners choose an event and
write a **two-source note** — this unit's first portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Use a primary source and a secondary source, each, to describe one past event.
  _(D08.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

When you tell about something that happened — a storm, a festival, an accident — you can
tell it two ways: as someone who **saw it**, or as someone who **heard about it and looked
into it**. Both are useful, and they sound different. A historian does the same thing: a
**primary source** is the voice of someone who was there; a **secondary source** is the
voice of someone who studied it later. Today you use both, together, to describe one real
event from the past.

## Materials

- Zheng He two-source poster
- Two-source event cards (three events)
- Describe-with-two-sources note

## Preparation

- Print or draw the Zheng He two-source poster.
- Copy the three event cards and a two-source note for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for the worked example and a flat space for writing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **describing one past
event using both kinds of source, and noticing what each adds**. Model the Zheng He example
fully, then let learners choose an event and write. Guard against "the primary source is
true, the secondary is not": both can be accurate or mistaken; the point is that they come
from different places and do different work. The three event cards are deliberately global —
Great Zimbabwe (Southern Africa), the 1324 pilgrimage of Mansa Musa (West Africa), and the
1857 rebellion in India (South Asia) — so no single people's story is the default. This
lesson produces the unit's **primary-versus-secondary source note** portfolio artifact. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall from last lesson: a **primary source** is made at the time by
   someone who was there; a **secondary source** is made later by someone who studied the
   evidence. Today you put both to work on one event.
2. **Worked example: Zheng He (15 min).** Read the poster. The event is the **voyages of
   Zheng He**, a Chinese admiral who led huge fleets across the **Indian Ocean** between
   1405 and 1433 — to Southeast Asia, South Asia, Arabia, and the coast of East Africa. The
   **primary source** is the account of **Ma Huan**, an interpreter who *sailed with the
   fleet* and wrote down what he saw — the people, the ports, the animals. The **secondary
   source** is a modern book, *The Treasure Fleets*, written by a historian centuries later
   who studied Ma Huan's writing and other evidence to explain the voyages. Notice what each
   adds: Ma Huan gives the *feel* of being there; the modern book gives the *big picture*.
3. **Choose an event (5 min).** Pick one of the three event cards. Each card gives one
   primary source and one secondary source about a real past event.
4. **Write your two-source note (15 min).** On your note, answer three prompts: **What was
   the event?** **What does the primary source tell you?** **What does the secondary source
   add?** Use the words of the sources, and note how the two voices differ.
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, read one of your prompts aloud. Does your
   partner agree that you have used one primary *and* one secondary source?
6. **Close (5 min).** A single past event can be described many ways. When you use both
   kinds of source, you get closer to a full picture. Keep this note — it is the first page
   of your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("The event was…; the primary source says…; the
  secondary source adds…") and scribe where needed; use only the Zheng He example.
- **Extension:** Add a third line to your note: "What could the primary source show that the
  secondary source could not — and the other way around?"

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner describe one past event using a
  primary source and a secondary source, and name what each contributes?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I use one primary and one secondary source, and can
  I say what each one added?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home about Zheng He's voyages — first as Ma Huan saw them, then as a modern
historian explains them — and ask which version felt different and why.

## Resources

- The voyages of Zheng He (1405–1433), in which Chinese fleets sailed the Indian Ocean as
  far as East Africa, are documented in primary and secondary sources; Ma Huan's eyewitness
  account, *Yingya Shenglan*, is translated and studied by historians (see, e.g., the work
  of J.V.G. Mills and later scholarship). Overviews appear in the UNESCO "Maritime Silk Road"
  project (<https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-road>) and the British Library
  (<https://www.bl.uk/>). The 1324 pilgrimage of Mansa Musa is described in the writings of
  the contemporary Egyptian scholar al-Umari and later histories (see the British Museum,
  <https://www.britishmuseum.org/>). Great Zimbabwe is documented through archaeology and
  UNESCO World Heritage listing (<https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/364/>). The 1857 rebellion
  in India is documented in British and Indian records and in later scholarship. Dates are
  approximations that scholars revise as new evidence appears.
