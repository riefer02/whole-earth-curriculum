# Lesson 9 — What Is Power, and Who Holds It?

## Summary

Learners build a working idea of **power** — the ability to influence or decide, for
yourself and others — and distinguish **"power over"** (one decides for others) from
**"power with"** (people deciding together). They ask who holds power in a community,
comparing a monarch, a council of elders, a village assembly, and a **confederacy that
decides by consensus**, then map power in a place they know.

## Objectives

- Explain what power is, who holds it in a community or society, and how it can be used
  fairly or unfairly. _(D08.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of one decision made in your day — what time to eat, who sits where, what the class
studies, who runs your town. Someone decided it. **Power** is the ability to influence or
decide, for yourself and for others. It is everywhere — in families, schools, villages, and
whole societies — and it can be held by one person, a few, or many. Today you look at what
power is and who holds it, in your world and across the human story.

## Materials

- What-is-power poster
- Who-holds-power cards (six examples)
- Power map

## Preparation

- Print or draw the what-is-power poster.
- Copy the six cards and a power map for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for discussion and a flat space for writing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **describing power
without loading it with judgment**: power is simply the ability to influence or decide, and
it can be held many ways. Distinguish **power over** (one decides for others) from **power
with** (deciding together), but keep both as descriptions, not verdicts — the fairness
question comes in the next lesson. Use examples across societies and honor shared,
consensual forms of power (a council, an assembly, a confederacy) as equal to any other.
Anchor to the learner's own life so the abstraction lands concretely. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have traced sources, knowledge, movement, and place. Now you
   look at something that runs through all of them: **power**.
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** Read the poster. **Power** is the ability to influence or
   decide — for yourself and for others. It comes in two shapes. **Power over:** one person
   or group decides for others. **Power with:** people decide together, as equals. Both are
   real, and both exist all around you.
3. **Who holds it? (15 min).** Read the six cards. In one society, a **monarch** holds much
   power alone. In another, a **council of elders** decides together. In another, a
   **village assembly** lets many voices speak. In still another, a **confederacy of
   nations** decides by **consensus** — everyone talking until they agree. Power can also
   live in a **family** and in a **school**. For each card, ask: is this power over, or
   power with — or a mix?
4. **Map power in your world (15 min).** On your power map, write one place you know (home,
   school, or community), then name **who holds power** there and **whether** it is mostly
   power over, power with, or both.
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, read your map and listen to theirs. Did you
   both notice that power can be shared, not only held by one?
6. **Close (5 min).** Power is not good or bad by itself — it is a fact of living together.
   What matters is how it is used. That is the next question.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two cards (a monarch and a village assembly) with picture prompts; scribe
  the learner's power map.
- **Extension:** Name a real decision and trace who has power *over* it and who has power
  *with* others in it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define power, name who holds it in at least
  one society, and distinguish power over from power with?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I say what power is, who holds it, and whether it
  is power over or power with — in a place I know?"

## Home connection

With someone at home, list three decisions made in your household and who makes each one —
and ask which are made *over* others and which are made *with* everyone.

## Resources

- The idea of power as the ability to influence or decide is a standard concept in the
  social sciences (see, for example, the work of the political theorist Steven Lukes,
  *Power: A Radical View*). The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy and its Great Law of
  Peace, with its consensus-based councils and the role of clan mothers, are documented in
  Haudenosaunee oral tradition and in scholarship and museum collections (see the
  Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, <https://americanindian.si.edu/>).
  Consensus and assembly forms of decision-making are documented in many societies,
  including West African village assemblies. These are presented side by side, not ranked.
