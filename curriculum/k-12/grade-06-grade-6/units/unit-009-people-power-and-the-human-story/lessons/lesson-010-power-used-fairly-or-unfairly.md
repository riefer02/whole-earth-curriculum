# Lesson 10 — Power Used Fairly or Unfairly

## Summary

Learners apply the idea of power to the question of **fairness**: how power can be used
fairly (all voices heard, serving all) or unfairly (one decides, some silenced, serving the
few). A three-question **fairness test** — does it serve everyone? who speaks? can it be
challenged? — helps them judge scenarios, while they learn that "fair" is a value judgment
as well as a description.

## Objectives

- Explain what power is, who holds it in a community or society, and how it can be used
  fairly or unfairly. _(D08.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of a rule you once thought was unfair — and one you thought was fair. What made the
difference? Maybe: everyone had a say (fair) or one person decided for everyone (unfair);
it helped everyone (fair) or helped only a few (unfair). People have asked this question in
every society. Today you learn a simple **fairness test** to tell the difference, and you
practice it — while remembering that *what is fair* is also something people must decide
together, not just measure.

## Materials

- Fair-and-unfair poster
- Fair-or-unfair cards (six scenarios)
- Fairness-test note

## Preparation

- Print or draw the fair-and-unfair poster.
- Copy the six scenario cards and a note for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for discussion and a flat space for writing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **reasoning about
fairness with a simple, shared test — while distinguishing evidence from values**. The test
(does it serve everyone? who speaks? can it be challenged?) is a *tool for judgment*, not a
rule that decides everything; help learners see that "fair" and "unfair" are partly values
people hold, not only facts to measure. Use examples from many societies and from the
learner's own life. Guard against reducing fairness to "majority rule": a majority can still
treat a minority unfairly. This lesson prepares the next, on a movement for rights. See
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you learned what power is. Today you ask the harder
   question: **is this power being used fairly or unfairly?**
2. **Meet the fairness test (10 min).** Read the poster. **Fair use** of power: decisions
   are made with everyone, all voices are heard, and the power serves all. **Unfair use**:
   one person or group decides for others, some voices are silenced, and the power serves
   the few. The test has three questions: **Does it serve everyone, or only the powerful?
   Who gets to speak, and who is left out? Can a decision be questioned, or must it be
   obeyed?**
3. **Worked example: judge one scenario (10 min).** A village assembly where everyone may
   speak, and a decision is reached only when all agree — that is **fair use**. A ruler who
   taxes the poor heavily while the rich pay little, and who allows no one to object — that
   is **unfair use**. Apply the three questions to each and say why.
4. **Judge the scenarios (15 min).** Take the six cards. For each, run the fairness test and
   sort it as *fair* or *unfair* — and be ready to say which question tipped your judgment.
5. **Record your reasoning (5 min).** On your note, write one scenario, your judgment
   (fair or unfair), and the reason, using the test.
6. **Close (5 min).** Power itself is neither good nor bad — but how it is used can be fair
   or unfair. And when it is unfair, people have always found the courage to say so. That is
   the next chapter.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two clear scenarios with picture prompts; scribe the learner's note and
  read the test aloud slowly.
- **Extension:** Debate a close case — a decision that serves many but silences a few — and
  weigh what fairness would require.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner apply the fairness test to a scenario and
  give a reason for judging it fair or unfair?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I use the test — serving whom, who speaks, can it
  be questioned — and can I explain my judgment?"

## Home connection

With someone at home, take one household or community rule and run the fairness test on it
together, then talk about whether it passes.

## Resources

- The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy's consensus decision-making is documented in
  Haudenosaunee oral tradition and in scholarship (see the Smithsonian's National Museum of
  the American Indian, <https://americanindian.si.edu/>). West African village assemblies
  with open discussion are documented in scholarship (see, for example, the work of
  historians on Igbo and other societies). The fairness test is offered as a reasoning tool,
  not a universal rule; the curriculum teaches learners to distinguish evidence from values
  and to decide "fair" together, in line with `docs/philosophy.md` §5.
