# Lesson 8 — Plants, Animals, and People on the Move

## Summary

Learners analyze how the movement of plants, animals, and people between the **Americas**
and **Afro-Eurasia** — the **Columbian Exchange**, from 1492 — changed environments and
societies, using **maize, potato, tomato, cacao, chili, horses, cattle**, and the **forced
movement of people** as examples. The exchange is told honestly: it transformed diets and
landscapes everywhere and was entangled with **colonization and slavery**, which harmed many
peoples.

## Objectives

- Analyze how the movement of plants, animals, and people between world regions changed
  environments and societies, using one historical example. _(D08.S3.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of a food you eat that did not grow near where you live — it arrived from somewhere
else, long ago. Everywhere on Earth, plants, animals, and people have moved, and each
movement changed both the place they left and the place they reached. Sometimes that
movement was chosen and welcome; sometimes it was forced and cruel. Today you look at one
great movement of plants, animals, and people — and you hold both its gifts and its harms.

## Materials

- Columbian Exchange poster
- What-moved cards (eight items)
- Exchange-effects note

## Preparation

- Print or draw the Columbian Exchange poster.
- Copy the eight cards and a note for each learner.
- Review the note below on holding the harm of colonization and slavery with honesty, care,
  and dignity.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is a **difficult topic, held with
honesty and care**. The Columbian Exchange is often taught only as a list of crops "given"
to the world; that telling hides the harm. Teach it whole: (1) what moved, (2) how it
changed environments and societies, and (3) the honest truth that the exchange rode on
**colonization and the transatlantic slave trade**, which brought disease, dispossession,
and enslavement to Indigenous and African peoples. Spend real time on **what moved and
changed**, and name the harm clearly but briefly, always centering the **dignity, knowledge,
and agency** of Indigenous and African peoples — never victimhood. Respect a learner's right
to pause; connect difficulty to hope (peoples kept their knowledge and adapted). See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have seen how people and land shape each other in one place. Now
   you see what happens when **things move between** places — across an ocean.
2. **Meet the exchange (20 min).** From **1492**, after European ships reached the Americas,
   plants, animals, and people began moving **both ways** across the Atlantic. From the
   **Americas** to the rest of the world went **maize, potato, tomato, cacao, and chili**.
   Into the **Americas** came **wheat, rice, horses, cattle, sheep, and pigs** — and
   **people**, many of them **enslaved Africans** taken by force. These movements changed
   everything: the **potato** fed the Andes (where it began) and later Ireland; the **tomato**
   became central to cooking in Italy and South Asia; **maize** spread across Africa;
   **horses** transformed how peoples of the plains hunted and traveled.
3. **Name the harm honestly (5 min).** This exchange was not just crops. It rode on
   **colonization** — the taking of Indigenous peoples' land and self-rule — and on the
   **transatlantic slave trade**, which stole millions of African people from their homes.
   New diseases also devastated many Indigenous peoples. Even so, Indigenous and African
   peoples kept their knowledge, their languages, and their ways, and adapted. The harm is
   real; so is their strength.
4. **Sort what moved (10 min).** Take the eight cards and sort them: *moved from the
   Americas* or *moved into the Americas*. Check with a partner.
5. **Record one change (10 min).** On your note, write **one thing that moved**, **where it
   went**, and **one change it brought** to a place or a people.
6. **Close (5 min).** Movement changes the world — for good and for harm. A historian's job
   is to see both, honestly, and to honor the peoples who lived through it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort fewer cards with picture prompts; scribe the learner's note and keep the
  tone calm, honest, and hopeful.
- **Extension:** Trace one crop (for example, the potato) in two places it reached and write
  one change it brought in each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name what moved in each direction and one
  change it brought — while naming the harm of colonization and slavery honestly?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I see both the changes and the harms, and did I
  hold the peoples involved with dignity and respect?"

## Home connection

With someone at home, list three foods on your table that came from far away, and find out
where one of them came from and how it got to you.

## Resources

- The Columbian Exchange — the movement of plants, animals, and people between the Americas
  and Afro-Eurasia from 1492 — is a well-documented framework in world history (see Alfred
  W. Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange*, and the Smithsonian, <https://www.si.edu/>). The
  transatlantic slave trade, in which millions of African people were forcibly taken, is
  documented by UNESCO's "Routes of Enslaved Peoples" project
  (<https://www.unesco.org/en/routes-enslaved-peoples>) and by
  historians. Indigenous peoples' knowledge and agency throughout this period are central
  to the telling, in line with the curriculum's commitment to center dignity and
  liberation, not victimhood. Dates are approximations that scholars revise as new evidence
  appears.
