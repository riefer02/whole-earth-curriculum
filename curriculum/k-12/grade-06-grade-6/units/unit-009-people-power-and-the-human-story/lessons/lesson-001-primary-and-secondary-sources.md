# Lesson 1 — Primary and Secondary Sources: Two Ways to See the Past

## Summary

Learners meet the historian's first tool: the difference between a **primary source**
(made at the time, by someone who was there) and a **secondary source** (made later, by
someone who studied the primary sources). A worked example uses the **1883 Krakatoa
eruption**, and learners sort sources and record two sources of their own.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between a primary source and a secondary source. _(D08.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of a photo you took yesterday of something that really happened — a meal, a storm, a
game. That is like a **primary source**: it comes from the moment. Now think of a book or
video someone made *about* your town or country, years later. That is like a **secondary
source**: it was made later, by someone who studied the first-hand evidence. Historians use
both, the way you might use your own photo *and* a book about your home — one shows the
moment, the other steps back to explain it.

## Materials

- Source-kind poster
- Source sort cards (eight items)
- My two sources page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the source-kind poster.
- Copy the eight source sort cards and a "my two sources" page for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for the worked example and a flat space for sorting.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **recognizing primary
and secondary sources** — not memorizing the 1883 eruption. Model the worked example fully
(explicit instruction, guided practice) before learners sort on their own; source-kind
thinking is a new skill for most Grade 6 learners. Use the simple test: **was it made at
the time of the event, by someone who was there (or very close)?** That is primary.
**Made later, by someone who studied the evidence?** That is secondary. Note honestly that
the line can blur — a source can be "more primary" or "more secondary" — but the two poles
are what learners need first. This lesson sets up the next, where learners use both kinds
to describe one past event. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last year you put events on timelines. This year you begin working
   like a historian — asking *where does the story come from?* That question has two
   answers: a **primary source** and a **secondary source**.
2. **Meet the two kinds (10 min).** Look at the poster. On the left is a **primary
   source**: a ship captain's log written in **August 1883**, when the volcano **Krakatoa**
   (in Southeast Asia, between Java and Sumatra) erupted. The captain wrote it **at the
   time**, because he was there. On the right is a **secondary source**: a book called
   *Volcanoes: A History*, written in **2018**. Its author was not there in 1883; she
   studied the captain's log and other evidence, then wrote about it **later**.
3. **Worked example: sort a few together (10 min).** A **primary source** is made at the
   time, by someone who was there. A **secondary source** is made later, by someone who
   studied the primary sources. Sort these two together: a **telegraph message sent the
   day of the eruption** (primary — at the time) and a **museum panel about Krakatoa
   written last year** (secondary — later). You decide which is which and why.
4. **Sort your own (15 min).** Take the eight cards and sort them into two columns:
   *made at the time* and *made later*. Check with a partner: did you agree on all eight?
   If not, explain your reasons.
5. **Record two of your own (5 min).** On your page, write one event from your own life —
   a birthday, a trip, a storm. Then list one **primary** source about it (a photo, a
   memory, a note you wrote that day) and one **secondary** source about it (a story
   someone told later, an article, a documentary).
6. **Close (5 min).** A historian's first question is *where does this come from?* You now
   know the two answers — and why a good history uses both.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the two-pole test on real objects (a leaf from the ground vs. a drawing
  of it); sort fewer cards, and scribe the learner's own two sources.
- **Extension:** Write one sentence about why a historian would want both kinds of source —
  what each one adds that the other cannot.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner correctly sort sources as primary or
  secondary and give the reason ("made at the time" vs. "made later")?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Could I tell a primary source from a secondary one,
  and explain *why* each one counts the way it does?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to show you one thing from your family's past that was made "at the
time" (a photo, a letter, an object) and one thing made "later" (a story, a book, a show)
— and sort them together.

## Resources

- The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recorded history,
  is documented by the Smithsonian Institution's Global Volcanism Program
  (<https://volcano.si.edu/>). The distinction between primary and secondary sources is a
  standard tool of historical method; see, for example, the guidance of the U.S. National
  Archives (<https://www.archives.gov/>) and the British Library
  (<https://www.bl.uk/>). Dates are approximations that scholars revise as new evidence
  appears; the source-kind idea, not the exact day, is what matters here.
