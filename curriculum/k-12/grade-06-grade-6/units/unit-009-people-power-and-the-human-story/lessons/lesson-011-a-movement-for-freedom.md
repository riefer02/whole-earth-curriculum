# Lesson 11 — A Movement for Freedom: The People of India

## Summary

Learners study a movement for freedom — the **Indian independence movement** — as a mass
movement of ordinary people: women, workers, students, and peasants who marched, boycotted,
spun their own cloth, and refused unjust rule with dignity until India became free in 1947.
A second card (**Ghana's independence movement**) offers comparison. The people are centered
as **authors of their own freedom**, never as victims.

## Objectives

- Describe a movement for rights or freedom, such as a struggle against colonial rule or for
  equal treatment, and what its people did. _(D08.S4.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time people around you changed something unfair — not by force, but by acting
together, refusing to go along, and holding to their dignity. That is what a movement for
freedom is: ordinary people, many of them, deciding that an unjust rule will no longer
stand. Today you meet one of the great such movements, told through the courage of the
people who made it.

## Materials

- Freedom-movement poster
- Movement story cards (India, Ghana)
- Rights-movement note

## Preparation

- Print or draw the freedom-movement poster.
- Copy the two story cards and a note for each learner.
- Review the note below on holding difficult topics with dignity and hope.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is a **difficult topic, held with
dignity and hope — never victimhood**. Center the people as the heroes of their own story:
name the injustice (colonial rule, which denied self-rule and dignity) briefly and
factually, and spend most of the time on **what the people did** and **what they won**.
Emphasize that the Indian independence movement was a **mass movement of millions** — women,
workers, students, peasants, and leaders from many regions and faiths — not the act of one
person; a single famous name (Mohandas Gandhi) is honored but never treated as the whole
story. Offer Ghana's independence as a comparison that shows the same courage on another
continent. Connect difficulty to hope and to something the learner can do. This lesson
produces the unit's **rights-movement note** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have seen what power is and when it is used unfairly. Today you
   meet the answer people have always found to unfair power: a **movement** — ordinary
   people acting together for rights and dignity.
2. **Meet the Indian independence movement (20 min).** Read the card. Under **colonial
   rule**, India's people did not govern themselves; they were ruled by Britain, and many
   were treated unjustly. Over decades, **millions** of ordinary people — women, workers,
   students, and peasants, led by many, including Mohandas Gandhi — acted **peacefully**: in
   the **Salt March of 1930**, people walked to the sea to make their own salt, refusing an
   unjust tax; they **boycotted** foreign cloth and **spun their own**; they refused to
   cooperate with unjust laws, and they helped one another throughout. In **1947**, after
   decades of this courage, India won its **independence**.
3. **Meet Ghana's movement (10 min).** Read the second card. In **Ghana** (West Africa),
   people organized through the **Convention People's Party**, with boycotts and peaceful
   strikes — "positive action" — until, in **1957**, Ghana became the first sub-Saharan
   African colony to win independence, and many African nations soon followed.
4. **Record one movement (10 min).** Choose one. On your note, write: **What was the
   injustice?** **What did the people do?** **What did they win?** Center the people's
   actions and courage.
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, read your note. Did you both center what the
   people *did*, not only what was done to them?
6. **Close (5 min).** Freedom is not given; it is won, by ordinary people acting together
   with dignity. That is one of the deepest truths of the human story.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one movement at a time with a fill-in note and picture prompts; scribe
  the learner's words and keep the tone calm and hopeful.
- **Extension:** Compare the two movements — the injustice, the actions, and how ordinary
  people acted in each — and name one thing they share.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner name the injustice, what the people
  did, and what they won, in one movement — framing the people with dignity and agency?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I center the people's actions and courage, not only
  the injustice — and did I hold the story with respect?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a movement for rights or freedom they know of — near or far — and
what its people did to win it.

## Resources

- The Indian independence movement, including the 1930 Salt March and the boycotts and
  noncooperation that led to independence in 1947, is documented in Indian history and
  scholarship and in the Nobel Prize site (<https://www.nobelprize.org/>). It is told here
  as a mass movement of millions, not the act of one person. Ghana's independence movement,
  led by the Convention People's Party under Kwame Nkrumah, winning independence in 1957, is
  documented in Ghanaian and African history. These are told as stories of dignity, agency,
  and collective action, in line with the curriculum's commitment to center liberation, not
  victimhood.
