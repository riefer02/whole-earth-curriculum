# Lesson 12 — The Human Story, and Our Part in It

## Summary

The capstone gathers the unit's threads into **one human story**. Learners assemble their
portfolio artifacts — the two-source note, the change-and-continuity note, the
knowledge-transmission example, and the rights-movement note — and, in a gallery walk and
closing circle, connect each to the big question, recall **what the freedom movement's people
did**, and name **their own part** in the unfinished story of fairness and dignity.

## Objectives

- Describe a movement for rights or freedom and what its people did. _(D08.S4.06.02)_
- Revisit the unit's threads — sources, change and continuity, knowledge, technology, place,
  movement, and power — and connect them into one shared story. _(D08.S1.06.01,
  D08.S1.06.02, D08.S2.06.01, D08.S2.06.02, D08.S3.06.01, D08.S3.06.02, D08.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

A story is not one page but a whole book — and the human story is the longest book of all.
You have been collecting its pages all unit: two kinds of source, a hundred years of change
and continuity, three ways of keeping knowledge, a technology's journey, a place and its
people, a great movement of plants and animals, the question of power, and a people who won
their freedom. Today you bind those pages together, walk through them like a museum, and
find where **your** page fits — because the story is still being written, and you are part
of it.

## Materials

- Portfolio pages (the learner's own artifacts)
- Human-story web poster
- Capstone card
- Pencils and art supplies

## Preparation

- Have each learner gather their own artifacts from this unit (two-source note,
  change-and-continuity note, knowledge-transmission example, rights-movement note).
- Print or draw the human-story web poster.
- Copy a capstone card for each learner, and arrange a gallery wall or story path.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is the unit's **capstone and summative
assessment**, and a moment of **retrieval and synthesis** — learners recall the whole unit
and connect it, rather than re-reading it. Keep the tone celebratory and collective: success
is shared, mastery is individual, and no one is ranked. The closing should land on **hope and
agency** — the learner's own part in the unfinished story, and a return to **what the
freedom movement's people did**. If a learner's artifacts are incomplete, let them finish one
now rather than be judged "done" or "not done." This lesson completes the unit's portfolio.
See `docs/facilitation.md` on assessing without ranking.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look back with a partner: **what did we learn this unit?** Take turns
   recalling one thing each — sources, change and continuity, knowledge, a technology's
   journey, place, movement, power, a movement for freedom. This is retrieval: pulling it
   out of memory, not re-reading it.
2. **Assemble your pages (10 min).** Lay out your own artifacts in order: your two-source
   note, your change-and-continuity note, your knowledge-transmission example, and your
   rights-movement note. Check each is complete; finish any that need it.
3. **Walk the museum (20 min).** Display the artifacts and walk the gallery. At each page,
   notice what it shows about the human story. Then, on your capstone card, answer three
   prompts: **Choose one artifact** you are proud of. **Say how it connects** to the big
   question — how people have known, moved, held power, and stood for fairness. **Name one
   thing you can do** to honor fairness and dignity in your own life.
4. **Remember the freedom movement (10 min).** In pairs, recall the Indian independence
   movement: **what did its people do?** (They marched, boycotted, spun their own cloth,
   and refused unjust rule — millions of ordinary people, with dignity.) Name one way their
   courage connects to your one action.
5. **Close the circle (15 min).** Gather. Each learner shares their chosen artifact and
   their one action for fairness — one sentence is enough. Listen to how many different
   pages make up this one story.
6. **Look forward (5 min).** The story is not finished. You are not only reading the human
   story — you are writing the next line of it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Choose one artifact with the learner and scribe their capstone card; celebrate
  completion of a single page rather than all four.
- **Extension:** Write a one-paragraph synthesis linking two or more threads (sources,
  change, knowledge, movement, power) with an example from the unit and one from your own
  life.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The learner's assembled artifacts — the two-source note, the
  change-and-continuity note, the knowledge-transmission example, and the rights-movement
  note — together show mastery of the unit's standards.
- **Peer/self:** In the circle, learners listen and each names one connection they heard and
  one action they can take for fairness and dignity.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I connect my artifact to the big question, recall
  what the freedom movement's people did, and name a real thing I can do for fairness and
  dignity?"

## Home connection

Share your whole portfolio with someone at home — walk them through your pages like a
museum — and tell them the one action for fairness and dignity you chose.

  the curriculum's aim that learners become agents of care and justice, not only readers of
