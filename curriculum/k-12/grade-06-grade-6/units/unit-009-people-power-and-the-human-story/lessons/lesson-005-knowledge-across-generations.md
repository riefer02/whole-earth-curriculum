# Lesson 5 — Knowledge Across Generations: Oral, Written, Apprenticeship

## Summary

Learners meet three ways knowledge passes between generations — **oral tradition**,
**written records**, and **apprenticeship** — through the **songlines** of Australian
Aboriginal peoples, the **Timbuktu manuscripts** of Mali, and **master-and-apprentice** craft
in Japan. The three traditions are held side by side as equals, none better than another.

## Objectives

- Explain how knowledge is passed from one generation to the next through oral tradition,
  written records, or apprenticeship, using examples from different peoples.
  _(D08.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of something you know that no one wrote down — a family recipe, a song, a way of
fixing things, a story your grandparent tells. That is knowledge passing from one generation
to the next. Humans have done this in many ways: some knowledge is carried **by word**, some
**on the page**, and some **by doing** — a craft learned hand to hand. Each is a real way of
knowing, and no single way is "better." Today you meet all three, held side by side.

## Materials

- Knowledge traditions poster
- Knowledge traditions cards (three traditions)
- Knowledge-transmission note

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-panel poster.
- Copy the three tradition cards and a note for each learner.
- Arrange a circle for the story and small groups for the cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to build is **explaining how
knowledge passes in each tradition, and holding all three as equal**. This is a values and
perspective lesson as much as a knowledge lesson: guard firmly against ranking ("written is
more advanced than oral"). Oral tradition is not a lesser version of history; it is a
complete knowledge system, and it has kept land, law, science, and story alive for many
generations. The three examples span three continents and honor Indigenous and non-European
knowledge traditions. Learners may share their own traditions — welcome that; a learner's
own way of knowing is part of the human story. This lesson produces the unit's
**knowledge-transmission example** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** So far you have read the past through sources and through change and
   continuity. Now ask: *how does knowledge survive from one generation to the next?*
2. **Meet the three traditions (20 min).** Read the cards. *Oral tradition — songlines
   (Australia):* Australian Aboriginal peoples carry knowledge of the land, its water, its
   law, and its ancestors in **songlines** — songs that map the country and are sung and
   passed down across many generations, so the way is never lost. *Written records —
   Timbuktu manuscripts (Mali):* families in and around Timbuktu kept thousands of
   manuscripts — in Arabic and local languages — on law, astronomy, medicine, and math,
   copied and preserved from parent to child for centuries. *Apprenticeship — Japan:* a
   craft, such as sword-making, pottery, or tea, is learned **by doing**, alongside a
   master, over years — the knowledge lives in skilled hands and careful practice, passed
   from teacher to learner.
3. **Notice what they share (5 min).** All three pass knowledge from one generation to the
   next — the difference is *how*: by word, by page, or by doing. And many peoples use more
   than one way at once.
4. **Record one tradition (15 min).** Choose one tradition. On your note, write: **which
   tradition it is**, **how knowledge passes in it** (by word, by page, or by doing), and
   **one thing it keeps alive** (the land, the laws, the stars, a craft).
5. **Share and check (5 min).** With a partner, read your note and listen to theirs. Can you
   each name how *your partner's* tradition passes knowledge?
6. **Close (5 min).** Knowledge is the gift one generation gives the next. Humans give it in
   many ways — and every way is real.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one tradition at a time with a fill-in note and picture prompts; scribe
  the learner's words and keep the three traditions clearly labeled.
- **Extension:** Compare two traditions — what each one is especially good at holding, and
  where they agree or differ in how knowledge is kept.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner explain how knowledge passes in one
  tradition and name one thing it keeps alive — holding the three traditions as equals?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I treat each way of knowing with respect, and can I
  explain how knowledge passes in the one I chose?"

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how they learned one thing they know well — by word, by page, or by
doing — and tell them the three traditions you met today.

## Resources

- Australian Aboriginal songlines, which encode knowledge of land, water, and law, are
  documented in Indigenous knowledge and scholarship (see the Australian Institute of
  Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies, <https://aiatsis.gov.au/>). The Timbuktu
  manuscripts, thousands of documents preserved by families, are documented in the Timbuktu
  Manuscripts Project and UNESCO (<https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/119/>). Japanese
  master-apprentice craft traditions are documented in Japanese cultural heritage practice
  (see UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage listings, <https://ich.unesco.org/>). These three
  traditions are presented side by side as equals, in line with the curriculum's commitment
  to honor many knowledge traditions, not to rank them.
