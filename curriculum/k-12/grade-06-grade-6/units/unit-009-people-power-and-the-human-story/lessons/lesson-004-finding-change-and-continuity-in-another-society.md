# Lesson 4 — Finding Change and Continuity in Another Society

## Summary

Learners apply **change and continuity** to a second society, choosing among three cards —
**Māori settlement in Aotearoa**, the first century of **Japan's Edo period**, and **Ming
China** — and record one change and one continuity in a note. This becomes the unit's
**hundred-year change-and-continuity** portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Describe change and continuity over a period of about a hundred years in one society,
  giving an example of each. _(D08.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

You practiced change and continuity on one society — the Songhai and Timbuktu. Now you do
it on a society you choose, the way a historian picks a place and asks: *over this hundred
years, what became different, and what stayed the same?* It is the same skill you use when
you notice that your own street has a new shop (change) but the same corner tree (continuity)
— turned toward the past.

## Materials

- Society cards (Māori settlement, Edo Japan, Ming China)
- Change-and-continuity note
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Copy the three society cards and a note for each learner.
- Review the card facts so you can retell them aloud.
- Arrange a circle for retrieval and a flat space for writing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is the **application and retrieval
step** of the change-and-continuity skill: learners recall the Songhai example, then apply
the frame to a society of their own choosing. The three cards are deliberately from three
regions — Oceania (Aotearoa), East Asia (Japan and China) — and each centers the society's
own life, not outside conquest. For each society, change and continuity are both present; a
learner's job is to find one of each with reasons. This lesson produces the unit's
**change-and-continuity note** portfolio artifact. See `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Without looking at your notes, tell a partner one example
   of change and one of continuity from the Songhai hundred years. This is retrieval —
   pulling it from memory.
2. **Meet the three societies (10 min).** Read the three cards. *Māori settlement in
   Aotearoa (about 1300–1500):* ancestors of the Māori voyaged across the Pacific and
   settled Aotearoa (New Zealand); they adapted crops and life to a colder land, while
   **whakapapa** (genealogy) and oral tradition carried their history forward. *Edo Japan
   (1600–1700):* after long wars, peace arrived; cities like Edo grew and new roads
   connected the land, while rice farming and family and community life stayed steady.
   *Ming China (about 1368–1500):* a new dynasty rebuilt the land, printed books, and sent
   fleets across the Indian Ocean, while farming, family, and classical learning continued.
3. **Choose and record (20 min).** Pick one society. On your note, write the society and the
   hundred years, then fill two boxes: **one change** (what became different) and **one
   continuity** (what stayed the same) — each with a reason.
4. **Share and check (10 min).** With a partner, read your note. Does your partner agree
   that your change is a real change and your continuity a real continuity for that society?
5. **Close (5 min).** You can now hold two truths at once about any place: it changes, and
   it carries on. Keep this note — it joins your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use one society card with sentence starters ("One thing that changed was…;
  one thing that stayed the same was…") and scribe where needed.
- **Extension:** Choose a second society and write one change and one continuity for it too,
  then name one similarity between the two societies' stories.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio/observation):** Can the learner name one change and one continuity
  for a chosen society, with a reason for each?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Is my change really a change, and my continuity really a
  continuity — and can I explain why?"

## Home connection

Tell someone at home the hundred-year story of the society you chose — one change and one
continuity — and ask what changed and what stayed the same in your own family over a similar
span.

## Resources

- Māori settlement of Aotearoa and the centrality of whakapapa are documented in Māori oral
  tradition and in New Zealand's national collections (see Te Ara — the Encyclopedia of New
  Zealand, <https://teara.govt.nz/>). The Edo period of Japan (1603–1868), including the
  growth of Edo and early Tokugawa peace, is standard in Japanese history (see the Tokyo
  National Museum, <https://www.tnm.jp/>). The Ming dynasty (1368–1644), including the Zheng
  He voyages and printing, is standard in Chinese history (see the Palace Museum collections,
  <https://en.dpm.org.cn/>). Dates are approximations that scholars revise as new evidence
  appears; the change-and-continuity idea, not the exact year, is what matters here.
