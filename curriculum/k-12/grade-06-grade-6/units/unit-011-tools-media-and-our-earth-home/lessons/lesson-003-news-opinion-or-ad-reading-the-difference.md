# Lesson 3 — News, Opinion, or Ad? Reading the Difference

## Summary

Learners distinguish **news, opinion, and advertisement** by the clues each leaves. A worked
example takes one event — a new park opening — and writes it three ways: **news** reports
checkable facts, **opinion** voices a view with words like "best," and an **advertisement**
tries to sell with words like "buy" and "free offer." Learners then sort mixed messages about a
bus route, a fruit snack, and a library into the three kinds, reading critically **without
cynicism** — the goal is clarity, not distrust.

## Objectives

- Distinguish between news, opinion, and advertisement, and identify clues for each.
  _(D10.S2.06.01)_

## Connection

Scroll any screen, open any paper, walk past any wall, and you meet three kinds of messages:
one is telling you what happened, one is telling you what someone thinks, and one is trying to
sell you something. They mix together on purpose, and telling them apart is a skill — like
learning to hear three different voices in a crowd. Today you learn the clues each voice
leaves behind.

## Materials

- Three-message poster
- Message-sort cards
- Message-sort page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the three-message poster.
- Copy message-sort cards for each pair and a message-sort page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **three-way sort**:
read a message, name its kind (news, opinion, or advertisement), and point to the clue. Model
the park example fully as a worked example before independent sorting (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). **Teach without cynicism:** the tone is "be clear-eyed, not suspicious" — news
is still worth reading, opinions still worth hearing, and ads are honest when they say they
are selling. Distinguish evidence from values: *which words and structures mark each kind* is
observable; *that we should know which is which* is a value this curriculum holds. See
`docs/philosophy.md` §5, §7 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Ask: when you see a message, how do you know if it is telling you what
   happened, what someone thinks, or what to buy? Today you learn the three voices and their
   clues.
2. **Meet the three messages (12 min).** Look at the poster — one event, the new park, written
   three ways. **News:** "A new park opened Tuesday. It has trees, a pond, and a play area."
   Clue: it reports facts you can check — who, what, when, where. **Opinion:** "The new park is
   the best thing to happen to this town!" Clue: it is someone's view, with words like "best"
   and "should." **Advertisement:** "Visit the new park café — buy one drink, get one free!"
   Clue: it tries to sell, with words like "buy" and "free offer." One event, three purposes.
3. **Sort your own messages (18 min).** Take a card set about a **new bus route**, a **fruit
   snack**, and a **library**. For each message, decide — news, opinion, or advertisement — and
   write it in the right column with the clue that gave it away. Remember the clues: news =
   checkable facts; opinion = a view, "best," "should"; ad = a sale, "buy," "offer."
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Swap pages. Say: "this is ___, because ___." Ask: does
   the clue really match the kind? Fix any that do not.
5. **Close (5 min).** Three voices speak in your feed every day. Now you can tell them apart —
   not to shut them out, but to know exactly what each one is doing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a message-sort page with the clue hints printed at the top, and work one
  message aloud first.
- **Extension:** Write a fourth message of your own about the same event, then have a partner
  classify it and name the clue.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner classify a message as news, opinion, or
  advertisement and point to the clue?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name the kind and the clue — and would someone else
  see the same clue I saw?"

## Home connection

Find three messages at home or nearby — one news, one opinion, one ad — and say what clue told
you which was which.

## Resources

- The distinction between news (reporting), opinion (editorial/commentary), and advertising is
  standard media-literacy content; the clues used here (checkable facts vs. judgment words vs.
  selling words) follow widely used frameworks such as the Center for News Literacy and
  Common Sense Media's media-literacy materials. The "clarity, not cynicism" framing follows
  `docs/philosophy.md` §7 and `docs/facilitation.md`. On modeling before practice, see
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1>.
