# Lesson 5 — Populations, Communities, and Predator and Prey

## Summary

Learners explain the difference between a **population** and a **community**, and describe the
**predator–prey** relationship as a rise-and-fall cycle. A worked example follows a **meadow**:
the population is all the grasshoppers (one kind); the community is the grass, grasshoppers,
mice, and hawk together (many kinds); and hawks and mice rise and fall together in a repeating
cycle. Learners then order the cycle for a **pond heron and fish, a reef grouper and small
fish, or a savanna lion and gazelle**.

## Objectives

- Explain the difference between a population and a community, and describe one relationship
  among organisms, such as predator and prey. _(D10.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

Stand in any place where life is — a garden, a field, a shore, a tree. Look at one kind of
living thing: those are a **population**, all the same kind together. Now look at everything at
once — the plants, the insects, the birds, the worms: that is the **community**. And watch the
community long enough and you will see a rhythm: the hunters and the hunted rising and falling
together, like a dance neither one can stop alone.

## Materials

- Population-and-community poster
- Predator-prey cards
- Cycle page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the population-and-community poster.
- Copy predator-prey cards for each pair and a cycle page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **population-vs-
community distinction** plus the **predator-prey cycle trace**. Model the meadow fully as a
worked example before learners order their own cycle (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Build
on Grade 4 (producers, consumers, decomposers) and Grade 5 (food webs and interdependence).
Present predation factually and without blood or fear: it is one of many relationships in a
community, and the cycle keeps both sides in balance. Distinguish evidence from values: *how
populations rise and fall together* is observable ecology; *that we should understand and care
for communities of life* is a value this curriculum holds. See `docs/philosophy.md` §5 and
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall the food web: energy flows from what is eaten to what eats it.
   Today you sharpen two words — *population* and *community* — and trace the rise-and-fall
   dance of predator and prey.
2. **Meet the meadow (12 min).** Look at the poster. A **population** is all of *one kind* of
   living thing in a place — all the grasshoppers in the meadow. A **community** is *all* the
   living things there together — the grass, the grasshoppers, the mice, and the hawk. Now the
   cycle: many mice → hawks eat well → hawk numbers rise → fewer mice → hawks eat less → hawk
   numbers fall → mice recover → the cycle repeats. Predator and prey do not just interact;
   they **rise and fall together**.
3. **Order your own cycle (18 min).** Take a card. The **pond** (fish and heron): many fish →
   herons eat well → heron numbers rise → fewer fish → herons eat less → heron numbers fall →
   fish recover. The **reef** (small fish and grouper): many small fish → groupers eat well →
   grouper numbers rise → fewer small fish → groupers eat less → grouper numbers fall → small
   fish recover. The **savanna** (gazelle and lion): many gazelles → lions eat well → lion
   numbers rise → fewer gazelles → lions eat less → lion numbers fall → gazelles recover. On
   your cycle page, put the four steps in order, then write one sentence for *population* and
   one for *community*.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Say the cycle in one breath: "when prey is many,
   predators rise; when prey is few, predators fall; then prey recovers." Ask: does the order
   loop back to the start?
5. **Close (5 min).** A population is one thread; a community is the whole woven cloth. The
   predator-prey dance is one of the threads that keeps the cloth moving.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a cycle page with the first box filled in and a sentence starter for the
  population/community difference; work one cycle aloud first.
- **Extension:** Add a second predator to the cycle and explain how the rise-and-fall pattern
  changes.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner distinguish a population from a community and
  order a four-step predator-prey cycle so it loops?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name one kind for the population and many kinds for
  the community — and does my cycle rise, fall, and loop back?"

## Home connection

Pick a nearby place with life. Name one population there, then the whole community, and ask who
hunts whom.

## Resources

- Population (all individuals of one species in an area) vs. community (all species in an area)
  and the predator-prey cycle are core ecology concepts documented in children's science
  references (BBC Bitesize, Britannica Kids, National Geographic Kids); the classic
  predator-prey oscillation is described from field studies of lynx and snowshoe hare and is
  summarized in age-appropriate form by those sources. The "dance, not drama" framing follows
  `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/philosophy.md` §5.
