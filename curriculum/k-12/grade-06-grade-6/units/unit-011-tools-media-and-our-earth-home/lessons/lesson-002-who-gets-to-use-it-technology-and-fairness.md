# Lesson 2 — Who Gets to Use It? Technology and Fairness

## Summary

Learners analyze **who gets to use** a technology and who does not, and why that matters for
**fairness**. A worked example follows one entrance through three doors — **cost, ability, and
availability** — showing how each lets some people through and not others, and how a **ramp**
opens the door for more. Learners then read a **school library computer, a water tap, a
wheelchair-accessible bus, or a mobile phone signal** the same way, framing access as a
fairness question people can act on — never as a verdict on any person or place.

## Objectives

- Analyze who gets to use a technology and who does not, and why that matters for fairness.
  _(D10.S1.06.02)_

## Connection

Think of a technology you use easily — a tap, a phone, a book, a road. Now ask a simple
question: who *cannot* use it? A person who cannot pay, a person whose body does not match the
design, a person who lives where it is not available. Who gets left out is not an accident of
the tool; it is a choice its design and its price made. Fairness means noticing that question —
and asking how to open the door wider.

## Materials

- Access doors poster
- Access question cards
- Access note page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the access doors poster.
- Copy access question cards for each pair and an access note page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **access analysis**:
name a technology, name who is in and who is out, name the barrier (cost, ability,
availability), and ask a fairness question. Model the entrance example fully as a worked
example. **Frame access as a fairness question, never a deficit story:** do not describe
people or regions as "lacking" or "behind"; describe *designs and prices* that leave people
out, and the real solutions — ramps, low-cost water kiosks, shared community phones, mobile
clinics — that people everywhere have already built. Distinguish evidence from values: *who
can and cannot use a tool* is observable; *that everyone deserves fair access* is a value this
curriculum holds. See `docs/philosophy.md` §4 (egalitarianism) and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson: tools are designed by people. Today's question: when a
   design is finished, *who gets to use it?* And who is left standing outside?
2. **Meet the three doors (12 min).** Look at the poster. One entrance, three doors. **Cost** —
   if it costs money, only those who can pay get through. **Ability** — if the entrance has
   only steps, a person who uses a wheelchair cannot get in. **Availability** — if it is not
   built where you live, you cannot reach it. Each door lets some people through and not
   others. Now the fairness move: add a **ramp** beside the steps, and more people get in —
   the design changed, and so did who belongs.
3. **Read your own card (18 min).** Take a card. The **school library computer** (in: learners
   with an account and time; out: those with no card, no electricity, or a screen they cannot
   see or use; fairness question: how does every learner get a turn and an accessible screen?).
   The **water tap** (in: people near clean piped water; out: people who carry water from far
   away; fairness question: how does clean water reach everyone?). The
   **wheelchair-accessible bus** (in: people who can reach the stop and board the low floor;
   out: people far from the route; fairness question: how does the route reach every
   neighborhood?). The **mobile phone signal** (in: people in covered areas who can afford a
   phone; out: people outside the signal or the price; fairness question: how does connection
   reach rural and remote places?). On your page, name the technology, who is in, who is out,
   and one fairness question — then one idea for opening the door.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Say it in one breath: "for ___, ___ can use it, ___ is
   left out, and the fairness question is ___." Ask: did your partner name a barrier *and* an
   idea for opening it?
5. **Close (5 min).** Access is not luck; it is a design choice. When you ask who is left out,
   you are asking a fairness question — the first step toward opening the door.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with the barrier named and a sentence starter for the fairness
  question; work one card aloud first.
- **Extension:** Compare two access solutions (a ramp vs. a fare discount, say) and argue which
  opens the door more fairly, and for whom.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name who is in and who is out for a technology,
  identify the barrier, and pose a fairness question?
- **Peer check:** Partners ask each other, "Did you name a barrier — cost, ability, or
  availability — and did you suggest a way to open access?"

## Home connection

Pick one technology your family uses. Ask: who in our community cannot use it, and what could
open it up — a lower price, a different design, or bringing it closer?

## Resources

- Access to technology varies by cost, disability, and geography, and design decisions (ramps,
  low-cost options, coverage) change who is included — documented by the World Bank's World
  Development Report on technology and by accessibility standards (the Web Content
  Accessibility Guidelines and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities).
  The framing of access as a fairness question, not a deficit story, follows
  `docs/philosophy.md` §4 and `docs/facilitation.md`.
