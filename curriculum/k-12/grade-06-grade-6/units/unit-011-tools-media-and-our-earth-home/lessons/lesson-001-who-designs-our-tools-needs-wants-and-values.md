# Lesson 1 — Who Designs Our Tools? Needs, Wants, and Values

## Summary

Learners explain that **technologies** are **designed** by people with particular **needs**,
**wants**, and **values**. A worked example traces the **bicycle** through its need (travel
farther and faster), its want (a smooth, fast ride), and its value (clean travel, no fuel).
Learners then read a **water filter, a solar lamp, a braille writing slate, or a fishing net**
the same way, seeing every tool as a person's or community's answer to what they needed and
cared about — never as a thing that simply appeared.

## Objectives

- Explain that technologies are designed by people with particular needs, wants, and values,
  and give an example. _(D10.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Hold any object near you — a cup, a shoe, a phone, a broom. Someone, somewhere, decided what
problem it would solve, how it should feel to use, and what mattered enough to build into it.
A tool is a person's thinking made solid. Today you learn to read a tool the way you would
read a person: what did its makers need, want, and value?

## Materials

- Design triangle poster (the bicycle)
- Design triangle cards (water filter, solar lamp, braille slate, fishing net)
- Design triangle page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the design triangle poster for the bicycle.
- Copy design triangle cards for each pair and a design triangle page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is **every technology is
someone's answer to a need, a want, and a value** — not magic, and not "just there." Model the
bicycle fully as a worked example before learners read their own card (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). Keep it global and egalitarian: choose objects that span cultures and regions (a
qanat, a dugout canoe, a braille slate) so no single place or people appears as the default
"inventor." Distinguish evidence from values: *what a tool does and the problem it solves* can
be investigated; *that clean travel or fair access matters* are values a designer chose to
build in. See `docs/philosophy.md` §4–5 and `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Recall from Grade 5: tools change daily life. Now ask the deeper
   question — *who* decided the tool would be this way? A tool is designed by people; it did
   not make itself.
2. **Meet the bicycle (12 min).** Look at the poster. Every design carries three things. The
   **need** — the problem it solves: carrying people and goods farther and faster than walking.
   The **want** — what makes it pleasant: a smooth, comfortable, fast ride. The **value** —
   what its makers believed mattered: clean travel, no fuel burned, no smoke. Say the triangle
   together: "need… want… value."
3. **Read your own tool (18 min).** Take a card. The **water filter** (need: safe water to
   drink; want: easy to use every day; value: everyone, even a small child, deserves clean
   water). The **solar lamp** (need: light after dark where there is no grid; want: cheap and
   sturdy; value: light should not depend on costly fuel). The **braille writing slate** (need:
   writing for people who are blind; want: small and portable; value: everyone should be able to
   read and write). The **fishing net** (need: catch fish to feed a family; want: light to
   throw and strong to hold; value: taking from the water without taking everything). On your
   page, write or draw the need, the want, and the value — and one sentence on *who* likely
   designed it and what they cared about.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Swap pages. Ask: "Does the triangle make sense? Did you
   name the need, the want, and the value — and the people behind it?" Fix anything unclear.
5. **Close (5 min).** A tool is not magic; it is a decision. When you ask "who designed this,
   and what did they care about?", you begin to see the people inside every object.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a card with the need already filled in and a sentence starter for the
  value; work one card aloud first.
- **Extension:** Compare two tools that answer the *same* need (a clay pot and a metal pump for
  water) and explain how different values shaped two different designs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a technology's need, want, and value, and
  point to the people who designed it?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I name the need, the want, and the value — and did I
  say who designed it and what they cared about?"

## Home connection

Choose one tool at home and ask someone who uses it: what need does it answer, what makes it
nice to use, and what value does it carry?

## Resources

- The idea that technologies encode their designers' needs and values is central to technology
  studies and is taught across cultures — from the qanat water channels of ancient Persia to the
  abacus and the dugout canoe; children's references (Britannica Kids, DK's *How Things Work*)
  document these designs and the problems they answer. The framing that design involves need,
  want, and value follows the curriculum's "technology as shaped by people, not magic"
  commitment (`docs/philosophy.md` §4). On modeling a routine before independent practice, see
  Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1>.
