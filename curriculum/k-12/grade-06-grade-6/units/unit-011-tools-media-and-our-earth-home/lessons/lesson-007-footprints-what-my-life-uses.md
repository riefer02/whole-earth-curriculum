# Lesson 7 — Footprints: What My Life Uses

## Summary

Learners explain what a **footprint** is — the **resources** a person or place uses — and
**compare** the footprints of two lifestyles as **information, not as a ranking**. A worked
example defines the footprint and lists the resources of two example lives (travel, food,
energy, things). Learners then compare a **city bus-rider, a rural farm family, a fishing
village, and a long-distance commuter**, resource by resource, **without shaming any life** —
different lives use different amounts, and knowing that is the first step to choosing.

## Objectives

- Explain what a footprint (the resources a person or place uses) is and compare the
  footprints of two different lifestyles. _(D10.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Every day your life reaches out to draw on the world: the food you eat, the water and energy
you use, the way you travel, the things you own and throw away. Together those draws make up
your **footprint** — the mark your life leaves on the Earth's resources. Two people can live
very different lives and leave very different marks. Today you learn to measure and compare
those marks — not to judge anyone, but to understand, so you can choose.

## Materials

- Footprint poster
- Lifestyle cards
- Footprint compare page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the footprint poster.
- Copy lifestyle cards for each pair and a footprint compare page for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land is the **footprint compare**:
define the footprint, list each life's resources (food, travel, energy, things), and compare
resource by resource. Model the two example lives fully as a worked example. **This comparison
must never shame any lifestyle or region.** A rural family that cooks over wood, a city dweller
who rides the bus, and a commuter who drives all face different realities and constraints —
none is "better" as a person because of the resources they use. The footprint is a measurement
of *systems and choices*, not a score on *character*. Distinguish evidence from values: *how
much each life uses* is measurable; *that we should use resources with care for others and the
future* is a value this curriculum holds. See `docs/philosophy.md` §4–5 and
`docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Ask: what did your life use *today* — food, water, energy, travel,
   things? Add them up and you have your footprint.
2. **Meet the footprint (12 min).** Look at the poster. A **footprint** is the resources a
   person or place uses. It has parts: **food** (where it comes from), **travel** (how you
   move), **energy** (cooking, light, heat), and **things** (what you buy and throw away).
   Compare two example lives. **Life A** walks and rides the bus, grows some food, buys mostly
   local, reuses and repairs. **Life B** drives a car daily, buys packaged food flown from far
   away, replaces things often. Life B's footprint is larger — that is a fact to *understand*,
   not a reason to *shame*. Neither life is better as a person.
3. **Compare your own (18 min).** Take a card set. The **city bus-rider** (travels by bus and
   foot; buys from nearby markets; lives in a shared flat) vs. the **rural farm family** (grows
   much of its food; cooks over wood or gas; travels by cart or motorbike). The **fishing
   village** (eats local fish and crops; travels by boat; few bought things) vs. the
   **long-distance commuter** (drives far daily; buys packaged food; many electric devices). On
   your compare page, list each life's food, travel, energy, and things — then write one
   sentence on what the comparison shows.
4. **Check with a partner (10 min).** Say: "___ uses more of ___ because ___." Then ask
   together: does saying a footprint is larger say anything about who is a *better person*?
   (No — it says something about the *choices and systems* around a life.)
5. **Close (5 min).** A footprint is a measurement, not a score. Understand yours, and you
   hold the first tool for choosing with care.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a compare page with one row pre-filled and a sentence starter for the
  comparison; work one card set aloud first.
- **Extension:** Estimate your own day's food, travel, energy, and things, and describe how one
  change (walking more, wasting less food, reusing a bag) would shrink your footprint.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define a footprint and compare two lifestyles
  resource by resource — without ranking the people as better or worse?
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Did I compare food, travel, energy, and things — and did I
  keep the comparison about *choices and systems*, not about who is a better person?"

## Home connection

With your family, list one day's food, travel, energy, and things. Ask: which part uses the
most, and what is one small change we could try?

## Resources

- The ecological footprint — the resources a person or place uses — is a standard concept from
  the Global Footprint Network and is taught in age-appropriate form by organizations such as
  WWF and the UN Environment Programme; the four-part breakdown (food, travel, energy, things)
  is a common classroom simplification. The explicit non-shaming framing follows
  `docs/philosophy.md` §4 (egalitarianism) and `docs/facilitation.md`.
