# Lesson 7 — Signs of Stress and One Self-Care Action

## Summary

Learners learn to **notice signs of stress or distress** in themselves — in the **body**,
the **feelings**, and the **behavior** — and choose **one simple self-care action** from a
menu, framed as **information, never weakness**, and never stigmatizing mental health.
They record one sign and one action as a portfolio artifact, setting up the capstone's
trusted-person plan.

## Objectives

- Identify signs of stress or distress in themselves and name one self-care action and
  one person to talk to. _(D03.S4.06.02)_

## Connection

A fire needs a smoke signal to warn you there is smoke; your body and feelings send you
signals too. When life gets heavy — a test, a change, a worry, too many things at once —
your body might feel **tired**, your **shoulders** might tighten, your **heart** might
race, and you might feel **worried** or **irritable** or find it hard to **sleep**. These
are not flaws and not failures; they are **signals** — your own early warning system,
telling you it is time to care for yourself. Noticing the signal is the first step.
Choosing one small **self-care action** is the second.

## Materials

- Signs of stress poster
- Self-care menu
- My self-care page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the "signs of stress" poster (body, feeling, and behavior signs, with the
  caption "signs are information, never weakness").
- Copy a self-care menu and a "my self-care" page for each learner.
- Recall Lesson 1 of this grade's movement unit: stress shows up as tension in the body,
  and there are ways to release it; today learners name the *signs* and choose one
  *self-care action*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **signs of
stress are information, not weakness — and a small self-care action helps**. Keep it
**empowering and non-stigmatizing**: never label a learner "stressed," "anxious," or
"depressed," and never suggest that struggling is a defect. Use ordinary language — "a lot
on your mind," "feeling heavy," "worried," "tired" — and treat stress as something every
person feels, at every age, in every culture. Frame **distress** gently as heavier,
longer-lasting stress that deserves extra care — and, crucially, that when feelings feel
too big to carry alone, **telling a trusted adult is itself self-care** (the capstone's
focus). Invite only safe, ordinary examples; a learner may carry heavy or ongoing stress,
so never require disclosure and always leave room to step away. The self-care actions must
be simple, free, and universal — water, slow breathing, movement, rest, talking to
someone, stepping away — never luxuries some learners cannot access. See
`docs/facilitation.md` on holding difficult topics safely.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Your body and feelings send you signals when life gets heavy. Today
   you learn to **notice the signs** — and choose **one self-care action** that helps.
2. **Meet the signs (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of the three
   kinds of signs:
   - **In the body:** feeling tired, tight shoulders, a racing heart, a churning stomach.
   - **In the feelings:** worry, irritability, feeling overwhelmed.
   - **In the behavior:** trouble sleeping, snapping at people, wanting to hide away.
   Notice the caption: these signs are **information, never weakness**. Everyone has them;
   they mean the body and mind are asking for care.
3. **Meet the menu (10 min).** Now the **self-care menu** — simple, free actions you can
   do almost anywhere: **drink water, breathe slowly, move your body, rest, talk to
   someone, step away** for a moment. These are not cures; they are small, kind steps that
   help your body and mind settle.
4. **Choose and try yours (15 min).** Think of one sign you sometimes notice in yourself.
   Write it on your page. Then choose one action from the menu and actually **try it once**
   right now — slow breaths, a stretch, a sip of water, a moment of quiet. Notice: does
   anything soften, even a little? That is self-care working.
5. **Close (5 min).** You are not broken when you feel stress — you are *human*. Noticing
   the sign and choosing one kind action is a skill you now own. Next time: the capstone —
   your trusted person and your whole self-care plan.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a picture menu of signs and actions to circle; scribe as needed;
  practice one action together slowly.
- **Extension:** Distinguish everyday **stress** (pressure that passes) from **distress**
  (heavier, longer-lasting) and write one sentence about why big feelings deserve both
  self-care *and* a trusted adult.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one sign of stress they notice in
  themselves and choose one self-care action?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The "my self-care" page (one sign + one action), kept to feed
  the capstone plan.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Can I name a sign I actually notice in myself, and
  does my chosen action help — even a little?"

## Home connection

At home, notice one sign of stress in yourself this week and try your self-care action,
then share with someone what you noticed and what helped.

## Resources

- That stress shows up as body sensations, feelings, and changes in behavior, and that
  simple self-care actions (slow breathing, movement, rest, water, and social support)
  help the body and mind settle, is well-established in stress and self-regulation
  research and in the CASEL self-awareness and self-management competencies
  (<https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>). The lesson holds "signs are information,
  never weakness" as both a factual framing and a value, and deliberately uses
  non-stigmatizing, everyday language for mental wellbeing.
