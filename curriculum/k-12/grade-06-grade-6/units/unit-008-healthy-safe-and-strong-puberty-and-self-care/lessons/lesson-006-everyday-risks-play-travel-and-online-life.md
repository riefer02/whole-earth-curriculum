# Lesson 6 — Everyday Risks: Play, Travel, and Online Life

## Summary

Learners assess **everyday risks** in three settings — **play, travel, and online life** —
and choose **safer actions** for each, building **agency without fear**. The online panel
covers the basics: keep **personal information private** and **tell a trusted adult** if
something feels wrong. They sort real situations, record one safer action per setting,
and learn that a risk is a *chance* of harm — one a safer choice can lower.

## Objectives

- Assess everyday risks in play, travel, and online life and choose safer actions.
  _(D03.S4.06.01)_

## Connection

Riding a bicycle is not dangerous in itself — but riding it into a busy road without
looking is. The difference is **choice**. Every day you meet small moments like this, in
three places: when you **play** (a game, a climb, a jump), when you **travel** (walking,
cycling, riding), and when you are **online** (a game, a chat, a post). A **risk** is not a
monster hiding in the dark; it is simply a *chance* that something could go wrong. And
you have real power over that chance — because in almost every moment, there is a **safer
choice** you can make.

## Materials

- Spot the safer choice poster
- Everyday risk cards
- My safer-choice page

## Preparation

- Print or draw the "spot the safer choice" poster (three panels — play, travel, online —
  each with a situation and a safer action).
- Copy a set of everyday risk cards and a "my safer-choice" page for each learner.
- Recall Grade 5: learners practiced emergency response and safe-and-kind online behavior;
  today they assess *everyday* risks and choose safer actions.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is **agency, not
fear**: a risk is a *chance* of harm, and a safer choice lowers the chance. Keep it
**non-frightening** — use everyday, manageable situations, never worst-case horror
stories, and always end on the safer action the learner *can* take. For **online** life,
land the two basics clearly and without panic: **never share personal information**
(name, address, school, phone number, passwords, and photos of yourself that reveal where
you are) with people online, and **tell a trusted adult** if anything online feels wrong,
scary, or too secret. Reassure learners that telling is not tattling and is never their
fault. Frame trusted adults broadly — a parent, grandparent, aunt or uncle, teacher,
coach, or health worker. Model one example from each setting as a **worked example**
before the card sort. See `docs/facilitation.md` and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** A risk is not a monster — it is a *chance* that something could go
   wrong. And in almost every moment, you have the power to make a **safer choice**. Today
   you practice, in three places: play, travel, and online.
2. **Meet the three settings (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** for
   each:
   - **Play:** warm up before a game, wear a helmet when you ride or skate, play fair.
   - **Travel:** look and listen before you cross, walk facing traffic where there is no
     footpath, wear a seatbelt or hold on safely when you ride.
   - **Online:** keep **personal information private** — your full name, address, school,
     phone number, and passwords — and if anything online feels wrong or scary or asks
     you to keep a secret, **tell a trusted adult**.
   Notice the pattern: **notice** the risk, **think**, **choose the safer way**.
3. **Sort the cards (15 min).** With a partner or small group, read each card and name
   the safer action. *A game with no warm-up?* Warm up first. *A stranger online asks
   where you live?* Do not answer; tell a trusted adult. *A road to cross?* Stop, look,
   listen, and cross when clear. There is always a safer choice.
4. **Record yours (10 min).** On your page, write one situation from each setting — play,
   travel, online — and the safer action you would choose. Make each one real for *your*
   life.
5. **Close (5 min).** You are not afraid of the world; you are *ready* for it. Notice,
   think, choose the safer way — and when something feels too big, a trusted adult is your
   backup, always. Next time: noticing stress in your own body and one self-care action.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide picture cards and a choice-of-two safer-action page; scribe as
  needed; act out rather than write.
- **Extension:** Explain that a risk is a *chance* of harm, not a certainty, and that a
  safer choice lowers the chance — then give one example of lowering a risk in each of
  the three settings.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner assess an everyday situation and choose a
  safer action in play, travel, and online life?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The "my safer-choice" page (one situation and safer action
  per setting), kept as a portfolio artifact.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "For each setting, did I name a real risk and a safer
  choice — and do I know a trusted adult to tell if something online feels wrong?"

## Home connection

Ask a trusted adult at home to name one everyday risk in each setting, and share your
safer choice for each — especially your rule to keep personal information private online.

## Resources

- Assessing everyday risk and choosing safer actions — look before crossing, wearing
  protection in play, and keeping personal information private online — reflects standard
  child-safety guidance: the World Health Organization's road-traffic-injury guidance
  (<https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries>), UNICEF's
  guidance on keeping children safe online
  (<https://www.unicef.org/protection/violence-against-children-online>), and the ITU's
  Child Online Protection guidance
  (<https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Cybersecurity/Pages/COP.aspx>). The lesson holds "tell a
  trusted adult" as both safety guidance and a right to protection, consistent with the
  UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 19.
