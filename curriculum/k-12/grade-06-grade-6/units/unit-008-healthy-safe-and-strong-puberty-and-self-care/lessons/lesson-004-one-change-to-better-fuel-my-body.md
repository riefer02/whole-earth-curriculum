# Lesson 4 — One Change to Better Fuel My Body

## Summary

Learners move from *noticing* their day of food and drink to choosing **one small, real
change that better fuels the body** — framed always as **additions and swaps, never
restrictions**, and never a judgment of any body or family. They meet **worked examples**,
choose a change that fits their own life, culture, and means, and write the **reason** and
a **first small step** as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Analyze a typical day of food and drink and identify one change that would better fuel
  the body. _(D03.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

A farmer improving a field does not tear the whole field up and start over. They make
**one careful change** — add a little water here, plant one more useful crop there — and
watch how it helps. Your body's "field" is your everyday eating, and it is already
growing well in its own way. So today you do not overhaul anything; you choose **one small
change** that gives your body a little more of what fuels it. Small and steady beats big
and short-lived — and a change that fits *your* life, *your* food, and *your* family is
the one that will stick.

## Materials

- One small change poster
- My one change page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the "one small change" poster (four choose-one options with drawings:
  drink more water, add a fruit or vegetable, choose a whole grain, eat a fuller
  breakfast).
- Copy a "my one change" page for each learner.
- Have learners bring or recall their "my day" page from Lesson 3.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **the best
change is one small, real change that fits the learner's own life** — never a rule from
outside. Frame every option as an **addition or a swap, never a restriction**: we never
tell a learner to cut out a food, restrict eating, or shrink their body. This is
especially important at ages 11–14, when food and body-image pressures rise; keep the
message body-positive and food-neutral ("more fuel for your growing body," never "eat
less" or "get thinner"). Let learners choose only what is genuinely available and
affordable in their home and culture, and honor that some learners have little control
over what is on the table — for them, "add water when you can" is a perfectly good
change. Model the choose-one flow as a **worked example** first. See `docs/facilitation.md`
and the safety notes on body image in `docs/development.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Last lesson you looked at your day of food and drink with
   curiosity. Today you choose **one small change** that better fuels your growing body —
   and it is yours to choose.
2. **Meet the choices (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of four
   choose-one options, all additions or swaps:
   - **Drink more water** — water helps every cell work.
   - **Add a fruit or vegetable** — they carry vitamins and minerals.
   - **Choose a whole grain** — whole grains give lasting fuel.
   - **Eat a fuller breakfast** — morning fuel sets the day.
   Notice what is *not* on the list: nothing says "eat less" or "cut out" a food. This is
   about **adding fuel**, never taking away.
3. **Choose yours (5 min).** Look back at your "my day" page from last time. Which one
   change would fit *your* life best? Maybe you drink little water — choose that. Maybe
   your table already holds plenty of fruit — choose something else. There is no wrong
   answer, only *your* answer.
4. **Write it (20 min).** On your page, write: **my one change** (what you will do); **my
   reason** (how it fuels your body); and **my first step** (one small, real thing to do
   this week). Keep it small enough to actually do — that is what makes it real.
5. **Close (5 min).** A body growing through puberty needs good fuel, and you have just
   chosen one steady way to give it more. Small and steady wins. Next time: where your
   food comes from, and the people and planet behind every plate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a picture-choice page and a sentence bank; scribe the reason and
  first step; allow one drawing and one word.
- **Extension:** Connect the chosen change to a nutrient — water for every cell, a fruit
  for vitamins, a whole grain for lasting fuel — and name the nutrient in one sentence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner choose one small, realistic change and
  say, in their own words, how it better fuels the body?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The "my one change" page (change + reason + first step), kept
  as a portfolio artifact.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Is my change real and small enough to do? Does it add
  fuel, not take away? Does it fit my life and my family's food?"

## Home connection

Share your one change with someone at home — and, if you can, do your first step together
this week.

## Resources

- Choosing an *addition* (more water, a fruit or vegetable, a whole grain) rather than a
  restriction reflects the World Health Organization's "Healthy diet" guidance
  (<https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet>), which frames healthy
  eating around what to *include* (a diversity of foods, water, whole grains) rather than
  deprivation. The lesson holds "no restriction, no body judgment" as both a health
  position and a value, in line with the curriculum's commitment to teach the body with
  dignity and never shame any body or way of eating.
