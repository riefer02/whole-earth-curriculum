# Lesson 5 — From Farm to Table: How Food Is Produced and Transported

## Summary

Learners trace food from where it grows to their table — **grow, harvest, transport, and
eat** — and meet the **people** at each step and the **environment** it draws on (soil,
water, fuel, packaging). They trace one food's own journey and name **one effect on
people** and **one effect on the environment**, carrying the technology-and-environment
lens: every plate connects us to the land and to the hands that grew and moved our food.

## Objectives

- Explain how food is produced and transported and how this affects people and the
  environment. _(D03.S3.06.02)_

## Connection

Hold up a piece of food — a grain of rice, a slice of bread, a fruit. Before it reached
you, it had a whole life and a whole journey. Somewhere, soil and water and sunlight grew
it; someone planted and tended it; someone harvested it; and it traveled — by foot, by
cart, by truck, by boat, or by train — to the market or garden where your family got it.
Whether your food comes from a field you can see from your window or from across an
ocean, **every plate connects you to the land and to the hands that grew and moved your
food**. Today you learn to see that hidden journey.

## Materials

- Farm-to-table journey poster
- One food's journey page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the farm-to-table journey poster (grow → harvest → transport → table,
  with people and environment labeled at each step).
- Copy a "one food's journey" page for each learner.
- Recall Grade 5: learners named the nutrients and learned where common foods come from
  (plants, animals, farms, gardens); today they follow the full production-and-transport
  chain and its effects.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land is that **food is
produced and transported by real people using land, water, and fuel — so what we eat
affects both people and the environment**. Keep it **concrete and hopeful, never
frightening or preachy**: the point is awareness and gratitude, not guilt. Honor every
source of food — a family garden, a small farm, a big farm, a market, a shop — and never
rank them. Name the **workers** with dignity (farmers, harvesters, drivers, sellers) and
note that many hands make one meal. For the environment, keep the effects simple and
true: food needs **soil and water** to grow and **fuel** to travel, and **packaging** can
become waste. This is the unit's clearest **technology-and-environment** lesson, so make
the lens explicit, but always attach it to **agency** — "when we know, we can choose
wisely" — not despair. See `docs/facilitation.md` and the environment lens in
`docs/philosophy.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of one food you ate yesterday. Where did it come from? Before
   today you might say "the shop" or "the garden." Today you follow it all the way back —
   to the soil, the water, and the hands that made it.
2. **Meet the journey (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of the food
   journey, in four steps:
   - **Grow:** a farmer plants and tends a crop. It needs **soil, water, and sunlight**.
   - **Harvest:** a worker picks or gathers it when it is ready.
   - **Transport:** it travels by truck, boat, or train — and that takes **fuel**.
   - **Eat:** it reaches a table, and a family shares it.
   Notice the two things running through every step: **people** (the farmer, the
   harvester, the driver, the seller) and the **environment** (soil, water, fuel, and
   sometimes packaging).
3. **Trace one food (20 min).** Choose one food from your own table. On your page, trace
   its journey: where might it have grown? Who might have grown and picked it? How did it
   travel to reach you? Write or draw each step, and then answer two questions: **one
   effect on people** (many hands worked to bring it to you) and **one effect on the
   environment** (it used soil and water to grow, fuel to travel, and maybe packaging).
4. **Compare near and far (5 min).** Some food grows close to home; some travels far. Food
   grown nearby may use less fuel to reach you; food from far away may bring tastes no
   local field can grow. Neither is "bad" — each is a choice with costs and gifts.
5. **Close (5 min).** Every plate is a map: it points to the land that fed it and the
   people who carried it. Knowing the journey lets you choose with care and eat with
   thanks. Next time: keeping yourself safe in play, travel, and online life.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn journey with blanks to fill and picture prompts;
  scribe the two effects; allow drawing the whole journey.
- **Extension:** Compare a local food and a far-traveled food, and name one cost and one
  benefit of each for people and the environment.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the steps of producing and
  transporting food and give one effect on people and one on the environment?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The "one food's journey" page, kept as a portfolio artifact.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Is my journey in the right order, and did I name the
  people and the environment at each step — without judging any source of food?"

## Home connection

At your next meal, ask someone at home where one food on the table came from, and trace
its journey together — from soil and hands to your plate.

## Resources

- That food is produced and transported by people using land, water, and fuel, and that
  this affects both people and the environment, is documented by the FAO's work on
  sustainable food value chains (<https://www.fao.org/sustainable-food-value-chains/en/>)
  and its State of Food and Agriculture reports
  (<https://www.fao.org/publications/home/fao-flagship-publications/the-state-of-food-and-agriculture/en>).
  The lesson frames the journey as a fact to understand and a reason for care and
  gratitude (a value), keeping evidence and values distinct; it attaches awareness to
  agency rather than guilt, in line with the curriculum's technology-and-environment lens.
