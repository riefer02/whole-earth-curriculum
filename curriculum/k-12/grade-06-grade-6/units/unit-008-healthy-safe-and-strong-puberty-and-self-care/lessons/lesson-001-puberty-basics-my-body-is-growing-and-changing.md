# Lesson 1 — Puberty Basics: My Body Is Growing and Changing

## Summary

Learners meet the **basic, factual changes of puberty** — the **growth spurt**, **body
hair**, **oilier skin**, the **voice**, and the reproductive changes (**menstruation**
for bodies with a uterus; **erections** and sometimes **wet dreams** for bodies with
testes). They learn that these changes are driven by **hormones** and unfold
**gradually, over several years** — never all at once. Every change is taught as normal,
with dignity and without shame; every body is honored, and questions are welcomed.

## Objectives

- Describe the basic changes of puberty and that they happen at different times for
  different people. _(D03.S1.06.01)_

## Connection

Think of a seed becoming a plant. A seed does not turn into a tall plant in one night; it
grows a root, then a stem, then leaves — step by step, over time. Your body is doing
something similar right now, in its own way: it is growing from a child's body into an
adult body. This long, gradual change is called **puberty**. It begins at different ages
for different people — for most, somewhere between about 8 and 15 — and it brings a set
of changes that prepare the body for adult life. Whether you grow up in a busy city, a
village, or on a farm, the same changes come — each body simply arrives at them on its
own schedule.

## Materials

- Puberty changes poster
- What I notice page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the puberty changes poster (a body with labeled arrows to each change,
  plus the two reproductive-change notes, and the caption "changes happen gradually over
  years").
- Copy a "what I notice" page for each learner.
- Prepare a private "wonder box" (a covered box or envelope) where learners can place
  written, unsigned questions.
- Recall Grade 5: learners learned that bodies grow and change at different rates and
  that every pace is normal; today they learn *what* those changes are.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is **factual, body-positive, and
non-shaming** — the tone matters as much as the facts. Present the changes plainly, as
normal biology, with no giggling invitation and no embarrassment; if a learner is
embarrassed, meet it with calm warmth ("every adult you know went through this"). Use
**inclusive, accurate language**: some bodies have a **uterus and ovaries** and will
experience **menstruation**; some bodies have **testes** and will experience **erections**
and sometimes **wet dreams**. Do not sort learners by sex, and never compare or rank
bodies. Emphasize that these changes happen **gradually** and at **different times for
different people** (the heart of the next lesson). Keep the reproductive content at the
basic factual level of the standard — no graphic detail, no sexual content — and remind
learners that a growing body is not ready to be a parent; these changes are the body
*preparing* for a future that is still far off. Where family or community norms differ,
adapt respectfully and encourage learners to take questions to a trusted adult at home.
See `docs/facilitation.md` on holding sensitive topics safely and `docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have already learned that bodies grow and change at different
   rates. Today you learn the name for this big change — **puberty** — and the basic
   changes it brings. It is normal, it is gradual, and it happens to everyone.
2. **Meet the changes (15 min).** Look at the poster. Puberty is driven by **hormones** —
   chemical messengers the body makes — that tell the body to grow and change. The main
   changes:
   - **Growth spurt:** you grow taller and heavier, faster than before.
   - **Body hair:** hair grows under the arms, on the legs, and in the pubic area.
   - **Skin:** the skin makes more oil and sweat, so it may feel oilier and **pimples**
     can appear.
   - **Voice:** for some bodies the voice gets lower and may "crack" while it changes.
   - **Reproductive changes:** for bodies with a **uterus**, **menstruation** (a monthly
     period of blood and fluid) begins; for bodies with **testes**, **erections** happen,
     sometimes with **wet dreams** during sleep.
   Notice the caption: none of this happens all at once. It unfolds **gradually, over
   several years**, and it begins at a different age for each person.
3. **Worked example (5 min).** Think of one change — say, the growth spurt. One person
   may shoot up taller at 10; another may not start until 14. Both are normal. The body
   keeps its own clock; there is no "right" or "wrong" time, only *your* time.
4. **What I notice (15 min).** On your page, write or draw in the first column the
   changes you already know about — perhaps from your own body or from an older sibling.
   In the second column, write any **questions** you have. No question is silly, and no
   one must share theirs aloud. If you prefer, place your questions in the **wonder box**,
   unsigned.
5. **Close (5 min).** Puberty is not a problem to hide; it is a body growing the way
   bodies do — with dignity and at its own pace. Next time: why *every* pace is normal.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a picture-word bank of the changes and a page with pre-drawn
  arrows; learners circle instead of write; scribe questions as needed.
- **Extension:** Explain that hormones are the chemical messengers behind puberty, and
  name one reason the same change can begin earlier or later in different people (family
  patterns, nutrition, and many other factors).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least three basic changes of
  puberty and say, in their own words, that they happen gradually and at different times
  for different people?
- **Self-check:** The learner reviews their "what I notice" page and asks, "Can I name
  the changes plainly, without shame, and do I know a trusted adult I could ask?"

## Home connection

At home, if it feels comfortable, ask a trusted adult one question from your "what I
notice" page — and share one change you now understand better.

## Resources

- The basic changes of puberty — the growth spurt, body hair, oilier skin, the voice, and
  the reproductive changes of menstruation and erections — are standard, well-established
  adolescent biology, described in the World Health Organization's adolescent health and
  development resources (<https://www.who.int/health-topics/adolescent-health>) and in
  widely used, age-appropriate puberty guides. These are *facts about how bodies work*,
  never judgments about any body, and the curriculum holds the timing of each change as
  normal across the full human range. For the practice of naming questions safely and
  managing the feelings that can come with change, see the CASEL framework:
  <https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/>.
