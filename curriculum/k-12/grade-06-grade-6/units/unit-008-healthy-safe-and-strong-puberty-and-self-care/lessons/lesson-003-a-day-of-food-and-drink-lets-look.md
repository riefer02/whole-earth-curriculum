# Lesson 3 — A Day of Food and Drink: Let's Look

## Summary

Learners begin the nutrition half of the unit by doing what scientists do first:
**collecting data with curiosity instead of judgment**. They record a **typical day of
food and drink** — morning, midday, afternoon, evening, and drinks — from their own real
life, meeting **worked examples** from several regions, and set the stage for choosing
**one change that better fuels the body** in the next lesson. Every way of eating is
honored; no food, body, or family is judged.

## Objectives

- Analyze a typical day of food and drink and identify one change that would better fuel
  the body. _(D03.S3.06.01)_

## Connection

A mapmaker does not change a landscape until they have first *looked* at it carefully.
Before you decide anything about how you eat, you first **look** at what you actually eat
and drink — with curiosity, the way a scientist records what they see. Your day of food
might look very different from a friend's: yours might hold rice, beans, and fruit; a
friend's might hold flatbread, lentils, and tea; another's might hold maize, fish, and
water. There is no one "right" plate. The first step is simply to **see** your own day
clearly — because you can only care for what you notice.

## Materials

- A day of food and drink poster
- My day page
- Pencils

## Preparation

- Print or draw the "day of food and drink" poster (four time sections plus a drinks row,
  with example foods from several regions).
- Copy a "my day" page for each learner.
- Recall Grade 5: learners learned the six nutrients and their jobs; today they apply
  that by *recording* what they actually eat and drink.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea to land is that **analyzing
starts with honestly noticing** — recording a real day, not an idealized one. Guard the
**no-shame** rule at every step: there are no "good" or "bad" foods in this activity,
only *information*. A learner's day reflects what their family has, can afford, and eats
by culture and habit — never a personal failing. Use **food examples from many regions**
(rice, maize, cassava, plantain, bread, beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, fish, eggs,
milk, fruit, vegetables, nuts, tea, water) so every home's table belongs. Model one day
as a **worked example** before learners record their own, and never require anyone to
share their day aloud if they do not wish to. See `docs/facilitation.md` and
`docs/contexts.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You know the nutrients — carbohydrates for fuel, proteins to
   build, fats to store and build, plus vitamins, minerals, and water. Today you use that
   knowledge by first **looking** at a real day of food and drink.
2. **Meet the poster (10 min).** Look at the poster — a **worked example** of one
   recorded day. It has four time sections — **morning, midday, afternoon, evening** —
   and a **drinks** row. Notice the foods come from many places: rice and beans,
   flatbread and vegetables, fruit, soup, nuts, a cup of water. Your day does not need to
   match this one; it needs to be *yours*, recorded truly.
3. **Record your day (20 min).** Think of a **typical** recent day — not a special feast
   day, just an ordinary one. In each box on your page, write or draw what you ate and
   drank. Include the little things: the fruit you grabbed, the water or tea you drank,
   the snack between meals. Be honest — this is **data about you**, not a test to pass.
4. **Look for a pattern (5 min).** Read your day back to yourself. What do you notice?
   Do you drink much water? Do fruits and vegetables show up? When do you feel most
   hungry? Just notice — no judgment. This noticing is the **analysis**.
5. **Close (5 min).** You have looked at your day clearly and kindly. Next time you will
   choose **one change** that better fuels your body — a small, real step that is yours.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide pre-drawn boxes with food pictures to circle, and scribe as
  needed; allow drawing instead of writing.
- **Extension:** Notice a pattern across the day — when you eat, what you drink most, or
  which food groups appear often — and write one sentence naming that pattern.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner record a typical day of food and drink in
  the four time sections plus drinks, without judgment?
- **Portfolio (artifact):** The completed "my day" page, kept to guide the next lesson's
  one change.
- **Self-check:** The learner asks, "Is this day recorded truthfully and completely — and
  did I notice it with curiosity, not criticism?"

## Home connection

At home, ask someone what a typical day of food looks like for them, and share one thing
you noticed about your own day.

## Resources

- Recording and reflecting on what one actually eats and drinks is the descriptive first
  step in nutrition education, distinct from judging; the World Health Organization's
  "Healthy diet" fact sheet (<https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet>)
  and the FAO's food-based dietary guidelines
  (<https://www.fao.org/nutrition/education/food-dietary-guidelines/en/>) describe the
  wide variety of healthy ways people eat around the world. These describe what food
  *does* and what healthy patterns look like across cultures — never a judgment about any
  body or family.
