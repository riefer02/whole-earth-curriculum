# Lesson 2 — Taking Turns in a Conversation

## Summary

Learners practice the give-and-take of real conversation: taking turns, staying on
one topic, and speaking clearly enough to be understood. A talking object marks
whose turn it is, so everyone gets to speak and everyone gets to be heard.

## Objectives

- Take turns in a conversation, staying on topic and speaking clearly.
  _(D04.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

A conversation is like passing a ball: you throw an idea, someone catches it and
throws one back. If everyone talks at once, no one is heard; if the ball flies off
to a new game every second, the first game is lost. Taking turns — and keeping the
same topic — is how people plan a meal, share news, and decide things together, in
every language on Earth.

## Materials

- A talking object (stone, stick, or soft ball)
- Topic cards (a simple topic with a small picture on each)

## Preparation

- Choose a talking object that is safe and easy to pass.
- Prepare three or four topic cards with everyday topics.
- Arrange a circle or facing pairs.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Model a two-turn exchange first, then let learners practice.
Stay-on-topic means the ideas connect; a gentle redirect ("let's stay with the
market story") is help, not scolding. Speaking clearly does not mean speaking in a
"correct" accent — every accent, dialect, and way of speaking is honored; "clear"
means the listener can understand, and the learner decides how to get there. A
learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing takes turns in sign or with a visual turn
marker; a learner who does not use a speaking voice "speaks" by signing, pointing to
pictures, or using a communication device — their turn counts exactly the same.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle or with a partner. Hold the talking object.
   Only the person holding it speaks; everyone else listens.
2. **Meet the rules of a turn (5 min).** A conversation has three moves: **take a
   turn** (speak when it is your turn), **stay on the topic** (keep the ideas
   connected), and **speak clearly** (so your partner understands).
3. **Watch a model (5 min).** Watch two turns modeled: one person speaks about the
   topic, then passes the object; the other adds an idea on the same topic, then
   passes it back.
4. **Practice taking turns (20 min).** Pick a topic card. Pass the talking object
   around. When you hold it, add one idea on the topic and speak clearly; then pass
   it along. Keep going until everyone has had several turns.
5. **Notice the topic (5 min).** When a turn wanders far from the topic, gently
   bring it back — "that reminds me, but let's stay with our topic."
6. **Close (5 min).** A good conversation is fair: everyone gets a turn, and the
   ideas stay connected. That is how we think together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a timer or chime to mark each turn; model a one-word or
  one-picture turn for learners still building sentences.
- **Support (non-speaking):** A turn can be a signed idea, a pointed-to picture, or
  a device message — it is still a turn.
- **Extension:** Hold a longer conversation and gently practice a "return" — when
  someone adds an idea, connect your next turn to theirs.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Does the learner wait for their turn, add an
  idea on the same topic, and speak (or sign, or show) clearly enough to be
  understood?

## Home connection

Have a two-turn conversation with someone at home about what you will eat tonight —
one idea each, staying on the topic, taking turns.
