# Lesson 1 — Wonder Becomes a Prediction

## Summary

Last year learners noticed things and asked "why?" and "how?" and made a guess they
could check. This year, wonder grows a step further: into a **prediction** — a guess
you say clearly and plan to test. Learners turn a shared wonder into a question, offer
their own prediction, and name how they will test it. Being wrong is welcomed, because
a wrong guess is the start of finding out.

## Objectives

- Make a prediction, test it by doing, and tell whether the result matched the
  prediction. _(D06.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

You drop a seed in a cup of soil and you wonder: *Will it grow?* You drop a leaf in a
puddle and you wonder: *Will it float?* A scientist does not stop at wondering — she
says her guess out loud: "I predict it will float because it is light." A **prediction**
is a guess you can test. It is not about being right; it is about finding out, and
being wrong is how we learn.

## Materials

- One thing everyone can wonder about (a seed, a floating leaf, a toy car, a shadow, or
  a cup of water)
- A class "wonder board" or large paper
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Choose one thing everyone can look at and wonder about together — something safe and
  close at hand, indoors or out.
- Prepare the wonder board with two columns: "I wonder…" and "I predict…".

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"), so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Make predicting safe and joyful: say plainly that a prediction
does not have to be right — "a wrong prediction is still a great prediction, because
now we can test it." Never let a guess be mocked or ranked. Where questioning adults is
not safe or usual, frame this as *wondering together*, not challenging anyone, and offer
a written or drawn "wonder box" so learners can share without speaking up (see
`docs/contexts.md`). The goal is the habit: wonder → question → prediction → a way to
test. Some questions we cannot test yet — and that is wonderful, not a failure. This
lesson introduces the skill with a worked example and guided practice: you model the
chain once, then learners try it with a partner before working alone (Kirschner,
Sweller & Clark, 2006).

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Last year you noticed things and asked "why?" and
   "how?" What is one thing you noticed and wondered about? Today your wonder grows
   into a **prediction**.
2. **Wonder together (10 min).** Look at the thing together. Turn what you notice into a
   question: "I wonder if it will float." "I wonder if it will grow." "I wonder how far
   it will roll." There are no silly questions — write or draw them on the wonder board.
3. **Predict (15 min).** For one question, say your own guess out loud: "I predict it
   will float because it is light." A prediction is a guess you can **test**. Say it,
   sign it, point to a picture, or have it written for you. Then ask: how could we test
   it? "We could drop it in water and watch." "We could plant it and wait."
4. **Draw your prediction (15 min).** On your paper, draw three little boxes: **I
   wonder**, **I predict**, and **I can test by**. Fill them in with words or pictures.
5. **Close (5 min).** Notice, ask, predict, test — that is the rhythm of science. Keep
   your prediction card; tomorrow we will test it and see.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer the spoken frame "I wonder ___" and "I predict ___" and fill it in
  together with a partner.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to a picture of the question and the prediction, or
  act the prediction out.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Wonder about a thing you can touch or hear (a seed, a
  shell, a bell) instead of a shadow; a partner names it aloud as you explore it.
- **Extension:** Write a second prediction for the same object and predict which test
  would be the better check and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner turn a shared wonder into a question and
  offer a prediction they could test?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The drawn "I wonder… I predict… I can test by…" page.

## Home connection

At home, wonder about one thing out loud — "I wonder if this seed will grow in a cup on
the window?" — then tell a grown-up your prediction. You can even plan how to test it
together.
