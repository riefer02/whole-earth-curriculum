# Lesson 6 — Materials and Their Jobs

## Summary

Everything we make is made of a **material** — wood, metal, cloth, glass, stone, paper,
plastic — and every material has **properties**: it is hard or soft, strong or weak,
clear or cloudy, stiff or bendy. Learners feel and name these properties, sort materials
by them, and explain how a property makes a material just right for a certain **job**. We
choose materials on purpose, and that choosing is a quiet kind of technology people do
everywhere.

## Objectives

- Sort materials by their properties and explain how properties make them useful for
  different jobs. _(D06.S3.02.01)_

## Connection

Your spoon does not melt in hot soup, and your blanket is soft against your skin. That is
not luck — people chose those **materials** for those jobs. Metal is **strong** and does
not melt easily, so it makes a good spoon. Cloth is **soft** and bendy, so it makes a
good blanket. When you pick a material because of what it is like, you are thinking like
a maker, a builder, and a scientist all at once.

## Materials

- A set of real objects made of different materials (a wooden block, a metal spoon, a
  cloth, a plastic or glass cup, a rubber band, a paper sheet)
- A "material and job" matching chart
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather safe objects with clearly different properties.
- Prepare the matching chart (property on one side, job on the other).
- Plan one worked example: feel a material, name its property, name its job.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill is a chain — **feel the
property → name it → connect it to a job** — so model the whole chain once, then guide,
then release. Use real objects learners can touch; touch is the concrete anchor. Honor
global variety: houses are made of mud brick, wood, stone, or ice in different places,
and cups of clay, glass, gourd, or metal — the same job done by different materials,
each chosen for what the place has and what the job needs. A light environmental and
technology lens: some materials can be used again and again, and some are thrown away —
wonder together about choosing materials that last and can be reused, without fear or
blame. Note the safety of glass for very young hands and swap in a clear plastic cup
where needed.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** We sorted living things by where they live. Now we
   sort the things people *make* — by what they are **made of**.
2. **Feel and name properties (15 min).** Touch each object. What is it like? **Hard** or
   **soft**? **Strong** or **weak**? **Clear** or **cloudy**? **Stiff** or **bendy**?
   Say each property and sort the objects into groups by it.
3. **Match materials to jobs (15 min).** Why do we choose each material? "Wood is hard
   and strong, so it is good for a chair." "Cloth is soft, so it is good for a blanket."
   Match each material to a job and tell a partner your reason.
4. **One job, many materials (10 min).** Pick one job — a cup, a mat, a roof. How many
   different materials could do it? Which would you choose, and why?
5. **Close (5 min).** A material's **property** tells us its **job**. People everywhere
   choose materials on purpose — and now you can too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Feel two materials (soft cloth, hard wood) and match each to one simple
  job with the frame "It is ___, so it is good for ___."
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the property word and the job after feeling each
  material.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Identify materials by touch — rough, smooth, heavy,
  light, bendy — and name the job by telling.
- **Extension:** Compare two materials for the same job (wood vs. metal spoon) and
  explain which you would choose and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two material properties and
  connect one to a job ("It is ___ so it is good for ___")?
- **Peer:** Partners check each other's material-to-job matches and reasons.
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "material and job" matching page.

## Home connection

At home, find three things made of different materials. Feel each one and tell a grown-up
its property and its job: "This bowl is smooth and strong, so it is good for holding
soup."
