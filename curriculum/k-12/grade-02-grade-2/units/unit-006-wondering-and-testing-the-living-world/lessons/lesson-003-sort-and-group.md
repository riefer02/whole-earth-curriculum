# Lesson 3 — Sort and Group

## Summary

The living world is full of many things. Science's first way of making sense of them is
to **sort** — to put things into **groups** by a **feature** they share. Learners sort a
collection by shape, color, size, or where something lives, and — the important part —
explain *how* they grouped them. They learn that the same things can be sorted many
different ways, and none of those ways is wrong.

## Objectives

- Sort and group objects or events by one or more features and explain how they grouped
  them. _(D06.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

At home you already sort things: spoons with spoons, socks with socks, seeds with seeds.
When you put all the round stones in one pile and all the flat stones in another, you are
doing what a scientist does — sorting by a **feature**. The trick is to say *how* you
sorted: "I put all the round ones together." That explanation is how others understand
your thinking.

## Materials

- A collection of things to sort (leaves, shells, stones, seeds, buttons, or picture
  cards of living things)
- Sorting circles, trays, or drawn hoops
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather a collection with clear, safe features to sort by (shape, color, size, texture).
- Set out two or three sorting spaces (trays, hoops, or chalk rings).
- Plan a quick first example to model sorting and explaining the rule aloud.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill here is not just sorting — it is
**explaining the rule** ("I grouped them by shape"), which is where thinking becomes
visible. Model once aloud (worked example), then guide, then release. Honor that there is
no single "right" way: the same leaf could go with "green things" or with "long things,"
and both are good — what matters is that the learner can say the rule. Include objects
and living things from many places (desert, sea, forest, mountain) so sorting carries a
global lens. Sorting by "where it lives" quietly previews habitats, which arrive in
Lesson 5 — this is intentional spacing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** What did you predict and test these last two days?
   Today we use a new science tool: **sorting**.
2. **Sort together (10 min).** Look at the collection. Choose one **feature** — shape,
   color, or size. Put the things into groups by that feature. Say the rule out loud: "I
   am putting all the long leaves here and all the round leaves there — I sorted by
   **shape**."
3. **Sort your own (15 min).** Take a set yourself. Choose your own feature and sort the
   things into two or three groups. When you finish, tell a partner your rule: "I sorted
   these by **color** — all the brown stones together."
4. **Sort another way (10 min).** Now sort the *same* set by a *different* feature. How
   did the groups change? Tell your partner: "This time I sorted by **size**, not color."
5. **Draw your sort (7 min).** Draw your two sorts and label each with the feature you
   used — "by shape," "by color," "by size."
6. **Close (3 min).** Sorting is how a scientist begins to make sense of many things.
   There is no one right way — there is only the way you can explain.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort with one clear feature (shape) and use the spoken frame "I put all
  the ___ together."
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort by pointing or moving objects, then point to a picture
  of the feature (a color patch, a shape) to show the rule.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sort by touch — rough and smooth, heavy and light, round
  and flat — with a partner naming each object.
- **Extension:** Sort one set by two features at once (e.g., color *and* size) and
  explain how the groups overlap.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a collection by at least one feature
  and say the rule they used?
- **Peer:** Partners restate each other's sorting rule in their own words.
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The labeled "my sorts" drawing.

## Home connection

At home, help sort one real thing — the spoons, the socks, or the seeds — and say your
rule out loud: "I am grouping these by…" Sorting is everywhere, and you already know how.
