# Lesson 1 — A Timeline of Our Community

## Summary

Learners build a simple timeline — a line that orders events from the past toward
today — and place three or four events from their family's or community's recent past
in order. They explain the order using **before** and **after**. This is the first
real historian's skill at this grade: telling what came first and what came next.

## Objectives

- Order a few events from their family's or community's recent past on a simple
  timeline and explain the order. _(D08.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

You already know that a day has an order: you wake, you eat, you play, you rest. A
**timeline** does the same thing for bigger stretches of time — it lines up events
that happened before today, one after another, so we can see which came first. Your
family and your community have their own order of recent events: a baby was born,
then a new building opened, then a festival happened. Putting them in order is how
people begin to tell the story of time.

## Materials

- A long strip of paper or a drawn line (one per learner)
- A set of "community events" cards for the worked example

## Preparation

- Draw or gather simple cards for recent community events (a new building, a
  festival, a baby being born, a storm, a harvest).
- Give each learner a timeline strip with a "long ago" end and a "now" end.
- Decide whether the line reads left-to-right or top-to-bottom, and keep it the same
  for everyone.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. **Ordering is a foundational skill, so teach it with a worked
example first** (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model placing three community
events on the line, saying "this came first... then... then...," before learners do
their own. **Keep events recent, local, and gentle** — a baby being born, a festival,
a new road or school — never frightening or private material. Honor every body and
every family shape: a learner who is blind or has low vision sequences real objects
by touch and says or signs the order; a learner who does not use a speaking voice
points or acts the order. Some families have moved, or have members far away — every
family's order of recent events is real and worthy, and none is "correct." Remember
that different peoples mark time in different ways (many calendars, many ways of
naming years) — a timeline is one tool among many, not the only truth about time.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and remember (5 min).** Think back: what is one thing that happened in
   your family or your town not long ago — a celebration, a new baby, a change? Did it
   happen before or after something else you remember?
2. **Meet the timeline (10 min).** Look at the worked example. A line with a "long
   ago" end and a "now" end. Three cards: a new building opening, a festival, a baby
   being born. Place them in order, saying the words aloud together: "The baby was
   born **before** the festival. The new building opened **after** the baby was born."
3. **Guided practice (10 min).** Mix the cards. Together, put them back in order on
   the line. For each card, say "this came before..." or "this came after..." so the
   order has a reason.
4. **Build your own timeline (15 min).** Choose three or four real events from your
   family's or community's recent past. Draw or show each one on its spot along your
   line, from "long ago" (the furthest back) to "now" (the most recent).
5. **Explain your order (5 min).** Point to each event and tell why it is there:
   "This came first because... this came after that because..." You may point, act,
   draw, or sign.
6. **Close (5 min).** You just did what historians do: you ordered events and
   explained the order. A timeline is a story of time told in order.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only three events, and place them together with clear gestures
  toward "before" and "after."
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sequence real objects (a shell, a stone, a leaf) by
  touch and say or sign the order aloud; a partner reads the labels.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Order picture cards or objects, and point to "before"
  and "after" spots instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are already pictures — read the order with your
  eyes and hands, and sign "before" and "after" as you place each card.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large, stable line and bigger cards; point or
  eye-gaze to each spot while a partner moves the card, or name the order while
  someone else places it.
- **Extension:** Add a fifth event, and tell two "before/after" pairs ("the storm
  came before the harvest; the festival came after").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place three or four recent events on
  the timeline in a sensible order, from furthest back to most recent?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner explain the order using at least
  one "before" and one "after" — by speaking, signing, pointing, or acting?

## Home connection

At home, ask someone to tell you two or three things that happened in your family
recently, and put them in order together. Then tell them the order using "before" and
"after."
