# Lesson 4 — A Knowledge and Skill People Developed

## Summary

Learners meet one knowledge and skill a particular people developed — **wayfinding**,
the Polynesian skill of reading stars, waves, birds, and clouds to sail across the
open ocean — and explain what it helps people do. They hold it alongside farming and
weaving as other living knowledge traditions, passed down hand to hand, never
"primitive."

## Objectives

- Identify one knowledge or skill a particular people has developed, such as farming,
  weaving, or wayfinding, and explain what it helps them do. _(D08.S2.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a skill you are learning — reading, cooking, fixing something. Someone
taught you, and someone taught them, back and back. Every people has developed its
own knowledge and skills this way, over a very long time. One of the most remarkable
is **wayfinding**: long before maps and machines, Pacific Island navigators learned
to read the stars, the ocean waves, the clouds, and the birds to sail their canoes
across thousands of kilometres of open sea — and they still do it today.

## Materials

- Wayfinding picture cards (stars, waves, a sailing canoe, a navigator)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw the wayfinding picture cards.
- Prepare to tell the story accurately: Polynesian and Micronesian navigators read
  the night sky, ocean swells, clouds, and birds to find their way; in 1976 the
  Hōkūleʻa canoe sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti guided by the Micronesian master
  navigator Mau Piailug using these traditional skills (see Resources).
- Have ready one or two other knowledge examples — farming (terracing and potatoes in
  the Andes) and weaving — to offer as choices.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **This is the unit's clearest chance
to honor a non-European knowledge tradition as real science, not legend.** Wayfinding
is careful observation and deep knowledge — navigators read the rising points of
stars, the feel of ocean swells, the shapes of clouds, and the flight of birds to
know where land lies, sometimes over thousands of kilometres of open water. Tell it
with respect and accuracy, and name the people: Pacific Island navigators across
Polynesia and Micronesia, and the 1976 Hōkūleʻa voyage guided by master navigator Mau
Piailug (see Resources). **Never call it "primitive" or "simple," and never rank it
below a compass or GPS** — it is a different, living way of knowing. Offer farming and
weaving as other examples so learners see that knowledge traditions are many, on
every continent. Honor every body: describe the cards in words for a learner who
cannot see them; a learner who does not use a speaking voice acts the signs or points
to the cards.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** If you were in a boat on the wide sea with no map and no phone,
   how would you know which way to go? Pause and wonder.
2. **Meet wayfinding (15 min).** Listen to the story. Long ago, and still today,
   Pacific Island navigators sail canoes across the ocean. They read the **stars**
   (which rise where), the **waves** (how the sea swells and bends around islands),
   the **clouds** (which gather over land), and the **birds** (which fly home to land
   at dusk). These signs tell them where they are and which way to sail.
3. **Say what it helps them do (10 min).** Together, finish the sentence: "Wayfinding
   helps people..." (cross the open ocean, find islands, travel between homes, share
   food and family). Point, act, or sign your answer.
4. **Meet another knowledge (10 min).** Look at two more: **farming** (people in the
   Andes built terraces on steep mountains to grow potatoes) and **weaving** (people
   weave cloth and baskets, passing the patterns from elder to child). Each helps
   people live — food, clothing, carrying.
5. **Draw and tell (10 min).** Draw one knowledge or skill and write or tell one
   sentence: "This helps people..." You may choose wayfinding, farming, weaving, or a
   skill your own family or people keep.
6. **Close (5 min).** Every people has developed knowledge and skill, passed down
   hand to hand. All of it is real, and all of it helps people live.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Meet only wayfinding, and finish the "it helps people..." sentence
  together with picture support.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel a woven sample, ripple water in a bowl to feel
  "waves," and hear the story told clearly; describe aloud what the skill helps do.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Act out reading the stars and steering the canoe, and
  point to the "what it helps" picture instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The cards are already visual — read them with your eyes, and
  sign "stars," "waves," and "sail" as you place each one.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Direct a partner to move the canoe card and the
  sign cards while you point, sign, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Name a second knowledge or skill and tell one thing it helps people
  do, and how it is passed from one person to the next.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one knowledge or skill a people
  developed and identify at least one sign or step of it?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner explain, in a finished sentence,
  what the knowledge or skill helps people do?

## Resources

- Polynesian Voyaging Society (Hōkūleʻa) — documents traditional Polynesian
  wayfinding (reading stars, swells, clouds, and birds) and the 1976 Hōkūleʻa voyage
  from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti, guided without instruments by master navigator Mau Piailug.
  https://hokulea.com/
- International Potato Center (CIP), "Potato" — the potato was first domesticated in
  the Andes of South America; thousands of native varieties are still farmed there,
  supporting the Andean farming/terracing example.
  https://cipotato.org/potato/
- UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — frames living heritage as the practices,
  knowledge, and skills that communities pass from one generation to the next,
  supporting the idea of knowledge passed down hand to hand.
  https://ich.unesco.org/

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: what is one skill or piece of knowledge our family keeps — a
recipe, a song, a way of making or fixing something? What does it help us do, and who
taught it to whom?
