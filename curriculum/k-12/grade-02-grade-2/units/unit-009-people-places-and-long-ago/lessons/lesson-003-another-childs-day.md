# Lesson 3 — A Day in Another Child's Life

## Summary

Learners step into the daily life of children in another place and time — what they
eat, where they live, how they help, and how they play — and sort what is **alike**
and what is **different** from their own day. Every child's day is real and worthy;
no way is "better."

## Objectives

- Describe the daily life of children in another place or time, including food,
  homes, work, and play, using stories or pictures. _(D08.S2.02.01)_

## Connection

You wake up, you eat, you live in a home, you help, and you play. So does almost
every child, everywhere. What changes from place to place is *how*: one child eats
bread and another eats rice, one lives in a house of wood and another in a home of
clay or a tent, one helps carry water and another helps herd animals. Looking at
another child's day — and seeing both what is alike and what is different — is how we
come to know and respect one another.

## Materials

- Two or three "a day in a child's life" picture sets (food, home, work, play)
- A compare chart with four rows: food, home, work, play

## Preparation

- Choose or draw two or three short child-life picture sets. Good, well-documented
  examples: a Quechua-speaking child in the high Andes who helps farm potatoes and
  herd llamas (see Resources), a child in a Pacific island community who helps fish
  and paddle, and a child from long ago in a farming village who helps with the
  harvest.
- Prepare the four-row compare chart (food, home, work, play).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Hold the examples with respect, as
real children's lives, not costumes.** Do not present any place as "exotic" or any
life as "sad" — the goal is accurate, warm description of food, homes, work, and
play, and noticing what is alike across all of us (every child eats, sleeps, helps,
and plays). **Use present tense for living ways and past tense only for "long ago"
examples** — many children today still farm, herd, and fish, and that is not
"backward," it is a living way of life. Keep facts accurate and hedged (see
Resources). Honor every body: describe the picture cards in words for a learner who
cannot see them; a learner who does not use a speaking voice acts or sorts the cards.
Never require a learner to compare their own home if that feels private — they may
compare the two *given* children instead.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of your own day: one thing you eat, where you live, one
   way you help, and one way you play. Hold those in your mind.
2. **Meet a child's day (15 min).** Listen or look at the picture set of one child in
   another place. What do they **eat**? What is their **home** like? What **work** do
   they help with? How do they **play**?
3. **Sort alike and different (15 min).** On the four-row chart, sort each part —
   food, home, work, play. Which parts are like your day, and which are different?
   Say one thing aloud: "We both... " and "Here, one way it is different is..."
4. **Meet a second child (10 min).** Look at a second child's day — from another
   place, or from long ago. Notice the same four parts: food, home, work, play.
5. **Close (5 min).** Children everywhere eat, live somewhere, help, and play — the
   *parts* are alike, the *ways* are many. Every way is real and worthy of respect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Meet just one child and name only two parts (food and play) with heavy
  pointing support.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear the day described in clear words and sort real
  objects (a grain for food, cloth for home, a tool for work, a ball for play).
- **Support (non-speaking):** Sort picture cards onto the chart and point to "alike"
  and "different" spots instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The picture sets are already visual — read them with your
  eyes, and sign each part (food, home, work, play) as you place it.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use larger cards and a stable chart; direct a
  partner to place each card while you point, sign, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Write or tell one sentence about each part for the second child,
  using the words "alike" and "different."

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least three of the four parts
  of another child's day — food, home, work, play?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner say or show one thing that is
  alike and one thing that is different, without ranking either child's life as
  better?

## Resources

- DK / UNICEF, *Children Just Like Me* — a book that shows real children's daily
  lives (food, homes, work, play) across many countries, side by side. (Book;
  no URL.)
- International Potato Center (CIP), "Potato" — notes the potato was first
  domesticated in the Andes mountains of South America and that thousands of native
  potato varieties are still grown there, supporting the Andean child-life example.
  https://cipotato.org/potato/

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a child's day in a place they know — maybe where they grew
up, or a place far away. What did that child eat, and how did they play? What is like
your day, and what is different?
