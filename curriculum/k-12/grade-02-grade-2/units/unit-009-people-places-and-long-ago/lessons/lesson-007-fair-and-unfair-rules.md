# Lesson 7 — Fair and Unfair Rules

## Summary

Learners sort **rules** and ways of treating people into **fair** and **unfair**, and
practice giving a **reason why**. The goal is noticing and naming fairness — a
foundation of the study of power — held gently, without blame, and always ending in
the possibility of making it right.

## Objectives

- Identify a rule or a way of treating people that is fair or unfair and explain why,
  using a classroom or story example. _(D08.S4.02.01)_

## Connection

Every group has **rules** — in your classroom, your games, your home. A rule is
**fair** when it gives everyone the same chance and has a good reason; a rule is
**unfair** when it leaves someone out for no good reason. You notice this every day.
Naming *why* a rule is fair or unfair is the first step toward making things fair.

## Materials

- A set of rule cards (fair and unfair examples)
- A "fair" spot and an "unfair" spot

## Preparation

- Prepare simple rule cards. Fair examples: "everyone gets a turn," "everyone shares
  the crayons," "we listen to each person." Unfair examples: "only children wearing
  red may play," "only the tallest may use the swing," "one child may take two turns
  every time."
- Prepare a gentle story (optional) where a rule changes to leave someone out, ending
  in repair without blame.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **The skill is reasoning about
fairness, not moralizing about the learners.** Keep examples blame-free and end with
repair: an unfair rule is something we can notice and change together — the unfairness
is in the rule, never in a child. **The "why" is the point:** fair means everyone gets
the same chance and there is a good reason; unfair means someone is left out for no
good reason. (This is a values commitment, held as such — not a factual claim.) Honor
every body: a learner who does not use a speaking voice points to the fair/unfair
spots or acts the rule; a learner who is blind or has low vision hears each rule
stated clearly and describes why aloud. **Never require a learner to share a real
unfairness from their own life** — the given cards and stories are enough. Note the
gentle bridge to the wider world: the same noticing of fair and unfair, applied to
communities and peoples, is how fairness in the big human story begins to be studied
in later grades.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a game with rules. What makes a game fun and fair?
   What would make it feel unfair?
2. **Meet fair and unfair (10 min).** **Fair** means everyone gets the same chance,
   for a good reason. **Unfair** means someone is left out, or treated differently,
   for no good reason. Say it together: "Fair = same chance, good reason. Unfair =
   left out, no reason."
3. **Sort the rules (15 min).** Take each rule card. Put it on the "fair" spot or the
   "unfair" spot. For each one, say **why**: "This is fair because everyone gets a
   turn." "This is unfair because only children wearing red may play, and that is not
   a good reason."
4. **Hear or act a story (10 min).** Listen to a short story where a rule leaves
   someone out. Point to the moment it is unfair, and say why. Then hear the repair:
   someone notices, and the rule is changed so everyone is included.
5. **Close (5 min).** Noticing fair and unfair, and saying why, is a skill you can use
   all your life — in games, in class, and in the wide world. And unfair things can be
   changed.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort just two rules (one fair, one unfair) and give one "why" together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Hear each rule stated clearly and describe why it is
  fair or unfair aloud, or sort by pointing to a textured fair/unfair spot.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the fair or unfair spot and act out the rule,
  or draw a fair and an unfair scene.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The rule cards and pictures are visual — read them with your
  eyes, and sign "fair" and "unfair" as you sort each one.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Direct a partner to move each card to its spot while
  you point, sign, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Make up a new fair rule for the story's game and tell why it is fair.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a fair rule and an unfair rule
  correctly?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner give a reason why a rule is fair
  or unfair (everyone gets a turn / left out for no good reason)?

## Home connection

With a grown-up, think of a rule at home or in a game you both know. Is it fair or
unfair, and why? If it is unfair, what is one small way it could be made fair?
