# Lesson 1 — Feelings That Look Alike

## Summary

Learners return to the year's hardest naming task: feelings that look alike. They
compare two "cousin" pairs — mad and frustrated, sad and disappointed — and learn to
tell each one apart by its body clue and by the situation that made it. This closes
the year's feeling work by celebrating how precise their feeling language has become.

## Objectives

- Use feeling words to tell the difference between similar emotions, such as mad and
  frustrated or sad and disappointed. _(D01.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

Some feelings look almost the same, like cousins in a family — but they are not the
same. When a tower you built falls down, you might feel **mad** (hot and tight,
wanting to push) or **frustrated** (stuck, blocked, wanting to try again). When a
friend cannot come to play, you might feel **sad** (a loss, heavy and slow) or
**disappointed** (you had hoped and it did not happen). All year you have been growing
the words and body clues to tell these cousins apart. Today you put that power to use.

## Materials

- Feeling cards for the four cousin feelings (mad, frustrated, sad, disappointed)
- Chart paper and markers
- The class "feelings wall" (optional)

## Preparation

- Gather or draw the four cousin-feeling cards, carried forward from the year's
  opening unit.
- Set out chart paper to record body clues for each pair.
- Arrange a circle for acting out and guessing.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. It is a **spiral-back**: the year opened by learning to tell
mad from frustrated and sad from disappointed, and now we gather that skill with
confidence. The deepest commitment here is that **a feeling is never wrong** — only a
guess about someone else's feeling can miss, and these two feelings look alike on
purpose, so a careful guess is a kind guess. Honor whatever a learner names, even if
it is a different word; wonder together about which clue led them there. Feeling words
live differently in every language and culture: invite a learner to share a home-word
for one of these feelings, and notice how some languages keep two feelings apart that
others fold together. For a learner who is blind or has low vision, describe the
body-sense of each pair aloud (mad = hot and tight; frustrated = stuck and blocked;
sad = heavy and slow; disappointed = a let-down in the chest) so the difference can be
*felt*. A learner who does not use a speaking voice shows the feeling with face and
body and a partner names it; a learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing reads and shows
the difference through face, body, and sign.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember the start of the year. You met two pairs of feelings
   that look alike: **mad** and **frustrated**, **sad** and **disappointed**. Say each
   pair aloud and make each face. They look a little the same — like cousins.
2. **Find the body clue (15 min).** For each feeling, find where it lives in the body
   and what the situation was. Mad is hot and tight — it wants to push. Frustrated is
   stuck and blocked — it wants to try again. Sad is heavy and slow — something was
   lost. Disappointed is a let-down in the chest — you had hoped, and it did not
   happen. Write each clue on the chart.
3. **Guess the cousin (10 min).** A partner acts out one feeling without words, and you
   guess *which cousin* it is — mad or frustrated, sad or disappointed — then swap.
   If you are not sure, ask: "Is it mad (wanting to push) or frustrated (wanting to
   try again)?"
4. **Name a real moment (10 min).** Think of a time you felt one of these feelings
   this year. Say the situation with the frame "I felt ___ when ___," and tell how you
   knew which cousin it was.
5. **Close (5 min).** Your feeling words got sharper all year. Now you can name a
   feeling so precisely that a friend feels truly understood. That is something to be
   proud of.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Show just one pair at a time; use faces and bodies, not written words.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to a card or make the face; a partner names the
  cousin and the clue.
- **Support (Deaf/hard-of-hearing):** Show and read the difference through faces,
  bodies, and signs; pair each word with a visual cue.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Describe the body-sense of each feeling aloud and
  let a partner name it, or name it and let the partner show it.
- **Extension:** Sort a fuller set of cousin feelings (scared and nervous, happy and
  excited) by their body clues.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name mad, frustrated, sad, or
  disappointed, give its body clue, and tell it apart from its cousin?

## Home connection

Teach someone at home the difference between two cousin feelings — mad and frustrated,
or sad and disappointed — and tell them how you tell them apart.
