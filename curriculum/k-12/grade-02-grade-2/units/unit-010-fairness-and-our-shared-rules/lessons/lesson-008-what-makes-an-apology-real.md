# Lesson 8 — What Makes an Apology Real

## Summary

Learners discover what makes an apology real: **meaning it** and **trying to fix the
harm**. They tell the difference between an empty "sorry" and a real apology that
names what happened, notices the other person's feelings, and repairs the hurt — so
both people can be well again.

## Objectives

- Explain what makes an apology real, such as meaning it and trying to fix the harm.
  _(D09.S4.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time someone said "sorry" but did not seem to mean it — the words came out,
but nothing changed, and you still felt hurt. Then think of a time someone said sorry
and *did* something — helped you up, returned the toy, fixed what was broken. The
second one was a **real apology**. Words alone are not enough; a real apology **means
it** and **tries to fix the harm**. People everywhere do this — mending a torn net,
returning a borrowed tool, replanting a broken branch, saying sorry with their hands
and their work. Today you will practice telling the difference, and making a real one.

## Materials

- A "real apology" picture strip (say what you did, say how the other felt, mean it,
  fix the harm)
- Two puppets or soft toys
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the "real apology" picture strip.
- Set up a small space for acting out an empty and a real apology.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **apology as repair, not as
words alone**. An apology is real when the person means it and tries to fix the harm —
naming what happened, noticing how the other felt, and doing something to make it
right. Hold this as a question ("what does this apology *do* for the other person?"),
never as a forced ritual: never make a learner perform an apology or confess a real,
private hurt — invented or acted harms are welcome, and a learner may always pass.
Name the *act*, never the child; there are no "bad children," only hurts to repair.
The goal is understanding, not shame: an empty "sorry" is a *starting* point, and a
learner can always add the repair to make it real. A learner who does not speak aloud
shows the steps by pointing or signing; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing
follows the strip and the acted apology by sign and gesture; a learner who is blind or
low-vision hears the two apologies described and names which is real and why; a
learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to do the repair while they
choose it. The aim is that every learner can say, in their own way, "a real sorry
fixes the harm."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a "sorry" that felt real and one that felt empty.
   What was different?
2. **Meet "real apology" (10 min).** A **real apology** has four steps: say what you
   did, say how the other felt, **mean it**, and **fix the harm**. Words alone are not
   enough.
3. **Hear both (15 min).** With the puppets, show an empty "sorry" — one puppet
   knocks over the other's tower and mumbles "sorry" while turning away. Then show a
   real apology: "I knocked your tower. You look sad. I am sorry — can I help you
   build it again?" Then help rebuild. Wonder together: which was real, and why?
4. **Practice a real apology (10 min).** Pick a small, acted harm. Practice a real
   apology with all four steps (say, sign, point, or act each one). A learner who does
   not move the puppets can point to a step or direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** A real apology means it and fixes the harm. Then both people
   can be well again.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice two steps at a time — first "say what you did," then "fix the
  harm." A non-speaking learner points to the steps on the strip; a learner with
  limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to compare two repairs for the same harm and wonder
  which better fixes it — and whether words or actions matter more.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain what makes an apology real
  (meaning it, fixing the harm)?
- **Performance:** The acted-out real apology, shared with the group.

## Home connection

At home, if a small hurt happens, notice whether the "sorry" fixes the harm. With a
grown-up, practice adding the fix so the apology is real.
