# Lesson 3 — Same Is Not Always Fair

## Summary

Learners meet the unit's central fairness idea: **same is not always fair**. Treating
everyone exactly the same can leave some people out, while giving each person the
support they *need* lets everyone take part. They practice matching needs to supports
and explaining why, held as a question to keep wondering about.

## Objectives

- Explain why treating everyone the same is not always fair, using an example like
  different needs. _(D09.S2.02.01)_

## Connection

Think of three people trying to see over a wall: a tall grown-up, a small child, and
a child who uses a wheelchair. If you give all three the *same* small box to stand
on, the tall one sees over easily, the small child still cannot see, and the
wheelchair user cannot reach at all. Same — but not **fair**. If instead you give each
one the support they *need* — nothing for the tall one, a taller box for the small
child, a ramp for the wheelchair — then all three see. People everywhere figure this
out: a village gives a fishing net to the one who can cast it and a basket to the one
who carries. Today you will practice telling *same* from *fair*.

## Materials

- A needs-and-supports picture set (a small child who cannot reach, a person who uses
  a wheelchair, a tall person; a step stool, a ramp, nothing needed)
- Blocks or boxes of different heights

## Preparation

- Prepare the needs-and-supports pictures, or gather blocks and boxes of different
  heights.
- Set up a "wall" (a table edge, a line, or a branch) that is hard for some to see
  over.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson holds two ideas open, side by side, as a
question: **equality** — same for everyone — and **equity** — what each one needs. The
lesson does *not* teach that sameness is wrong; it teaches that sameness is not the
*whole* of fairness. Keep the examples gentle and real: reaching a shelf, seeing over
a wall, reading a sign, drinking from a fountain. Never single out or shame a learner
for a need — frame needs as ordinary and universal (everyone has needs; some are just
easier to see). Include ability naturally: a ramp and a step stool are just two ways
people get where they need to go, not a "special" case. A learner who does not speak
aloud shows which support matches which person by pointing or placing blocks; a
learner who is blind or low-vision feels the different heights of the blocks and
hears the scene described; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to
place the supports. The goal is the learner's own explanation — "the same box is not
fair because they have different needs" — not a memorized phrase. A learner may
always pass.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember a time something was too high, too far, or too hard
   for you but easy for someone else. Did the *same* help work for everyone?
2. **Meet "same" and "fair" (10 min).** **Same** means everyone gets exactly the
   same thing. **Fair** means everyone gets what they *need* to take part. Look at the
   pictures: same box for all, or the support each one needs.
3. **Build it (15 min).** Set up your wall. Try giving everyone the *same* small box.
   Who can see? Who cannot? Now try giving each person what they *need* — nothing, a
   taller box, a ramp. Who can see now? Say, sign, or point to which way is fair, and
   why.
4. **Explain why (10 min).** Finish the sentence: "Treating everyone the same is not
   always fair because…" Use your example. Wonder together: is there ever a time when
   same *is* fair?
5. **Close (5 min).** Fair does not always mean the same. Fair means everyone gets
   what they need to take part — a question to keep wondering about.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use just two people and two supports; model "the same box is not fair
  because…" A non-speaking learner points to the support each person needs; a learner
  with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to invent a new need (a friend who cannot read small
  print, someone with a broken arm) and wonder what support would make it fair.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain why treating everyone the same
  is not always fair, using a different-needs example?
- **Performance:** The built scene — same vs. what-each-needs — explained aloud.

## Home connection

At home, notice a place where people have different needs (a high shelf, a heavy
bucket, a door). Wonder with someone: what support would let everyone take part?
