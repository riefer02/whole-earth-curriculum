# Lesson 4 — The Rights Every Child Has

## Summary

Learners meet the idea of a **right** — something every child should be able to do or
have, everywhere on Earth. They name a right children have, such as the right to play
or to learn, and explain why it matters, noticing that a right belongs to *every*
child, not just some.

## Objectives

- Name a right that children have, such as the right to play or learn, and why it
  matters. _(D09.S2.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a day when you could play, or learn, or rest when you were tired, or be
listened to when you spoke. Those are not just lucky days — they are **rights**:
things every child should be able to do or have, no matter where on Earth they live,
how much money their family has, or what their body can do. The right to play, the
right to learn, the right to be safe, the right to be heard. People have written
these down so that *every* child is counted. Today you will name one and say why it
matters.

## Materials

- Rights picture cards (to play, to learn, to be safe, to be heard, to rest)
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare the rights picture cards, each with a simple icon and a label.
- Arrange a circle for acting out and sharing.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson introduces **children's rights** gently
and universally. Ground the idea in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of
the Child, which says every child has the right to things like play, learning, safety,
rest, and being heard — but keep it concrete for age 7–8: a right is something every
child should be able to do or have. Emphasize that rights belong to *every* child
everywhere — this is an egalitarian, global claim, not one nation's rule. Hold it as a
*should* (a shared hope and agreement) rather than a claim that every child already
has these things honored; learners may notice that not every child gets to play or
learn, and that noticing is welcome — it is the beginning of caring. Do not frighten;
connect any gap to agency and hope ("we can help make it true"). A learner who does
not speak aloud points to a rights card or signs; a learner who is deaf or hard of
hearing follows the rights through the cards and sign; a learner who is blind or
low-vision hears each right described and names it aloud; a learner with a motor
difference can direct a partner to act out the right. A learner may always pass.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of something you did today — played, learned, rested,
   or spoke. Did anyone have to give you permission? You have a **right** to it.
2. **Meet "right" (10 min).** A **right** is something every child should be able to
   do or have, everywhere on Earth. Look at the cards: play, learn, be safe, be
   heard, rest. Each is a right.
3. **Name one and why it matters (15 min).** Pick one right (point, sign, or say it).
   Finish the sentence: "Every child has the right to…, and it matters because…"
   Wonder together: what would life be like without it?
4. **Draw your rights wheel (10 min).** Draw yourself in the center of a circle, and
   around the edge draw or write the rights you think every child should have. If
   drawing is not your way, build it from found objects, act it out, or have a partner
   draw while you direct. Tell a partner why one of them matters.
5. **Close (5 min).** Rights belong to every child, everywhere. Naming a right is the
   first step to caring that every child gets it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer two rights cards and model one sentence "Every child has the
  right to play because…" A non-speaking learner points to the card; a learner with
  limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to wonder whether there is a right they would add
  that is not on the cards, and why it matters.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a right children have and say why
  it matters?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "rights wheel" drawing (or a built, acted, or
  partner-scribed version), kept in the learner's portfolio.

## Home connection

At home, ask a grown-up: what right did you love most as a child — to play, to learn,
to be safe, or to be heard? Tell them which right matters most to you and why.
