# Lesson 1 — Right and Wrong, With a Reason

## Summary

Learners meet the words "right" and "wrong" as names for *choices* (not for
children), and practice telling the difference between something that is right to do
and something that is wrong to do — then giving a reason with the word "because."
Reasons help us understand a choice; they do not make a wrong choice right.

## Objectives

- Tell the difference between something that is right to do and something that is
  wrong to do, and give a reason. _(D09.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

Think of a time you found something that was not yours — a coin on the path, a toy
left behind. You could keep it, or you could give it back. One of those is the
**right** thing to do, and the other is the **wrong** thing to do. And you can always
say *why*: "I gave it back because it belonged to someone else, and they would miss
it." Everywhere in the world — in a busy market, a quiet village, a boat, or a
classroom — people make choices every day and can give a reason for them. Today you
will practice telling right from wrong, and saying why.

## Materials

- Right-or-wrong situation cards (returning a found coin, taking without asking,
  helping someone up, leaving a friend out of a game)
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw a set of right-or-wrong situation cards showing everyday moments.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and acting out.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a self-directed
learner. The central commitment is **name the act, never the child**: say "that
choice was wrong," never "you are a bad child." Right and wrong here name *choices*,
not people — everyone makes wrong choices sometimes, and everyone can choose again.
Right and wrong are held as a question to explore ("what does this choice do for the
other person?") rather than a rule handed down. A **reason** is the "because" behind a
choice; invite learners to give one, and accept every honest reason — there is no
single correct answer, and a choice can be right in one situation and wrong in
another. A learner who does not speak aloud names the choice and its reason by
pointing to a card or a "because" picture, or by signing; a learner who is deaf or
hard of hearing reads each situation from the cards or the acted-out scene and gives
the reason by signing or pointing; a learner who is blind or low-vision hears each
situation described and gives the reason aloud or by touch; a learner with a motor
difference can direct a partner to act out the choice. Do not require anyone to speak
in front of the group; a learner may always pass.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit comfortably. Think of a time you did the right thing — or
   the wrong thing. Could you say *why* you did it?
2. **Meet the words (10 min).** Something that is **right to do** helps people and is
   fair to them. Something that is **wrong to do** hurts someone or is unfair. These
   words name the *choice*, not the person — everyone can choose again.
3. **Tell which is which, and why (15 min).** For each situation card, name one right
   choice and one wrong choice, and give a reason with "because." Say it, sign it,
   point to it, or act it out. Wonder together: what does each choice do for the other
   person?
4. **Draw your right choice and reason (10 min).** Draw one right choice you could
   make — for a friend, a family member, an animal, or the earth. Under it, write or
   tell the reason: "because…" If drawing is not your way, build it from found
   objects, act it out, or have a partner draw or scribe while you direct. Tell a
   partner what it is and why it is right.
5. **Close (5 min).** Right and wrong name choices, and you can always give a reason.
   Tomorrow you can choose again.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer just two situations; model one sentence "That choice is right
  because…" with a word and a gesture. A non-speaking learner points to a card and a
  "because" picture and nods or shakes; a learner with limited movement directs a
  partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to think of one choice that could be right in one
  situation and wrong in another (for example, shouting to warn of danger vs.
  shouting at a friend) and wonder why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner tell the difference between something
  right and something wrong to do, and give a reason?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "my right choice and its reason" drawing (or a built,
  acted, or partner-scribed version), kept in the learner's portfolio.

## Home connection

Tonight, notice one right choice and one wrong choice around you — in your home, on
the street, or in a story. Tell a grown-up which is which, and give a reason for each.
