# Lesson 2 — How Our Acts Reach Another's Feelings

## Summary

Learners discover that every act *reaches* someone. They practice saying how a kind
act and an unkind act might affect another person's feelings — a kind act can bring a
warm feeling, an unkind act a hurt one — and they do it in the other person's own
words, not just their own.

## Objectives

- Explain how a kind or unkind act affects another person's feelings. _(D09.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time someone said a kind word to you and it stayed with you all day — or a
time a mean word stung. Your acts do the same thing to other people: they **reach**
them. A kind act can make someone feel warm, seen, and safe; an unkind act can make
someone feel hurt, small, or left out. People everywhere — sharing bread in a village,
cheering a friend in a game, apologizing on a boat or in a city — send their feelings
to each other through what they do. Today you will practice seeing the feeling behind
the act.

## Materials

- Act-to-feeling cards (a kind word, a push, sharing a snack, a mean name — each
  paired with a feeling it might bring)
- Two puppets or soft toys

## Preparation

- Prepare the act-to-feeling cards: one set of acts, one set of feeling faces.
- Set up a small space for acting out acts and feelings.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **consequence for others** at the
level of feeling: before deciding, learners pause to ask "how will this act reach
them?" Keep the framing blame-free — the point is not to accuse but to *see* the other
person. Name the *act* ("that push reached them as hurt"), never the child. Feelings
are held as the person's own truth: two people can feel differently about the same
act, and the one who was affected gets to name how it felt — we listen, not argue. A
learner who does not speak aloud shows the feeling by pointing to a face card or by
signing; a learner who is deaf or hard of hearing follows each act by sign or the
feeling cards and answers by pointing or signing; a learner who is blind or low-vision
hears each act described and names the feeling aloud; a learner with a motor
difference can direct a partner to move the puppets. Never require anyone to share a
real, private feeling — invented or acted feelings are welcome, and a learner may
always pass. The aim is that every learner can say, in their own way, "my act reaches
you."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time someone's act reached *you* — kindly or
   unkindly. How did it feel? Where did you feel it in your body?
2. **Meet "acts reach feelings" (10 min).** Every **act** — something you do — reaches
   someone. A **kind act** can bring a warm, safe, seen feeling. An **unkind act** can
   bring a hurt, small, or left-out feeling. Follow the arrow: act → feeling.
3. **Match act to feeling (15 min).** Pick an act card. What feeling might it bring?
   Match it to a feeling face (point, sign, or say it). Wonder together: could the
   same act reach two different people in two different ways?
4. **Act it out (10 min).** With the puppets, show an act and the feeling that
   follows. Say it in the other person's words: "When you shared with me, I felt warm
   and glad." A learner who does not move the puppets can point to the feeling or
   direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** My acts reach other people. Before I act, I can pause and ask:
   how will this reach them?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use just two acts and two feeling faces; model one full sentence
  "When you…, I felt…" A non-speaking learner points to the feeling face; a learner
  with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to think of one act that could bring a warm feeling
  to one person and a mixed feeling to another (for example, telling the truth) and
  wonder why feelings can differ.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain how a kind or unkind act
  affects another person's feelings?
- **Performance:** The acted-out act-to-feeling exchange, reflected on aloud.

## Home connection

At home, notice one act you do today and ask yourself: how will this reach them?
Then, if you like, ask the person how it felt — and listen.
