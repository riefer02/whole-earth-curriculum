# Lesson 15 — Picture Graphs and Bar Graphs

## Summary

Learners turn the same data into two graphs — a **picture graph** and a **bar graph** —
and read both: which answer has the most, which the fewest, and **how many more or
less**. They discover that a bar graph is just a picture graph with bars instead of
pictures.

## Objectives

- Draw and interpret picture graphs and bar graphs and answer questions about how many
  more or less. _(D05.S4.02.02)_

## Connection

A class wants to know which fruit to bring for a celebration; a village wants to know
how many homes have a garden; a family wants to know each person's favorite game. When
you ask everyone the same question and draw the answers, you make a **graph** — and the
answer jumps out at you. Graphs are how people turn many voices into one clear
picture, so decisions are fair and everyone is counted.

## Materials

- A survey question and a way to record answers
- One small object, mark, or drawn picture per learner
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Choose one question everyone can answer.
- Prepare a chart with a row for each answer.
- Give each learner one object or mark to place.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It builds on Grade 1's picture graphs
and adds the **bar graph** — the new skill is drawing bars and answering "how many
more or less." Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13): build the picture
graph, then draw a bar the same height and show the two are the same data. The
powerful ideas are **one mark per answer**, **bars that match the count**, and **how
many more or less** as a subtraction question. Frame graphs as fairness tools: every
answer counts once, and no answer is more important than another (egalitarianism).
Choose a question that is respectful and has no "right" answer. For learners who are
Deaf or hard of hearing, pose the question in sign or pictures and count bars with
fingers; for learners who are blind or low-vision, build a *tactile* graph with
objects in rows the hands can count. A learner who does not speak places an object or
points. Every answer is one mark, and every mark matters equally.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — a question for everyone (5 min).** Today we ask one question of everyone
   and draw the answers two ways. Here is our question: ___ (for example, "Which fruit
   do you like best?").
2. **Worked example — build the picture graph (10 min).** Each learner places one
   object in the row for their answer — **one object for one person**. Count each row.
   Which has the **most**? Which the **fewest**?
3. **Draw the bar graph (12 min).** Now draw the same data as a **bar graph**: for each
   answer, draw a bar as tall as its count (one square per person). Give the graph a
   **title**. A bar graph is the same as a picture graph — bars instead of pictures.
4. **Read "how many more or less" (10 min).** Compare two bars. How many more people
   chose the top answer than the bottom one? Count the difference, or subtract. How
   many *less* chose one than another? Say and write the answers.
5. **Ask new questions (8 min).** What else can the graph tell us? How many people
   answered in all? Are any bars the **same**? Wonder about it together.
6. **Close (5 min).** You asked a question, collected answers, and drew them two ways.
   A graph turns many voices into one fair picture — and "how many more" is a question
   you can now answer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only two or three answer choices and count bars together, touching
  each square.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Build the graph with objects in rows and read it by
  feeling and counting each row.
- **Extension:** Write a sentence about the graph ("six chose mango, three chose
  apple, so three more chose mango"), or draw the graph with a scale of one square per
  person.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner draw a picture graph and a bar graph
  and answer questions about most, fewest, and how many more or less?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's bar graph (or a drawing of it), kept as a
  record of data work.

## Home connection

At home, ask everyone the same question ("which color do you like best?"), draw the
answers as a bar graph, and tell a grown-up which has the most and how many more.
