# Lesson 5 — Subtracting Within 100

## Summary

Learners subtract within 100 by taking away the **tens** and the **ones** separately.
They model with bundles, draw the take-away, and record the difference in parts — five
tens take away three tens, seven ones take away four ones — so written subtraction
grows out of real objects.

## Objectives

- Add and subtract within 100 using models, drawings, or written methods, and explain
  the strategy used. _(D05.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

Taking away is a story you know too. A cook has 57 beans and uses 34; a child has 57
steps left and walks 34; a shopkeeper has 57 eggs and sells 34. To find what is left
without getting lost, you take away the tens from the tens and the ones from the ones —
big groups from big groups, little ones from little ones.

## Materials

- Bundles of ten and single ones (or base-ten blocks)
- Paper or slate and a writing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair bundles and ones enough to model a number and a take-away.
- Prepare subtraction examples that stay within 100 (start without regrouping).
- Plan to model taking away tens and ones as a worked example.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It introduces **subtracting within 100
by place value**, the reverse of Lesson 4. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy
§13): build 57, take away 3 tens and 4 ones, count what is left. Keep numbers without
regrouping at first, so the strategy is clear before regrouping arrives later. The
goal is *sense-making*, not speed: a learner may count every object today and use a
written method tomorrow — both are correct, and explaining one's own way is the real
measure. **There is no single "right" strategy; every clear strategy is honored.** For
learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, model with bundles and write each step; for
learners who are blind or low-vision, take away real objects and count what is left by
touch. A learner who does not speak records by moving bundles or pointing to the
difference.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall taking away (5 min).** You know subtracting means taking away.
   Quick: what is 7 − 4? What is 5 tens − 3 tens? Today we take away bigger numbers —
   but the trick is the same: take tens from tens, ones from ones.
2. **Worked example — take away tens and ones (10 min).** Build 57 (five tens, seven
   ones). Take away 34: take three tens and four ones. Count what is left: two tens
   and three ones — **23.** Write it in parts: 5 tens − 3 tens = 2 tens, and 7 − 4 =
   3, so 57 − 34 = 23.
3. **Guided practice — build and take away (15 min).** Pick two numbers (the first
   bigger than the second). Build the first. Take away the second — tens first, then
   ones. Count what is left, then say and write the difference. Do several.
4. **Draw the take-away (8 min).** Choose one subtraction and draw it — the starting
   amount, the part taken away, and the part left, labeled. Does your drawing match
   your difference?
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade one subtraction. Build and solve a friend's
   problem, then compare differences. If they differ, build it again together and find
   where the counts went different — kindly.
6. **Close (2 min).** Subtracting bigger numbers is just two small take-aways: tens
   from tens, ones from ones. You can now find what is left within one hundred.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use numbers with no regrouping and model every step with bundles.
- **Support (motor):** Use large blocks and have a partner move objects while the
  learner directs and counts.
- **Extension:** Try a subtraction where the ones are not enough (for example,
  52 − 26), and discover what happens when you must break a ten.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner subtract within 100 with models or
  drawings, taking away tens and ones separately, and record the difference?
- **Formative (self):** Can the learner check their written difference against their
  model?

## Home connection

At home, subtract one amount from another under one hundred — beans left after a
share, steps left after a walk — by taking away tens and ones, and tell a grown-up
what is left.
