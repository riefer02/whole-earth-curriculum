# Lesson 1 — Tens and Ones

## Summary

Learners bundle objects into groups of ten and discover that a two-digit number is
**tens and ones** — for example, three tens and four ones is thirty-four. They read
and record numbers as tens and ones, treating place value as something you can hold
and count, not just a symbol.

## Objectives

- Use place value of tens and ones to read, write, compare, and order two-digit
  numbers. _(D05.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

A farmer counts eggs into trays of ten, a fisher ties fish into bundles of ten to sell
at market, a shopkeeper stacks coins in piles of ten to count money fast. Most peoples
around the world group by ten because ten fingers and ten toes make ten easy to see and
count. Some peoples have kept count other ways — by fives, by twenties, with tally
marks, or with knots on a cord — and every counting way is a real way to keep track.
Here we practice the ten-way: when you bundle ten and count the bundles, a big pile
becomes a small, easy number: three tens and four ones.

## Materials

- About 100 small objects (pebbles, seeds, beans, buttons)
- Ten small cups, bags, or cloth squares for groups of ten
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair about 100 objects and ten holders.
- Clear space to move, bundle, and count aloud.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) a quick example of "three tens and four ones."

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It builds directly on Grade 1, where
learners bundled tens to count to 120; today the bundles become the key idea of
**place value** — tens and ones. Teach the one new skill as a **worked example**
(philosophy §13): bundle ten, then ten more, then read the tens and ones aloud before
learners try it themselves. The central move is **unitizing** — seeing ten ones as one
ten (Clements & Sarama, 2014). Keep it concrete: objects first, symbols last. **No
one's speed at bundling measures their smartness, and "more" is a fact about a
collection, never a worth about a person.** Count in the home language; number words
differ across languages and all are correct. For learners who are Deaf or hard of
hearing, count in sign or by tapping each bundle; for learners who are blind or
low-vision, move objects into cups so the number grows by feel and sound. Learners
with limited hand or body movement direct a partner to move each object. Mastery
arrives at its own pace for each child.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — remember the ten (5 min).** Last year you bundled objects into groups of
   ten to count all the way to one hundred twenty. Make one group of ten right now.
   Hold up your ten fingers — that is one ten.
2. **Worked example — three tens and four ones (8 min).** Watch and help: take ten
   objects, bundle them (or fill one cup), and say "one ten." Do it again — "two
   tens." Again — "three tens." Now add four loose ones. Count: ten, twenty, thirty,
   then thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three, thirty-four. **Three tens and four ones
   is thirty-four.** Say it together.
3. **Guided practice — build and read (15 min).** From your own collection, make as
   many groups of ten as you can, and keep the leftovers loose. Count your tens by
   tens, then count on the ones. Say your number. Now write it: how many tens? How
   many ones?
4. **Trade and check (10 min).** Trade bundles with a friend. Read their tens and
   ones, and let them read yours. Do you both agree on the number? If not, count again
   together, kindly. A second count that matches is a sign you read it well.
5. **Record it (5 min).** Draw your tens and ones on paper — three lines for three
   tens and four dots for four ones — and write the number beside your drawing.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: every two-digit number is tens and ones. Ten, twenty,
   thirty — the tens — then the little ones on top. You can now hold a number in your
   hands.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build only up to two tens today; add more tens another day, echoing each
  decade number.
- **Support (motor):** Bundle larger, easy-to-grip objects, or have a partner move
  objects while the learner counts and directs.
- **Extension:** Build a number, then build the number that is ten more (or ten less)
  and say how you know.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner group into tens, count the tens by
  tens, and count on the leftover ones to say and write the number?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner tell how many tens and how many ones are in a
  number they built?

## Home connection

At home, gather a pile of beans, stones, or buttons, make groups of ten, and tell a
grown-up how many tens and how many ones there are — and what number that makes.
