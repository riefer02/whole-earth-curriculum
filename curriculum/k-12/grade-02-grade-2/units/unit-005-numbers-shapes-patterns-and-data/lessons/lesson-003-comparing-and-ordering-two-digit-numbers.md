# Lesson 3 — Comparing and Ordering Two-Digit Numbers

## Summary

Learners build two numbers with tens and ones and compare them, discovering that you
look at the **tens** first, and only check the **ones** if the tens are the same. They
then order small sets of two-digit numbers from least to greatest, using bundles as
the proof.

## Objectives

- Use place value of tens and ones to read, write, compare, and order two-digit
  numbers. _(D05.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

Every day we compare amounts — which pile of fruit is bigger, which jar has more, who
has more steps to walk. Comparing well is not about who is better; it is about
knowing amounts so we can share and plan fairly. To compare two numbers, you do not
have to count every single thing — you look at the big groups first. The tens tell you
most of the answer right away.

## Materials

- Bundles of ten and single ones (or base-ten blocks)
- Number cards
- Paper or slate and a writing tool

## Preparation

- Prepare number cards with two-digit numbers of varying tens and ones.
- Have bundles and ones ready for building two numbers at once.
- Plan to model the tens-first rule as a worked example.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It turns the tens-and-ones idea from
Lessons 1–2 into **comparison and order**. Teach the **tens-first rule** as a worked
example (philosophy §13): build 42 and 37, compare the tens (four tens beats three
tens), and only then decide. Keep the language careful and kind: we say "greater
than" and "less than" about *amounts*, never about *people*. **A number being bigger
is a fact about a collection, not a worth about a child — more is not better, it is
just more.** Comparing is a tool for fairness, not a contest. For learners who are
Deaf or hard of hearing, show the comparison with bundles and signs; for learners who
are blind or low-vision, let hands feel the bundles side by side. A learner who does
not speak points or places the greater-than card. No ranking of learners, ever.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall tens and ones (5 min).** You can read a number by its tens and
   ones. Which is more, three tens or four tens? Say why. Today we compare and order
   numbers.
2. **Worked example — look at tens first (10 min).** Build 42 (four tens, two ones)
   and 37 (three tens, seven ones). Which is greater? Compare the **tens** first: four
   tens is more than three tens, so **42 is greater than 37.** Write 42 &gt; 37. The
   ones did not matter this time, because the tens decided it.
3. **Guided practice — compare pairs (15 min).** Draw two number cards. Build each
   number. Say the tens first, then the ones if you need them. Write the comparison
   with &gt;, &lt;, or =. Do several pairs. What happens when the tens are the same —
   like 45 and 48? Then look at the ones.
4. **Order a small set (8 min).** Take three number cards. Put them in order from
   least to greatest, checking the tens first each time. Read them back in order.
5. **Check with a friend (5 min).** Trade comparisons. A friend reads your ordered
   numbers and checks with bundles. Do you agree? Talk it through kindly if not.
6. **Close (2 min).** To compare numbers, look at the tens first. It is a fast, fair
   way to see which amount is more — so we can share and plan well together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare only two numbers whose tens clearly differ; use bundles every
  time.
- **Support (motor):** Use large, easy-to-move cards and have a partner build while
  the learner directs.
- **Extension:** Order four or five numbers, or explain what happens when two numbers
  have the same tens and the same ones.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner build two numbers, compare the tens
  first, and record the comparison with &gt;, &lt;, or =?
- **Formative (peer):** Can the learner order a small set of numbers and check a
  friend's order with bundles?

## Home connection

At home, compare two amounts with a grown-up — two jars of beans, two piles of rice —
and say which is greater and how you know by looking at the tens first.
