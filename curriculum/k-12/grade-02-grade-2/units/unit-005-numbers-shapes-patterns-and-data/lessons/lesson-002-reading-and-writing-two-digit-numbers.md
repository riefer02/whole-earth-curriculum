# Lesson 2 — Reading and Writing Two-Digit Numbers

## Summary

Learners connect the two digits of a number to its tens and ones. They read and write
two-digit numbers in three matching ways: as digits (47), as tens and ones (four tens
and seven ones), and in expanded form (40 + 7) — the same number, told three ways.

## Objectives

- Use place value of tens and ones to read, write, compare, and order two-digit
  numbers. _(D05.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

The same amount can be said many ways. A market seller might say "four trays and
seven loose eggs"; a child counting might say "forty-seven"; a written sign says "47."
All three mean the same thing. Reading and writing numbers is learning the three
languages of a number — the bundles, the words, and the digits — so you can understand
and share an amount with anyone.

## Materials

- Place-value cards or folded paper (tens and ones)
- Paper or slate and a writing tool

## Preparation

- Prepare tens and ones cards (or paper folded into a tens column and a ones column).
- Have examples ready: numbers whose tens and ones are easy to see.
- Plan to model reading one number all three ways before learners try.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It deepens Lesson 1: now the tens and
ones become **digits** with a place. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13):
show 47, build it, say "four tens and seven ones," then write 40 + 7. The key idea is
that the *same number* has three written forms, and reading is translating among them.
Writing conventions differ by language — in some languages the ones are said before
the tens, and in some cultures numbers are written differently — and every way is
correct for the people who use it. Emphasize matching, not speed. **There is no single
"right" way to say a number, and a learner who needs more time is not behind — they
are still learning, which is exactly where learning lives.** For learners who are Deaf
or hard of hearing, sign the number names and point to digits; for learners who are
blind or low-vision, use raised bundles and braille or large print numerals. A learner
who does not speak points, signs, or matches cards to show reading.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall tens and ones (5 min).** Yesterday you bundled tens and counted
   ones. Show a friend (or say aloud): what is three tens and four ones? Today we
   learn to read and write that number in three ways.
2. **Worked example — one number, three ways (10 min).** Look at 47. Build it: four
   bundles of ten and seven ones. Say it: **four tens and seven ones.** Write it in
   expanded form: **40 + 7.** Read it: **forty-seven.** All three are the same number.
   Point to the 4 and the 7 — the 4 tells the tens, the 7 tells the ones.
3. **Guided practice — build, say, write (15 min).** Pick a two-digit number (a friend
   or a card can choose one). Build it with bundles and ones. Then write it all three
   ways: tens-and-ones words, expanded form, and digits. Check that all three match.
4. **Read to a partner (8 min).** Write five two-digit numbers. Read each one to a
   friend as "___ tens and ___ ones," and ask them to write the digits. Then switch.
   Do your digits match?
5. **Self-check (5 min).** Choose one number and check yourself: build it, say it,
   write it three ways. If one way does not match, fix it — that is learning, not
   failing.
6. **Close (2 min).** A number is one thing with three names: the bundles, the words,
   and the digits. You can now read and write all three.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only numbers with easy decade names (20, 30, 40) before adding
  tricky ones; match cards to digits before writing.
- **Support (motor):** Use large digit cards the learner can move instead of writing.
- **Extension:** Write the number in your home language and in another language you
  know, and notice where the tens and ones go in each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner read a two-digit number as tens and
  ones and write it in digits and in expanded form?
- **Formative (self):** Does the learner check their three written forms against their
  built model and correct a mismatch?

## Home connection

At home, write five two-digit numbers and read each one aloud to a grown-up as tens
and ones — then ask the grown-up to write down the number you said.
