# Lesson 7 — Growing Patterns

## Summary

Learners tell a **growing** pattern from a **repeating** one, then build growing
patterns — staircases that add one more each step. They learn to say *how* a pattern
grows: what changes from one step to the next.

## Objectives

- Identify and extend growing patterns and describe how the pattern changes from one
  step to the next. _(D05.S2.02.01)_

## Connection

Some patterns repeat — day and night, bead colors on a necklace — and some patterns
**grow**. A plant grows one leaf, then two, then three; steps of a staircase rise one
at a time; a tower grows a block taller each time you add one. When you see how a
pattern grows, you can predict what comes next — and prediction is how people plan a
garden, build a staircase, or guess tomorrow's weather.

## Materials

- About 30 small objects (counters, seeds, pebbles, blocks)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair about 30 objects.
- Prepare a repeating pattern and a growing pattern to compare.
- Plan to model building a growing staircase as a worked example.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It builds on Grade 1's repeating
patterns and introduces **growing patterns** — the new idea is describing *change*,
not just repetition. Start by recalling a repeating pattern, then contrast it with a
staircase that grows. Teach building and describing a growing pattern as a **worked
example** (philosophy §13): "one block, then two, then three — it grows by one each
step." Patterns are found in every culture — in woven cloth, in songs, in the steps of
a dance — and naming the change is a kind of poetry about structure. **No one's speed
at seeing a pattern measures their smartness; noticing is a skill that grows with
practice, at each learner's own pace.** For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing,
build with objects and point to each change; for learners who are blind or low-vision,
build patterns by touch and feel each row grow. A learner who does not speak shows the
change by pointing or adding the next step.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall repeating patterns (5 min).** Last year you made repeating
   patterns: red, blue, red, blue — the same thing over and over. Say one repeating
   pattern you remember. Today we meet a different kind: patterns that **grow**.
2. **Worked example — a growing staircase (10 min).** Build a staircase with blocks:
   one block, then two blocks, then three, then four. Look at it. Is it repeating?
   No — it **grows**. How does it grow? Each step has **one more** than the step
   before. Say it: "it grows by one each step."
3. **Build your own (15 min).** Use your objects to build a growing staircase. Start
   with one, then two, then three, then four. Say how it grows. Now try a pattern that
   grows by **two**: two, four, six, eight. How is it different?
4. **Draw and label (8 min).** Draw one growing pattern. Draw an arrow and write how
   it changes from one step to the next ("add one more," "add two more").
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade patterns. A friend says how your pattern
   grows and adds the next step. Do you agree? Talk it through kindly if not.
6. **Close (2 min).** A growing pattern is change you can see coming. When you can say
   *how* it grows, you can guess what comes next — and that is the start of prediction.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build only the "add one" staircase and say "one more" for each step.
- **Support (motor):** Use large blocks and have a partner place them while the
  learner directs and describes.
- **Extension:** Build a pattern that grows by three, or a staircase that grows on
  both sides, and describe the change.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner build a growing pattern and describe
  how it changes from one step to the next?
- **Formative (self):** Can the learner add the next step and check it matches the
  rule?

## Home connection

At home, build a growing staircase with stones, beans, or toys and tell a grown-up
how it grows each step.
