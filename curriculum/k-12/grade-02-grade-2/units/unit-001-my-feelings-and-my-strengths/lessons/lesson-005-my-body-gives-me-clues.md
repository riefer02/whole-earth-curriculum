# Lesson 5 — My Body Gives Me Clues

## Summary

Learners reverse yesterday's body map. Now they start from a body signal — tight
shoulders, a fast heart, a heavy chest, a hot face — and use it to guess the feeling,
then check the guess. They learn a three-step routine, **Notice, Name, Check**, and
practice it on their own bodies.

## Objectives

- Notice where a feeling shows up in the body, such as tense shoulders or a fast
  heartbeat, and name the feeling. _(D01.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

You are standing in line for the swing and your shoulders are up near your ears and
your jaw is tight. You did not decide to feel this way — your body already told you.
Now you can read the clue: tight shoulders and a tight jaw often mean *mad* or
*frustrated*. Your body sends clues first; the words come second. Learning to read the
clue lets you catch a feeling early, before it gets very big.

## Materials

- Body clue cards
- Feeling name cards
- Chart paper and markers

## Preparation

- Prepare the body clue cards and the feeling name cards.
- Write the three steps large: Notice, Name, Check.
- Set out a small space where learners can jump and rest to feel a real heartbeat.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The "check" step matters: a body
signal can mean more than one feeling (a fast heart can be scared *or* excited), so
the guess is checked with the situation. Honor that a guess is a guess — the learner
is the expert on their own body. Never insist a learner's signal means a particular
feeling. Keep the heartbeat activity short and inclusive; any learner can feel their
heart by resting a hand on their chest, seated or standing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Yesterday you found where feelings live in your body. Quick
   check: where does mad often live? (Tense shoulders, hot face.) Where does sad often
   live? (Heavy chest.) Today you read your body's clues *backward* — signal first,
   feeling second.
2. **Meet the three steps (10 min).** Learn **Notice, Name, Check**. Notice the body
   signal. Name your best guess of the feeling. Check it against what is happening.
   Example: you notice your heart is beating fast — you guess *scared* — you check: is
   something scary happening, or is it your birthday tomorrow? Fast heart can mean
   scared *or* excited. The situation is the check.
3. **Match signals to feelings (15 min).** Look at each body clue card. Point to the
   feeling name you guess: tight shoulders and a tight jaw (mad or frustrated); a fast
   heart and quick breath (scared or excited); a heavy, slow chest (sad or
   disappointed); hot cheeks (mad); a fluttery tummy (nervous). Remember: some clues
   point to more than one feeling — that is why we check.
4. **Feel it for real (10 min).** Jump or march in place for a moment, then stop and
   rest your hand on your chest. Notice: your heart is fast. Now sit still and breathe
   slowly. Notice: your heart slows. Say the three steps out loud for what you noticed:
   Notice (fast heart), Name (excited, maybe), Check (I was just moving!).
5. **Close (5 min).** Your body talks first, your words come second. When you Notice,
   Name, and Check, you can catch a feeling early — while it is still small enough to
   hold.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work with two body signals at a time; act out each one and offer two
  feeling choices.
- **Extension:** Keep a one-day "clue log" — record one body signal you noticed, your
  guess, and the check, in words or drawings.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a plausible feeling from a
  described body signal and check it against a situation?
- **Self-check:** The learner says the three steps — Notice, Name, Check — for one
  signal they felt today.

## Home connection

Tonight, notice one body signal in someone at home (tight shoulders, a fast breath, a
big sigh) and ask kindly, "Are you feeling okay?" — then listen.
