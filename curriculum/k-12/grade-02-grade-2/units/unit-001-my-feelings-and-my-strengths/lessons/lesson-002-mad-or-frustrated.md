# Lesson 2 — Mad or Frustrated?

## Summary

Learners go deep on one look-alike pair: **mad** and **frustrated**. Using the clue
question *"Did something block me, or did my trying not work?"* they sort everyday
moments and talk about the tricky ones. They discover that both feelings are okay to
have, and that naming the right one helps them ask for the right kind of help.

## Objectives

- Use feeling words to tell the difference between similar emotions, such as mad and
  frustrated. _(D01.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

A big rock falls across the path to the well — now you cannot get through. That is
**mad**: something is blocking you. Now imagine you are trying to carry water in a
bucket with a small crack, and it keeps leaking no matter how carefully you walk.
That is **frustrated**: your trying is not working. Both make your face hot and your
hands tight. The difference is what is happening — a block, or a trying that will not
work.

## Materials

- Mad vs. frustrated comparison card
- Scenario strips
- Two sorting baskets or hoops

## Preparation

- Print or draw the comparison card and the scenario strips.
- Set out the two labeled baskets or hoops: "mad" and "frustrated."
- Write the clue question large: "Did something block me, or did my trying not work?"

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Model the sorting with one clear
example before learners sort on their own, then do several together (guided practice)
before independent work. Both feelings are welcome here — never correct a learner for
"having the wrong feeling." If a moment is genuinely both (a block *and* a trying that
failed), say so and honor it; the clue question is a tool, not a test. Read scenarios
aloud for learners still building reading fluency.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember yesterday's look-alike pair? Quick check: name the two
   feelings that both feel hot and tight. (Mad and frustrated.) Today you become
   experts on telling them apart.
2. **Model the clue (10 min).** Look at the comparison card. **Mad** happens when
   something *blocks* you — a person grabs, a rule stops you, a thing is in the way.
   **Frustrated** happens when you are *trying* and it will not work — again and
   again. Watch: "A friend takes the crayon right out of your hand." That is mad —
   something blocked you. Now: "You try three times to zip your coat and it sticks
   every time." That is frustrated — your trying will not work.
3. **Sort together (10 min).** Hear a moment, then point to the mad basket or the
   frustrated basket. "Your brother changes the game right when it is your turn."
   (Mad.) "You practice jumping rope and keep tripping on the rope." (Frustrated.)
   "Someone cuts in front of you in line." (Mad.) Say your clue question out loud each
   time.
4. **Tricky moments (10 min).** Some moments are both! "You are building a tall tower
   and your baby sister knocks it over — and you had tried so hard to balance it."
   Talk with a partner: is it mad, frustrated, or a little of both? There is no trap —
   noticing both is very grown-up feeling-naming.
5. **Close (5 min).** Mad says "something is blocking me." Frustrated says "my trying
   is not working." When you know which one it is, you know what to ask for — space
   and fairness, or help and a new idea.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer only two picture scenarios at a time; skip the tricky both-moments
  until the pair is solid.
- **Extension:** Write your own tricky moment on a strip and have a partner sort it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort a new moment as mad or frustrated
  and give the clue ("blocked" vs. "trying did not work")?
- **Self-check:** The learner points to the clue question and says which half matches
  their feeling.

## Home connection

Tonight, tell someone at home about a moment you felt mad or frustrated, and use the
clue question to figure out which one it was.
