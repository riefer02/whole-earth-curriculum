# Lesson 5 — A Movement Sequence

## Summary

Learners create and repeat a short **movement sequence** — a few moves in order with
a clear **beginning** and **end**. They discover that a sequence is movement you can
say again, and that dances, games, and everyday tasks everywhere are built from
sequences.

## Objectives

- Create and repeat a short movement sequence with a clear beginning and end.
  _(D07.S3.02.01)_

## Connection

Think of the steps you already do in order — washing your hands, getting ready for
bed, greeting a friend. A **sequence** is steps that go in order, with a clear start
and a clear finish. Everywhere in the world, people move in sequences — in dances, in
games, in the way they welcome and say goodbye. Today you will make your own short
sequence and repeat it the same way.

## Materials

- Open floor space (with a seated option)
- A soft beat or clapped rhythm (optional)

## Preparation

- Clear a space big enough to move, and keep a seated option for everyone.
- Decide on a simple beat to move along, or count aloud.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. A sequence is a **skill structure**, so model it once — a start
pose, two moves, an end pose — before learners make their own (Kirschner, Sweller &
Clark, 2006). There is no correct sequence; a learner's own order is their dance, and
every body tells its own story. A learner who uses a wheelchair or moves with support
does the whole sequence from where they are — a "start" can be hands on knees, a
"move" a raised arm or a turn of the head, an "end" open hands, still. A learner who
is blind or has low vision follows spoken counts ("one, reach up; two, turn") or holds
a partner's elbow to feel the moves. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing follows
a visible beat — a tap on a drum or the floor — and your modeled moves. A learner who
does not speak shows the sequence with the body itself and tells the order by
pointing. Watching and copying is full participation.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a little dance you could make with three or four
   moves. A **sequence** is those moves in order — with a clear **beginning** and a
   clear **end**.
2. **Choose your moves (10 min).** Pick three or four moves — reach up, twist, clap,
   sway, stomp softly, turn. Put them in an order you like. Add a **beginning** (a
   ready pose) and an **end** (a still, finished pose). The beginning says "start,"
   and the end says "done."
3. **Repeat it (15 min).** Do your sequence the same way a second time — same moves,
   same order, same beginning and end. Does the second time match the first? If it
   drifts, that is okay — try again. Repeating is how a sequence becomes yours.
4. **Show and share (10 min).** Do your sequence for a friend while they watch. Then
   tell them (or point to) your beginning, your moves, and your end. Swap, and watch
   theirs.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: your body can make order out of movement. Every dance
   and every day is built from sequences — and now you have your own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a two-move sequence with a clear start and finish ("hands on
  knees, reach up, hands on knees") and let the learner copy, then repeat.
- **Support (seated/adapted):** Do the whole sequence from a chair — a start pose,
  raised arms, a turn of the head, and an end with open hands; that is full
  participation.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Follow spoken counts or hold a partner's elbow to
  feel the moves.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** Follow a visible beat on a drum or the floor and the modeled
  moves.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Show the sequence and tell the order by pointing.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to make a longer sequence that travels through the
  space, with a clear beginning and end, and repeat it without stopping.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner create and repeat a short movement
  sequence with a clear beginning and end?
- **Performance:** The learner's sequence, observed live (in any adapted form).

## Home connection

Teach someone at home your movement sequence — beginning, moves, end — and do it
together two times in a row. Then ask them to add one new move in the middle.
