# Lesson 1 — Mixing Colors

## Summary

Learners mix two colors to make a **new color** and use it in a picture. They
discover that colors all around them — orange fruit, green leaves, purple cloth —
are often made by mixing two other colors, and that people everywhere have mixed
colors to dye cloth, paint walls, and make pictures.

## Objectives

- Mix two colors to make a new one and use it in a picture. _(D07.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

Look at the colors around you — a green leaf, an orange fruit, a purple cloth, a
brown clay pot. Many of these colors are not found ready-made; they are **mixed**
from two others. For a long time, people everywhere have mixed colors to dye cloth,
paint homes, and make pictures — from indigo cloth dyed in one part of the world to
ochre earth painted on walls in another. Today you will mix two colors and use your
new color in a picture.

## Materials

- Paints in red, yellow, and blue (or food color)
- A mixing surface (a plate, paper, or tile)
- Paper and brushes or fingers

## Preparation

- Set out the three starting colors and a mixing surface for each learner or pair.
- Have paper ready, and a way to wash hands after painting.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Mixing is a **skill**, so model it once — "watch: red and
yellow make orange" — before learners try on their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark,
2006). There is no wrong new color; a muddy brown is a real discovery, not a mistake.
Name the colors learners make without judging them ("you made a warm orange!"). A
learner who is blind or has low vision mixes by **feel and temperature and smell**:
they feel the two blobs merge, and a partner or grown-up names the new color; they
can still choose the picture's subject and feel the wet paint with a finger. A
learner who does not speak names the new color by pointing to a color swatch or a
matching object. A learner with limited fine motor mixes with a bigger brush, a
sponge, or by directing a partner who does the brushwork — deciding the mix is
mixing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at the three colors in front of you: **red**, **yellow**,
   and **blue**. These are starting colors. Today you will **mix** two of them to
   make a brand-new color.
2. **Mix two colors (15 min).** Put a little red and a little yellow on your mixing
   surface and stir. What color appears? Try another pair — yellow and blue, or blue
   and red. Watch a new color be born from two others. Say or point to the new color
   you made.
3. **Use your new color (15 min).** Paint a picture that uses the new color you made.
   An orange sun, green leaves, a purple flower, a brown path — or an invented thing.
   Your new color is yours; no one has made exactly this mix before.
4. **Share (10 min).** Show a friend or grown-up your picture. Tell (or point to)
   which two colors you mixed and what new color appeared.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: many colors in the world are made by mixing. Every
   new color you mix belongs to you and to everyone who ever mixed color before you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer one clear mix ("red and yellow — stir them and watch"); let the
  learner name the one new color and paint with it.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Mix by feel; a partner names the new color and the
  learner chooses the picture's subject, feeling the paint with a finger.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Name the new color by pointing to a swatch or a
  matching object.
- **Support (fine motor):** Mix with a bigger brush or sponge, or direct a partner
  who stirs and paints.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to mix a light and a dark version of one new color
  (more or less of one starting color) and name the difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner mix two colors to make a new one and
  use it in a picture?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's picture using the mixed color, kept in the
  portfolio (or a photo of it).

## Home connection

At home, find two colors that could mix — two crayons, two drops of food color, two
bits of clay — and mix them with a grown-up. What new color appears? Then use it in
a small picture.
