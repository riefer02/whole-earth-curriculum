# Lesson 2 — Comparing Two Artworks

## Summary

Learners put two artworks side by side and practice **comparing**: they tell one way
the two are **alike** and one way they are **different**. Comparing is not about
which one is "better" — it is how we look more carefully, and every pair of artworks
has something to teach us.

## Objectives

- Compare two artworks and tell one way they are alike and one way they are
  different. _(D07.S1.02.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever held two cloths side by side in a market, or looked at two painted
walls, or set two pots on a shelf? When we put two things next to each other, we
suddenly see both more clearly — the color they share, the shape they do not.
Comparing is a way of looking harder, and it is a gift we give to the makers. Today
you will compare two artworks and name what is alike and what is different.

## Materials

- Two artworks to look at (two pictures or two real made objects)
- Two cards: "alike" and "different"

## Preparation

- Choose two artworks that share something and differ in something (two cloths, two
  pots, two paintings), presented respectfully as makers' work from living cultures.
- Set them side by side and place the two cards nearby.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. The goal is careful looking, never ranking — there is no
"better" artwork, only two works each worth noticing. Honor both makers and the
cultures the works come from as living peoples' work, not museum pieces. Accept
every answer; "they are both blue" is as true a likeness as "they both have circles."
A learner who is blind or has low vision compares by **touch or description**: feel
two cloths and find a rough-and-rough or a rough-and-smooth pair, or hear two careful
spoken descriptions and name the likeness and difference. A learner who does not
speak compares by pointing to the part they mean, or holding up the "alike" and
"different" cards.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at the two artworks side by side. Let your eyes travel
   from one to the other. Today you will **compare** them.
2. **Find one thing alike (10 min).** Look for something the two share — a color, a
   shape, a line, a size. What is **alike**? Say it, or point to it. Hold up the
   "alike" card.
3. **Find one thing different (10 min).** Now look for something that is not the same —
   a color that differs, a shape that changes, a material that is rough where the
   other is smooth. What is **different**? Say it or point to it. Hold up the
   "different" card.
4. **Share your compare (15 min).** Tell a friend or grown-up one thing alike and one
   thing different. Listen to a friend's compare — is theirs different from yours?
   Both are true, because careful eyes notice many things.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: comparing two artworks is a way of honoring both
   makers. When you look harder, you see more — and there is always more to see.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a sentence starter ("they are alike because they both ___" and
  "they are different because one ___ and the other ___") and accept pointing.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Compare by touch (two cloths, two carved pieces) or
  by two spoken descriptions.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the parts that are alike and different, or
  hold up the two cards.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to find a second likeness and a second difference,
  and to guess what each maker might have wanted to show.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner compare two artworks and tell (or
  point to) one way they are alike and one way they are different?

## Home connection

At home or nearby, find two made things — two cloths, two pots, two pictures. Set
them side by side and tell a grown-up one thing alike and one thing different.
