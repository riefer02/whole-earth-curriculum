# Lesson 8 — Two Ways to Make

## Summary

Learners solve a small making problem **two ways**, **compare** them, **choose** one,
and **explain why**. They discover that trying more than one way is not a detour but
the heart of good making, and that a reason is what turns a choice into a design.

## Objectives

- Compare two ways to solve a making problem and choose one, explaining why.
  _(D07.S4.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a time you tried something two ways — two ways to carry water, two ways to
tie a knot, two ways to make a tower stand. Makers everywhere do this: they try one
way, try another, look at both, and choose the one that works better, saying why.
Today you will solve one small making problem two ways, compare them, and choose.

## Materials

- A making problem to solve (joining two pieces, or making a bridge stand)
- Two kinds of materials or two ways to try

## Preparation

- Set up one clear making problem for learners to solve (e.g., make a bridge that
  holds a stone, or join two sticks).
- Provide two different materials or two different ways for each pair to compare.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. The goal is the **reason**, not the "right" answer — a choice
with a reason is the learning, and either way may be a fine choice. Model the compare
once — "way A sags, way B holds; I choose B because it is stronger" — before learners
compare their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Frame a failed attempt as
information, never a verdict. A learner with limited fine motor tries each way by
directing a partner and decides the choice — deciding is making. A learner who is
blind or has low vision compares by feel and sound (which holds, which bends, which
makes a solid thud) with a partner describing what happened. A learner who does not
speak points to the chosen way and to the reason on a picture card. Every reason is
welcome.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Here is a small making **problem** — a bridge that must hold a
   stone, or two sticks that must stay joined. Today you will solve it **two ways**.
2. **Try way A (10 min).** Make it the first way with the first material. Watch what
   happens. Does it hold? Does it sag or fall? What you see is a clue.
3. **Try way B (10 min).** Now make it the second way with the second material. Watch
   again. Does it hold better, worse, or the same?
4. **Compare and choose (10 min).** Set the two ways side by side. Which one works
   better? **Choose** one — and say **why** you chose it ("it is stronger," "it does
   not tip," "it is easier to make"). Your reason is what makes your choice a design.
5. **Share your why (10 min).** Tell a friend or grown-up the two ways you tried and
   the one you chose, and your reason. Listen to a friend's why — is their reason
   different from yours? Both can be good.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: trying two ways and choosing with a reason is how
   everything good gets made. There is no "wrong" choice — only a choice with or
   without a reason.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer one clear problem and two very different ways, and ask "which
  holds better?" so the compare is easy to see.
- **Support (fine motor):** The learner directs each way and decides the choice while
  a partner builds.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the chosen way and to the reason on a picture
  card.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Compare by feel and sound with a partner describing
  what happened; the learner decides and says the reason.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to try a third way and compare all three, explaining
  which they chose and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner compare two ways to solve a making
  problem, choose one, and explain (or show) why?

## Home connection

At home, find a small problem — a wobbly lid, a slipping knot, a tilting pile. Try two
ways to fix it with a grown-up, choose one, and tell them why.
