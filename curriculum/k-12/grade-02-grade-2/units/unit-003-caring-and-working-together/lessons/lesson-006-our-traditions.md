# Lesson 6 — Our Traditions

## Summary

Learners share a tradition or celebration their family or community keeps, and
discover that traditions everywhere — harvests, new years, naming days, weekly meals,
songs — give a group the same gifts: sharing, remembering, and belonging.

## Objectives

- Describe a community tradition or celebration and what it means to the group.
  _(D02.S3.02.02)_

## Connection

Think of a special time your family or neighborhood shares — a birthday, a harvest
feast, a new year, a weekly meal, a song or a dance, a moment of quiet together.
These are **traditions**: things a group does again and again, because they mean
something. Traditions are everywhere. A harvest festival thanks the earth for food. A
new year brings people together to start again. A naming day welcomes a new person.
Different as they look, traditions give every group the same gifts: they help us
**share**, **remember**, and **belong**. Today you will share one of yours and hear
the traditions of others.

## Materials

- Paper and crayons
- A talking stick or turn marker

## Preparation

- Arrange a circle for gathering.
- Set out paper and crayons for drawing.

## Facilitator note

Traditions are one of the places the global and anthropological lens lives most
naturally: celebrate the variety without ranking any tradition above another. The
commitments to hold: **every family and community shape is honored** — a learner may
have one parent, two, many, or a chosen family of neighbors and friends; a tradition
belongs to whatever community the learner names, and no one is asked to defend or
explain it. **Sharing is always by choice**, never requirement: a learner may pass, or
draw instead of speak, or tell a tradition quietly to a partner rather than the whole
group. Some traditions are private or sacred; a learner may share only what they
wish. A non-speaking learner shares by drawing or pointing to a picture; a learner
whose tradition is not well known here is a teacher today, not a curiosity. Read the
procedure aloud and share a tradition of your own to open the circle.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Think: what special time does my family or
   community share? What do we do together?
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** Wonder together: what is a tradition? Look at examples
   from around the world — a harvest feast, a new year, a naming day, a weekly meal, a
   song. Ask: what does each one give the group? Discover the three gifts: **share**,
   **remember**, **belong**.
3. **Draw your tradition (15 min).** Draw one tradition your family or community
   shares. Add the people, the food, the songs, the season. If you like, add a word
   for what it means.
4. **Sharing circle (10 min).** Pass the turn marker. When it comes to you, share your
   tradition if you want to — with words, with your drawing, or by acting a part of
   it. Listen to the traditions of others and find what is the same and what is
   different.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: traditions may look different, but they give every
   group the same gifts — sharing, remembering, and belonging.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer picture prompts (food, music, lights, gathering) and let a
  learner point to what their tradition includes. Sharing may be by drawing only.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to learn one tradition from a different culture and
  tell what gift it gives its group — share, remember, or belong.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe a tradition and say what it
  means to the group?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's tradition drawing, kept as a record of
  their community.

## Home connection

At home, ask a grown-up to tell you the story of one family tradition — where it came
from and why it matters — and share what you learned with the class next time.
