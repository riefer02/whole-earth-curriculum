# Lesson 1 — Words That Encourage

## Summary

Learners discover that encouraging words help a friend keep going, tell the
difference between a sincere compliment and empty praise, and practice giving real
encouragement during an activity. They learn that every language has its own cheers.

## Objectives

- Give a sincere compliment or word of encouragement to a classmate during an
  activity. _(D02.S1.02.01)_

## Connection

Think of a time you tried something hard — a puzzle that would not fit, a word that
would not come out, a jump you could not make yet. Then someone said, "You can do
it," or "Nice try," and you tried one more time. Encouraging words help people keep
going. All over the world — on a playing field, at a harvest, in a classroom — people
cheer each other on, and every language has its own cheers. Today you will learn to
give real encouragement that helps a friend keep going.

## Materials

- Chart paper and markers
- A soft turn marker (ball, stone, or beanbag)
- Sentence-starter cards ("You can do it!", "Nice try!", "I like how you...",
  "Keep going!")

## Preparation

- Arrange a circle for gathering.
- Hang a blank "cheer wall" chart where everyone can reach it.

## Facilitator note

Encouragement is a skill children build by doing, not by being told to be nice
(CASEL's relationship-skills competency). The key idea to protect is **sincere vs.
empty**: a compliment names something true and specific ("I like how you shared your
crayons"), while empty praise is vague and rote ("good job" about nothing). Model the
difference and invite learners to say *what* they are cheering for. Encourage never
requires — and never ranks: a child is not "better" for giving more cheers, and no
one is judged for cheering quietly. A learner who does not speak with their mouth
gives encouragement with sign, a gesture, a smile, or a picture card — all count
fully. Invite, never require, learners to share how they cheer at home; sign languages
are languages too. Some learners may not feel safe sharing a home language, and
saying "I cheer quietly at home" is fine. Read the procedure aloud; model each cheer
with voice and body.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Think: when did someone's words help you keep
   going? What did they say?
2. **Meet the cheers (10 min).** Say — or sign — "You can do it!", "Nice try!",
   "I like how you...", and "Keep going!" together. Act each one out: a fist in the
   air for "You can do it!", a thumbs-up for "Nice try!". Ask yourself: what cheers
   do we say in my family, in my language? Share one if you want to.
3. **Compliment circle (15 min).** Pass the turn marker. When it comes to you, give
   one *sincere* encouragement to the person beside you — something true and specific
   about what they are doing or trying. Then it is their turn.
4. **Cheer wall (10 min).** Draw or write one encouraging word — one from class and,
   if you like, one from home. Add it to the wall.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a real cheer names something true. Your words can help
   a friend keep going — in any language.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a sentence starter ("I like how you…") and model one cheer with a
  gesture. A learner who does not speak with their mouth uses sign or a picture cheer
  card as their way to encourage.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to teach the class one encouraging word in a home
  language and add it to the wall with a drawing of when they would use it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner give a sincere, specific encouragement
  to a classmate during the activity?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The learner's cheer-wall drawing or card.

## Home connection

At home, cheer on a family member who is trying something hard — say what you are
cheering for, and notice how it helps them keep going.
