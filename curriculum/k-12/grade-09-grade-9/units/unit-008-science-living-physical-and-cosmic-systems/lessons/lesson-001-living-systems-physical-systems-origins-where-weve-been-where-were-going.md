# Lesson 1 — Living Systems, Physical Systems & Our Origins: Where We've Been, Where We're Going

## Summary

Learners retrieve the **evidence-and-source discipline** from Unit 1 and map it onto the three
bodies of this unit — **living systems, physical systems, and Earth, space, and origins**. They
preview the six objectives and commit to the rule that will govern the whole unit: **evidence,
then claim, with limits stated**.

## Objectives

- Construct an evidence-based explanation that links data to a claim and states its limits and
  uncertainties. _(D06.S1.09.01)_
- Critically evaluate a source of scientific information for reliability, bias, and conflicts of
  interest. _(D06.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

You already know how a good answer to a hard question is built. In Unit 1 you learned to look at
the data, state what you can honestly claim from it, and say where the claim runs out — and to ask
*who* is telling you, *why*, and *who paid for it*. That same discipline now has a bigger job. This
unit asks three of the biggest questions science holds: how living things fit together, how matter
and energy behave, and where we — and everything else — came from. The method does not change; the
questions just get deeper.

## Materials

- Unit map sheet
- Sticky notes or small cards

## Preparation

- Copy the unit map sheet (or draw the three panels on a shared board).
- Retrieval: from Unit 1 (U.09.001), recall what made an explanation *evidence-based* (data → claim
  → limits) and what made a source *trustworthy* (reliability, bias, conflict of interest).
- From Grade 8, recall the systems you met: cells, genes, ecosystems, atoms, energy, stars, and
  plate tectonics.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its job is **retrieval and framing, not new
content.** The rule to land, in the learner's own words: **an evidence-based explanation links data
to a claim and states its limits and uncertainties (S1.09.01), and a source is judged for
reliability, bias, and conflicts of interest (S1.09.02).** Use a worked example first (S-011):
recall a claim from Unit 1 and ask what data backed it and what limit it admitted. The
critical-thinking lens is the spine: the discipline is the same whether the object is a forest, an
atom, or the age of the cosmos. The ethics lens: stating limits is honesty — pretending certainty
where there is none is a small falsehood, and in science that falsehood has consequences. The
egalitarian lens: asking "who funded this, and who benefits?" is how we keep knowledge from being
captured by the powerful. The global lens: this discipline is not one nation's invention — careful
observation, measurement, and honest reporting belong to every people; astronomy, medicine, and
natural history grew in many places at once. Keep origins explicitly open: we will meet accounts of
beginnings as *models with evidence, never creed* (philosophy §11). Cite the source-evaluation
practice from Unit 1; no new factual claim needs a new citation here.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (8 min).** From Unit 1: what two moves make an explanation *evidence-based*? (Name the
   data, state the claim, say the limit.) And what three questions do you ask of a source?
   (Reliable? Biased? Who pays for it?)
2. **Meet the map (10 min).** Look at the unit map: three panels — **living systems**, **physical
   systems**, and **Earth & origins** — all standing on one base: *the evidence discipline*.
3. **Place the six ideas (15 min).** Here are the six objectives of the unit, in plain words. Sort
   each onto the panel where it belongs:
   - "Matter cycles and energy flows through ecosystems; disruptions change carrying capacity."
   - "Biodiversity matters for resilient ecosystems and human well-being."
   - "Energy is conserved but transformed, with efficiency; atoms bond and reactions move energy."
   - "The structure of the atom explains bonding and chemical energy."
   - "Greenhouse gases regulate climate, and human activity is changing it."
   - "Evidence lets us measure the age of the Earth and the universe."
4. **Commit to the rule (10 min).** Write one sentence completing this frame: "In this unit, I will
   not accept a claim unless I can see ___, and I will always ask ___ about a source."
5. **Close (7 min).** These are big questions. The method is small and sturdy: evidence, then claim,
   with limits — and wonder kept open the whole way.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the six ideas pre-written on cards and a labeled map; the learner matches them.
- **Extension:** Draft a full "claim → evidence → limit" sentence for one of the six ideas, using
  prior knowledge from Grade 8.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner restate, in their own words, what makes an explanation
  evidence-based and what makes a source trustworthy?
- **Self (portfolio):** The completed "I will not accept a claim unless…" sentence, kept in the
  portfolio as the unit's opening artifact.

## Home connection

Ask an adult at home: "How do you decide whether to believe a claim you read?" Listen for the three
source questions — and share the evidence discipline you will use all unit.
