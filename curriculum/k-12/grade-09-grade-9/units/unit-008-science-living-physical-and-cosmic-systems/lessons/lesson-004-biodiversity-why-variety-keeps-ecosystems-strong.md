# Lesson 4 — Biodiversity: Why Variety Keeps Ecosystems Strong

## Summary

Learners describe **biodiversity** — the variety of living things — and explain why it matters: a
**diverse ecosystem is resilient** because many species can do each job, so losing one does not
collapse the whole, while a **monoculture** concentrates risk. They connect variety to **human
well-being** — food, water, medicine, and climate.

## Objectives

- Describe biodiversity and explain why it matters for resilient ecosystems and human well-being.
  _(D06.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

Picture a market with a hundred different stalls. If one stall closes, you still have bread, fish, and
fruit. Now picture a market with only one stall selling one thing; if it closes, the market is empty.
An ecosystem works the same way. **Biodiversity** — the variety of living things — is like a
many-stalled market. It spreads risk, so the system can bend without breaking. And people are not
outside this market: our food, water, medicine, and air all come from it.

## Materials

- Resilience comparison sheet
- Biodiversity case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the resilience comparison sheet and case cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall species relationships (predation, competition, symbiosis); from
  Grade 8, recall how one changed population ripples through an ecosystem.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **biodiversity is the variety of
living things — within a species, between species, and across ecosystems; it matters because (1) a
diverse ecosystem is resilient — many species perform similar roles, so if one is lost, others hold
the system up; and (2) human well-being depends on it directly — food, clean water, medicine,
pollination, and a stable climate all come from living variety.** Use a worked example first (S-011):
the diverse-versus-monoculture contrast — in a field of one crop, a single disease can wipe out
everything, while a mixed system keeps producing. The environment lens: biodiversity loss is a
shared, documented concern — the authoritative international assessment (IPBES) reports that many
species are declining and that this is driven by land and sea use change, overexploitation, climate
change, pollution, and invasive species (S-437). The egalitarian lens: the people who depend most
directly on wild biodiversity — small farmers, fishers, and Indigenous communities — are often the
least responsible for its loss and the first to suffer it, so protecting biodiversity is inseparable
from fairness (S-437; also FAO on biodiversity for food and agriculture, S-178). The ethics lens:
variety is not only useful — there is a case, held by many peoples, that other species deserve care
for their own sake, not only for what they give us; hold this as a value, distinct from the
ecological facts. The global lens: biodiversity is distributed unevenly across the Earth — tropical
forests and coral reefs hold outsized shares — so its stewardship is a worldwide, not a local, task
(S-220, S-218). Cite IPBES (S-437), CBD (S-279), FAO (S-220), and WWF (S-218); keep numbers hedged.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7 and 8: name three kinds of relationships between species. What
   happens when one population changes?
2. **Meet biodiversity (8 min).** **Biodiversity** is the variety of living things — among individuals,
   between species, and across whole ecosystems.
3. **Model resilience (12 min).** Worked example: compare a **diverse** ecosystem (many species doing
   each job) with a **monoculture** (one crop). Remove one kind from each. In the diverse system,
   others fill the gap; in the monoculture, the system empties. Annotate the sheet.
4. **Read the case cards (12 min).** Each card names an ecosystem and the benefits its variety gives
   people — a coral reef (food, coastal protection), a forest (medicine, carbon storage), a grassland
   (grazing, soil), a rice field (food, water). List the human benefit of variety in each.
5. **Connect variety to well-being (10 min).** Write one sentence per case: "If ___ loses its variety,
   people lose ___."
6. **Close (8 min).** Variety is not decoration; it is the load-bearing frame of the living world —
   and of the human world built on it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a picture of one diverse ecosystem; the learner lists the different living
  things and one benefit people get from each.
- **Extension:** Write a short argument weighing the high short-term yield of a monoculture against
  its fragility, and propose how to hedge the risk.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner define biodiversity and give one ecological reason and
  one human reason it matters?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The annotated resilience comparison sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Look at a meal or a garden. How many different living things are in it? Ask: if one of them
disappeared, what would the rest — and the people who eat it — lose?
