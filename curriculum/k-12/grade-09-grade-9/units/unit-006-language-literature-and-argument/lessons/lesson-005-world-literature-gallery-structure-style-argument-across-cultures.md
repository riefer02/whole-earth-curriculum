# Lesson 5 — World Literature Gallery: Structure, Style & Argument Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners run the Lesson 4 analysis on a **chosen text from world literature** — Du Fu, Rumi, Neruda, Tagore,
Joy Harjo, or the Sundiata epic — finding each writer's structure, style, and argument in their own form. The
gallery makes visible that the same three tools of text are wielded differently in every language and
tradition.

## Objectives

- Analyze how a text's structure, style, and argument work together to create meaning and effect, using a
  text from a world literature beyond the Western canon. _(D04.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

No single place invented the art of saying something well. For as long as people have gathered, they have
made poems and epics and songs — a Tang dynasty poet counting the petals of a spring he may never see again,
a Persian mystic longing for the beloved, a Chilean poet celebrating an onion, a Bengali writer offering
songs to the world, a Mvskoke poet telling a young person to remember, a West African griot keeping a
king's history alive by word of mouth. Each one orders words, chooses a voice, and makes a claim — each in
their own way. Today you walk through this gallery and choose one voice to study closely.

## Materials

- World-literature gallery cards
- Structure-style-argument sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the gallery cards and the three-column sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall the three parts — structure, style, argument — and how they combine into
  meaning and effect. Today we apply the same tool to six texts from six traditions.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the same three tools — structure, style,
argument — are wielded differently in every language and tradition, and reading across cultures is how a
mature reader learns that no one way of writing is the "normal" one.** The six texts (with faithful, brief
translations) are: **Du Fu** (杜甫, Tang China, 8th c.) — a poem of war and home, whose structure moves from
what the eye sees to what the heart fears (S-418); **Rumi** (Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī, Persia, 13th c.) — a short
lyric of longing whose style is ecstatic address and whose argument is that the heart's longing points
beyond itself (S-419); **Pablo Neruda** (Chile, 20th c.) — an "ode to an everyday thing," whose structure
elevates a humble object and whose argument is that the small and ordinary are worthy of praise (S-420);
**Rabindranath Tagore** (Bengal, 20th c.) — a lyric from *Gitanjali* ("song-offerings"), whose style is
prayer-like and whose argument is that the divine and the everyday touch (S-421); **Joy Harjo** (Mvskoke
[Creek] Nation, contemporary) — "Remember," whose structure is a litany of anaphora ("Remember…") and whose
argument is that memory links a person to sky, earth, ancestors, and language (S-422); and a **Sundiata**
passage (Mali, oral epic kept by griots) whose structure is episodic praise and whose argument is that a
people's history lives by being spoken and remembered (S-269). Keep each text short and faithful; treat
translation honestly — a translated poem is a re-creation, and something is always gained and lost (note
this; do not rank the original above the translation). The global lens is the heart of the lesson: these are
*living traditions*, honored with respectful questions, never specimens (philosophy §4, D04 rationale). The
critical-thinking lens: the three-column tool works on all six precisely *because* structure, style, and
argument are near-universal — but their forms are not. The ethics lens: honoring a text from another culture
means not flattening it into a "single story" (Lesson 4). The egalitarian lens: a world-literature gallery
that includes only one continent is not a gallery — it is a mirror; this set deliberately spans five
continents and both oral and written traditions. The technology & environment lens: writing, print, and
recording are how these traditions reach us today, while the Sundiata epic reminds us that story lived in
memory and voice long before writing — and several of these poems carry their own land, seasons, and
weather in their lines.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what are the three parts of a text, and what do they produce together?
2. **Walk the gallery (15 min).** Read (or hear) all six cards — Du Fu, Rumi, Neruda, Tagore, Joy Harjo,
   Sundiata. For each, note in one phrase: what is it *about*, and what *form* is it (poem, lyric, ode,
   epic)?
3. **Choose one (5 min).** Pick the text that pulled at you. You will study it closely.
4. **Run the three columns (20 min).** Fill the sheet for your chosen text: *structure* (how is it ordered
   — a list? a scene? a litany of "remember"?), *style* (what words or rhythms repeat? who is speaking?),
   *argument* (what claim does it make about the world?).
5. **Share one line of insight (7 min).** In a small group, each person reads one line from their text and
   names one thing its structure, style, or argument is doing. Listen for how different the voices sound.
6. **Close (3 min).** Six traditions, one tool, six different musics. Next: we put several texts together
   to form a position of our own.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a guided version of the sheet with one prompt filled per text ("Harjo's repeated word
  is…").
- **Extension:** Take two texts and write one paragraph on how their *styles* differ — and what each style
  makes you feel that the other does not.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** Choose, read, and annotate the text in writing or on a device; share a line by
  displaying it.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Work from the written cards; "rhythm" and "voice" are visible on the page in
  repetition and line breaks, and a signed recitation is a real performance of a text.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner locate structure, style, and argument in a text from a
  tradition they did not already know?
- **Peer:** Each learner shares one accurate, specific line of insight about their text.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed three-column sheet for the chosen text, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a song, poem, or saying from your own or another tradition — what does it claim,
and how is it said? Bring one example to the next lesson.
