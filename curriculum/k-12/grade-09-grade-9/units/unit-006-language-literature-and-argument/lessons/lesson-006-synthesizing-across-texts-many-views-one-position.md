# Lesson 6 — Synthesizing Across Texts: Many Views, One Position

## Summary

Learners **synthesize four texts on one topic — "home"** — by a Chilean, Palestinian, Mvskoke, and Bengali
writer, finding what they share, naming where they differ, and writing one supported position of their own
that cites the texts. This moves them from analyzing one text to *speaking for themselves* across several.

## Objectives

- Synthesize ideas and evidence from multiple texts on the same topic to form a supported position.
  _(D04.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

No single text tells the whole truth about anything that matters. Ask four people what "home" means and you
will get four answers: for one it is the smell of a kitchen, for another the ache of a place they had to
leave, for another the land their ancestors walked, for another a road that never quite ends. Each answer is
true and each is partial. To form a position of your own — one you can defend — you do not pick your
favorite and ignore the rest; you put them in conversation, keep the parts that pull against each other, and
then say something that is *yours*, supported by theirs. Today you do that with four poets.

## Materials

- Multi-text synthesis sheet
- Four short texts on "home"

## Preparation

- Copy the synthesis sheet and the four texts.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 2 and 4, recall synthesis (many voices → one account) and the three-column
  reading tool. Today we combine them: many *texts* → one supported position.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **synthesizing across texts means (1)
noting each text's idea and evidence, (2) naming what they share, (3) holding the tension where they
differ — and only then (4) stating a position of your own that cites them.** The four texts on "home":
**Neruda** (Chile) — an ode locating home in humble, loved things; **Mahmoud Darwish** (Palestine) — a poem
locating home in exile and the refusal to be erased from it (S-423); **Joy Harjo** (Mvskoke) — "Remember"
locating home in sky, earth, ancestors, and language (S-422); **Tagore** (Bengal) — a *Gitanjali* lyric
locating home in the road and the small, everyday meeting (S-421). The shared idea: home is not only a
building — it is carried in memory, people, and place. The tension: is home something you *have* (Neruda's
loved things) or something you can be *cut off from* (Darwish's exile)? A mature synthesis holds both: home
is precious *precisely because* it can be lost. This is a *skill*, so use a **worked example** of a
two-text synthesis first, then let learners add the other two texts (S-011). The critical-thinking lens: a
position that ignores the tension is weaker, not stronger — name it. The ethics lens: honor each writer's
meaning; do not bend a poem to fit your position — if a text resists, say so. The egalitarian lens: a
Palestinian, a Mvskoke, a Chilean, and a Bengali writer each speak of home; a synthesis that treats any one
as "exotic" or any one as "universal default" fails them all. The global lens: "home" is one of the oldest
and most widespread themes in human literature — and its meaning changes with each life. The technology &
environment lens: a shared document lets a synthesis be written together across distance, and in these four
poems "home" is often land and place as much as people — so what is lost with exile or displacement is also
a piece of the living world. Keep the texts
short and faithful; note that translation is a re-creation (as in Lesson 5). Cite by naming author and
title, so a reader can find the poem.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what is synthesis? From Lesson 4: what are the three parts of a
   text? Today we synthesize across *several texts* on one topic.
2. **Meet the topic (3 min).** The topic is **home**. Before reading, write your own one-line answer: what
   does home mean to you?
3. **Read the four texts (15 min).** Read (or hear) Neruda, Darwish, Joy Harjo, and Tagore on home. For
   each, note its *idea* (what it says home is) and one *piece of evidence* (a line or image that carries
   it).
4. **Find shared ideas and tension (12 min).** Fill the two boxes: **what they share** (the idea all four
   gesture toward) and **the tension** (where they pull apart — e.g., home as held vs. home as lost).
5. **Write your position (15 min).** Write one paragraph that begins with your own position, then supports
   it by citing at least two of the texts by author and title — and names the tension honestly. Keep the
   line between *their* words and *your* judgment clear.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Ask a partner: is my position really *mine*, or just a summary? Did I cite the
   texts fairly? Did I hold the tension or smooth it over? Fix what is weak.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a two-text starter — synthesize Neruda and Darwish with a frame before adding the
  others — and a sentence stem for the position ("Taken together, these poets suggest that home is ___,
  because ___.").
- **Extension:** Add a fifth text on home of your own choosing and fold it into the synthesis, naming what
  it adds.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** The position can be composed in writing or on a device; the synthesis lives in the
  *merging*, not in the voice.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Work from the written texts; cite author and title in writing, and treat a
  signed rendering of a poem as a legitimate way of meeting the text.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a shared idea, a real tension, and write one position
  that cites at least two texts and separates their judgment from the texts'?
- **Peer check:** Partner confirms the citations are fair and the tension is not smoothed over.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed synthesis sheet with the position paragraph, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Bring one short text — a song lyric, a poem, a post — about "home" from a tradition you know or are
curious about, and add its one idea to your synthesis.
