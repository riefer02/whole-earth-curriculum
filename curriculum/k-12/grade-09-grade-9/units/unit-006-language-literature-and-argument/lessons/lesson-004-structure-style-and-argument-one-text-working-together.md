# Lesson 4 — Structure, Style & Argument: One Text Working Together

## Summary

Learners analyze how **structure, style, and argument** work together in one text — Chimamanda Ngozi
Adichie's "The Danger of a Single Story" — tracing how her ordering of personal stories (**structure**),
her direct, repetitive voice (**style**), and her claim that single stories flatten people (**argument**)
combine to create meaning and effect.

## Objectives

- Analyze how a text's structure, style, and argument work together to create meaning and effect.
  _(D04.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

You have felt the difference between a story told well and the same story told badly — even when the facts
are identical. What changed was not the information; it was how the teller *ordered* it, the *words* they
chose, and the *point* they were building toward. A text is a machine with three working parts: **structure**
(how the parts are arranged), **style** (the voice and word choice), and **argument** (the claim it is
making). When they pull together, the reader feels something; when they fight each other, the text falls
flat. Today you take one powerful text apart to see the three parts turning together.

## Materials

- Structure-style-argument sheet
- "The Danger of a Single Story" passage

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and the passage.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall how point of view, tone, and figurative language shape a reader's
  understanding. Today we add how *structure* and *argument* join them.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a text's structure (how it orders its
parts), style (its word choice, voice, and rhythm), and argument (its claim and reasons) are not three
separate things — they work together, and the effect on a reader comes from their combination.** Worked
example: Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's 2009 TED talk "The Danger of a Single Story" (S-417). Its *argument*:
when we know only a single story about a person or place, we risk flattening them — so we must seek many
stories. Its *structure*: a series of personal anecdotes (growing up in Nigeria reading British books; her
American roommate's assumptions; her own single story of a poor family) ordered to move from being the one
*flattened* to the one *flattening others*, which makes the thesis land as earned, not preached. Its
*style*: first person, plain and direct, with the phrase "single story" repeated like a refrain — a spoken,
confessional rhythm built for a live audience. Show that the *effect* — the reader's felt conviction that
single stories are dangerous — comes from all three at once. The critical-thinking lens: naming structure,
style, and argument is the beginning of *reading with a tool*, not the end; ask what the text does not say.
The global lens: Adichie writes from and about Nigeria, Britain, the United States, and the way Western
readers see Africa — a global argument about who gets to tell whose story. The ethics lens: the "single
story" is an ethical problem — it treats a person as a category, and the cure is to seek the many stories
that honor their full humanity. The egalitarian lens: whose stories get told, and whose get flattened into
a single image, is a question of power. The technology & environment lens: "The Danger of a Single Story"
is a spoken text preserved and spread by video and streaming — digital media changes how a single story can
travel, for harm and for good — and a transcript turns a voice into a page a reader can study. Keep the
passage short and faithful; do not over-quote (fair use,
S-417). Emphasize that these three categories are *lenses*, not boxes — real texts blur them.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: how do tone, point of view, and figurative language shape how a text
   makes you feel? Today we add two more moving parts — structure and argument.
2. **Meet the three parts (8 min).** **Structure** = how the parts are ordered. **Style** = word choice,
   voice, rhythm. **Argument** = the claim and its reasons. Together they produce **meaning and effect**.
3. **Read the passage (12 min).** Read (or hear) the excerpt from Adichie's "The Danger of a Single Story."
   On first reading, just notice: what is she *arguing*? Where does she begin, and where does she end?
4. **Fill the three columns (15 min).** With a partner, fill the sheet: *structure* (list the anecdotes in
   order — what does each one do?), *style* (find the repeated phrase and the first-person voice), and
   *argument* (state her thesis in one sentence).
5. **Name the effect (10 min).** Put it together: how does the *order* of her stories make her thesis hit
   harder? How does the *repetition* drive it home? Write one sentence: "The effect comes from all three
   because…"
6. **Close (5 min).** Share your sentence. You have just done what a mature reader does — you saw the
   machine behind the meaning.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three columns pre-filled with prompts ("one anecdote is…", "the repeated phrase
  is…", "her claim is…") and ask learners to complete them.
- **Extension:** Compare the *first* anecdote and the *last* one, and explain why Adichie placed them where
  she did.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** Analysis can be written, pointed to, or composed on a device; the three-column
  sheet is a visual tool that needs no speech.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Work from the written transcript; "style" then includes Adichie's sentence
  rhythm and repetition on the page, not only her voice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the argument, list the structural moves, and find one
  stylistic choice — and say how the three combine into an effect?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed three-column sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Choose any text you meet this week — a song, a news post, a speech — and ask: what is its claim, how is it
ordered, and what words does it repeat? Bring one finding to the next lesson.
