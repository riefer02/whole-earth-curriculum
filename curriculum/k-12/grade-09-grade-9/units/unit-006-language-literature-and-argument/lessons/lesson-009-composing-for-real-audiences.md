# Lesson 9 — Composing for Real Audiences

## Summary

Learners reshape their Lesson 7 argument for a **real audience and purpose**, choosing among a letter, a
proposal, or a published digital piece, and matching tone, length, and content to the reader. The message
stays theirs; the form follows the audience — and the piece is prepared for actual sharing.

## Objectives

- Compose for real audiences and purposes, such as letters, proposals, or published digital pieces.
  _(D04.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

The same truth is said differently to different people. You would not explain a problem to a young child the
way you would to an engineer, and you would not ask a busy official the way you would ask a friend. Writing
comes alive when it has a *real reader* on the other side — someone who will actually open the letter, read
the proposal, or scroll the post. That reader decides how you open, how long you go, and what you lead with.
Today you take the argument you already built and aim it at a real person, so that it can actually do
something in the world.

## Materials

- Audience-shaping sheet
- Model real-audience pieces

## Preparation

- Copy the audience-shaping sheet and the three model pieces; identify a real recipient where possible.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall the five-part argument; from Grade 8, recall writing for different
  audiences and choosing a style to match. Today we aim the argument at a real reader.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **form follows audience — the same core
message is shaped differently for a letter (one reader, respectful, personal), a proposal (a decision-maker,
practical, with a plan of action), and a published digital piece (many readers, clear, scannable, shareable).**
This is a *skill*, so use **worked examples, then guided practice** (S-011): show the three model pieces on
the same local issue side by side and have learners name *what changed* between them and *why* (audience and
purpose). Then each learner chooses one form and reshapes their own Lesson 7 argument. The ethics lens:
writing to a real reader is a relationship — be honest, do not inflate, do not misrepresent a source to
persuade. The egalitarian lens: a real audience is also a question of access — who can read a long formal
letter, who can reach a digital post, whose language is the piece written in? A writer for real readers
thinks about who is being addressed and who is being left out, and may write in more than one language or
add a plain-language version. The global lens: letters, petitions, proposals, and community notices are how
people make change everywhere — in a village, a city, a school — and the same craft applies in any language.
The technology lens: a published digital piece reaches farther and faster than paper, but it also lasts
longer and can spread beyond the intended reader — so choose words with care (S-063). Emphasize **real
sharing**: the piece is not finished until it is delivered or published, even if only to a class wall, a
newsletter, or a real official. This lesson's artifact is the capstone of the writing strand.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: what is your argument's claim and its strongest cited evidence? Today
   you aim it at a real reader.
2. **Meet the three forms (10 min).** **A letter** = one reader, respectful, personal. **A proposal** = a
   decision-maker, practical, with a plan of action. **A published digital piece** = many readers, clear,
   scannable, shareable.
3. **Compare the models (10 min).** Read the three model pieces on the same issue. In each, find: *who* is
   the reader, *what* is the purpose, and *how* did the form change the opening, the length, and the tone?
4. **Choose your audience and form (5 min).** Pick a real reader for your Lesson 7 argument — an official, a
   principal, a council, a neighborhood group, or a wider public — and choose the form that fits.
5. **Draft your piece (20 min).** Write the opening, the claim, the cited evidence, and a closing that asks
   the reader to do something. Match tone and length to your reader. If you write for a public audience,
   keep it clear and scannable.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Ask a partner to play your reader: is the tone right for them? Is the ask clear?
   Would they act? Fix what does not fit.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide an opening line for each form ("Dear ___, I am writing because…", "This proposal asks
  ___ to…", "Here is one thing you should know about ___.") and a shorter required length.
- **Extension:** Produce the same argument in a second form, and write one sentence on what changed and why.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** The piece is written; a learner drafts on paper or a device, and the reader can be
  real even if the writer does not speak.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Written and digital forms need no speech; a proposal or letter is delivered in
  writing or sign, and "tone" is read on the page.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/observation):** Can the learner match tone, length, and content to a named real
  audience and purpose, and keep the argument's evidence intact?
- **Peer check:** Partner, playing the reader, confirms the tone and the ask fit.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The finished real-audience piece — prepared for actual delivery or publication —
  kept in the portfolio (the capstone of the writing strand).

## Home connection

Deliver or publish your piece this week — send the letter, hand over the proposal, post the piece (with
care), or share it with the person it was written for. Notice what your reader does with it.
