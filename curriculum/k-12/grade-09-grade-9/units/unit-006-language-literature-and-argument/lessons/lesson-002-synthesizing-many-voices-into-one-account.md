# Lesson 2 — Synthesizing Many Voices into One Account

## Summary

Learners practice **synthesizing multiple spoken sources** into one coherent account, using four short
first-person accounts of the same event. They find what the sources share, name where they differ, and
compose one account that honors every source before speaking for themselves.

## Objectives

- Synthesize information from multiple spoken sources and present a coherent account or argument.
  _(D04.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Every community meeting, every family retelling, every news story about your neighborhood is made this way:
several people saw or felt different pieces of the same thing, and someone has to put the pieces together.
If you only listened to one voice, you would have only one slice of the truth. Synthesis is the skill of
listening to *several* voices, noticing what they agree on and where they pull apart, and then telling one
honest account that does not pretend the differences are not there. Today you practice that skill.

## Materials

- Source-synthesis sheet
- Four spoken-source cards

## Preparation

- Copy the synthesis sheet and the four source cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall paraphrasing a speaker's points and distinguishing a main argument from
  supporting details. Today we move from *one* source to *several*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **synthesis is more than summarizing —
it is (1) noting each source's main point and key detail, (2) finding the *overlap* (what they agree on),
(3) naming the *difference* (where they conflict), and (4) composing one coherent account that honors both,
before stating your own supported judgment.** This is a *skill*, so use a **worked example first, then
guided practice** (explicit instruction carries skills for novices — S-011). Model it: take a simple,
non-controversial topic (e.g., "should the school courtyard host a weekly market?") and show four one-line
voices — a student who wants the music, a vendor who wants the income, a neighbor who wants quiet, a
teacher who wants the space for study — then show how a synthesis sounds: "Several people value the market
for different reasons (income, joy), while others worry about noise and lost space for study; what they
share is that the courtyard matters to them, and what they disagree on is how it should be used." The
critical-thinking lens: a synthesis that ignores a conflict is not synthesis — it is smoothing; hold the
tension. The ethics lens: each speaker's stake deserves to be reported faithfully, even the one you
disagree with. The egalitarian lens: notice *whose voice is missing* from the four sources — the person
left out of the meeting, the child, the speaker of a minority language — and say so; synthesis is fairer
when it names its own gaps. The global lens: this is how councils, elders, juries, and assemblies have made
sense of many voices in every culture — oral and written. The technology & environment lens: recording
lets a spoken source be re-heard and checked, and the accounts learners merge are often about a shared
place — a market, a road, a school — so synthesis is also how a community decides about its shared world.
Keep the worked example concrete and the four
practice cards short enough to hold in memory.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what does it mean to paraphrase a speaker's points accurately, and
   how do you find the main argument among the details?
2. **Meet synthesis (10 min).** Watch the worked example: four one-line voices on one topic, then one
   synthesis that names the overlap and the difference. Notice it is *not* just a list — it is a new,
   single account.
3. **Read and mark the four sources (15 min).** With a group, read (or hear) the four source cards about
   the same event. For each, note: *main point* and *one key detail*.
4. **Find overlap and difference (10 min).** Fill the two columns: **what they share** (the idea every
   source agrees on) and **where they differ** (the point they pull apart on).
5. **Compose one account (10 min).** Write or speak one coherent paragraph: begin with what they share, name
   where they differ honestly, then add one sentence of your own judgment — clearly marked as yours.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Ask a partner: does my account include every source? Did I name a real
   disagreement rather than skip it? Did I separate my judgment from theirs? Fix what is missing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a synthesis frame — "These speakers agree that ___. They differ on ___. Taken
  together, this suggests ___."
- **Extension:** Name whose voice is *absent* from the four sources, and write one sentence explaining how
  that absence might change the account.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** The synthesis can be composed in writing, by arranging cards, or on a device; the
  skill is in the *merging*, not in the voice.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Run the four sources as written cards or signed retellings; "spoken sources"
  includes any language, signed or spoken, and synthesis is the same act of merging.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner pull one main point from each source, name the overlap and a
  difference, and state one account that separates their own judgment from the sources'?
- **Peer check:** Partner confirms all four sources appear and a real disagreement is named.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed synthesis sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Tonight, ask two people at home the same question — "what makes our neighborhood good?" — and try to say
back, in one sentence, what they agree on and where they differ.
