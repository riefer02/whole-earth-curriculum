# Lesson 7 — Writing a Research-Supported Argument: Claim, Evidence, Citation

## Summary

Learners write the first draft of a **research-supported argument**, using a five-part frame — claim, reason,
evidence with citation, answer to a counterargument, and a supported position — modeled first, then drafted on
a chosen topic with at least two cited sources. They learn to cite so a reader can find the source, in their
own words, not a single national style.

## Objectives

- Write a research-supported argument that integrates and cites sources and evaluates conflicting evidence.
  _(D04.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

An opinion is cheap; an argument is earned. Saying "I think the market should move" costs nothing. Saying
"the market should move *because* the vendors' own survey shows most customers cannot reach it, and the
town's own report shows the current site floods each rainy season" — that is an argument, because it rests on
evidence someone else can check, and it names where that evidence came from. The difference between the two
is exactly the difference between a voice people can dismiss and a voice people have to answer. Today you
build the second kind.

## Materials

- Argument-builder sheet
- Topic menu

## Preparation

- Copy the argument-builder sheet and topic menu; assemble the starter sources.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall writing an argument with a claim, reasons, evidence, and a response to a
  counterargument, and citing sources accurately. Today we add *research* — finding and integrating real
  sources — and *evaluating* conflicting evidence.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a research-supported argument has five
parts — (1) claim (what I think, said clearly); (2) reason (why I think it); (3) evidence (fact or example
from a source) *with a citation*; (4) an answer to a counterargument; and (5) a supported position that is
recognizably yours.** This is a *skill*, so use a **worked example, then guided practice** (S-011). Teach
citation **generically and globally**: a citation names *author, title, source, and date* so a reader can
find the evidence — this is the shared purpose behind every citation style, and we teach the *purpose*, not
one nation's format (a learner may use the style their region uses). Model the worked example fully — a
claim about a real, local, low-heat topic (e.g., "our school should compost its food waste") with a reason,
a cited piece of evidence, and an honest answer to a counterargument. The critical-thinking lens: evidence
supports a claim; it does not *prove* it — teach learners to write "suggests" or "shows," not "proves"
(philosophy §5, §7). The ethics lens: citing is an act of honesty — it separates your words from others'
and gives credit; failing to cite is taking someone else's work (S-040 on separating one's words from
others'). The egalitarian lens: whose knowledge counts as a citable "source" matters — a local elder's
testimony and a government report are both sources, and a learner's home-language source is a real source,
not a lesser one. The global lens: the topic menu spans many places and stakes, and learners may choose any
topic they can research. The technology lens: where a device is available, a source is found and checked
online; where not, printed articles and people's testimony serve the same purpose. Keep the topics real but
not explosive; the point is the *skill*.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what are the parts of an argument, and what does it mean to cite a
   source accurately?
2. **Meet the five parts (8 min).** **Claim** → **reason** → **evidence + citation** → **answer a
   counterargument** → **supported position**. A citation names *author, title, source, date* so a reader
   can find it.
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** Read the model argument about a real local topic. Trace each of
   the five parts, and find the citation. Notice the claim uses "suggests," not "proves."
4. **Choose a topic and gather two sources (12 min).** Pick from the menu (or propose your own). From the
   starter sources, note one piece of evidence for your claim and, if you can, one against it.
5. **Draft your argument (15 min).** Write the five parts: your claim, one reason, one cited piece of
   evidence, one honest answer to a counterargument, and your supported position. Keep your words and your
   sources' words visibly separate.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap with a partner. Can they find your claim, your citation, and your answer to
   a counterargument? Is each source named well enough to find? Fix what is missing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the worked example with the five parts labeled, and a fill-in frame for the draft
  ("My claim is ___. One reason is ___. A source that supports this is ___ [citation], which shows ___.").
- **Extension:** Integrate a source that partly disagrees, and add one sentence explaining how it qualifies
  (narrows) your claim.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** The argument is written; a learner drafts on paper or a device, and citation is
  done in writing, not speech.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** All drafting is written or typed; pair work can run in sign or text, and the
  five-part frame is visual.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner write a claim, a reason, a cited piece of evidence, and an
  answer to a counterargument — with their own words clearly separate from the source's?
- **Peer check:** Partner confirms the five parts are present and the citation is findable.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The draft argument, kept in the portfolio (revised in Lesson 8 and reshaped in
  Lesson 9).

## Home connection

Read one news article or post this week and ask: what is the claim, what is the evidence, and does it name
where the evidence came from? Bring one observation to the next lesson.
