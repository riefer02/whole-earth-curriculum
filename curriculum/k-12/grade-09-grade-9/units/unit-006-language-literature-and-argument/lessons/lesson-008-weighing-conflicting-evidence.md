# Lesson 8 — Weighing Conflicting Evidence

## Summary

Learners practice **weighing conflicting evidence**, using four criteria — **author, purpose, evidence, and
currency** — to judge two sources that disagree, then writing a verdict that either leans toward one source or
honestly holds the question unsettled. This completes the "evaluates conflicting evidence" half of writing a
research-supported argument.

## Objectives

- Evaluate conflicting evidence when writing a research-supported argument. _(D04.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever read two things that flatly disagree — one says a food is healthy, another says it is not; one
says a plan will save money, another says it will waste it? Your first instinct might be to believe the one
you already agreed with. That is not weighing; that is choosing. Weighing means asking *the same questions*
of both sides: Who wrote this, and do they have something to gain? Why was it written? What evidence does it
actually show? Is it still true today? Sometimes the answers tip the scale; sometimes they leave it balanced,
and the honest thing is to say "we do not know yet." Today you learn to weigh, not just to choose.

## Materials

- Evidence-weighing sheet
- Two-source conflict sets

## Preparation

- Copy the weighing sheet and conflict sets.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall evaluating a source's credibility by checking author, purpose, and
  evidence. Today we apply that to *two sources that disagree*, and we add the discipline of holding
  uncertainty when the scale stays balanced.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **when sources disagree, weigh them on
four criteria — (1) author (do they know this? do they gain from a certain answer?), (2) purpose (to inform
or to persuade?), (3) evidence (data and named facts, or only assertions?), and (4) currency (still true
today?) — then state a verdict: lean toward one source *with reasons*, or hold the question unsettled.** This
is a *skill*, so use a **worked example, then guided practice** (S-011). Model it on one conflict set, showing
that weighing is *not* "the fancier source always wins" — a company's polished report can lose to a
researcher's plain study on the evidence criterion, and a decades-old figure can lose to a recent one on
currency. The critical-thinking lens is the spine of the lesson: a mature mind can hold two contradictory
claims at once and judge between them *on criteria*, not on feeling (philosophy §7). The ethics lens: honest
weighing means naming when your own preference is pulling you — and resisting the temptation to let it. The
egalitarian lens: power shapes which sources look credible — a well-funded campaign can out-shout a local
community's testimony; weighing fairly means asking whose evidence is being dismissed and why. The global
lens: whose knowledge counts as credible has differed across times and places — oral testimony, an elder's
word, a written record — and weighing evidence fairly means not assuming one culture's standard of proof is
the only one. The technology lens: online, speed and polish masquerade as credibility; the same four
questions are the
antidote, and cross-checking against an independent source is the strongest single move (S-063). Keep the
conflict sets real but low-heat, and model the honest third outcome — "the evidence is not settled yet" — as
a strength, never a failure (philosophy §11, §5). This lesson feeds Lesson 7's draft: learners will return
to their own argument and weigh its conflicting sources.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: how do you check whether a source is credible? Today we use that on two
   sources that *disagree*.
2. **Meet the four criteria (8 min).** **Author** (who, and do they gain?), **purpose** (inform or
   persuade?), **evidence** (data or assertion?), **currency** (still true?). These are the weights on the
   scale.
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** Watch (or read) one conflict set weighed: the same four questions
   asked of both sources, then a verdict that leans one way *for stated reasons* — or holds the question
   open.
4. **Weigh a conflict set (15 min).** With a partner, take a two-source conflict. Fill the table for each
   source. Then write your verdict in one sentence: which source do you trust more, and on *which* criterion
   — or is the question unsettled?
5. **Cross-check (10 min).** If you can, check one disputed claim against a third, independent source. Does
   it confirm or complicate your verdict? Add one sentence.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Ask another pair: did we ask all four questions of *both* sources, or only the one
   we liked? Did we state our reasons, or just our preference? Fix what is weak.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence stem for the verdict ("I trust Source ___ more because on the criterion of
  ___ it ___."), and let learners weigh with one criterion at a time.
- **Extension:** Find a real conflict on the learner's own Lesson 7 topic and weigh it, then revise their
  draft to reflect the verdict.
- **Non-speaking / AAC:** The verdict and table are written; a learner weighs on paper or a device, and pair
  discussion can run in writing or sign.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** Run pair work in sign or text; the table is visual, and the four criteria can
  be pointed to as labels.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner apply all four criteria to *both* sources and state a verdict
  with reasons — including, where warranted, holding the question unsettled?
- **Peer check:** Another pair confirms both sources were judged on the same criteria and the reasons are
  stated.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed weighing sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Find two things that disagree this week — two reviews, two weather apps, two people's accounts of the same
event — and ask the four questions of each. What tips the scale?
