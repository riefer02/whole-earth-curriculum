# Lesson 2 — A Richer Feeling Vocabulary: Subtle and Secondary Emotions

## Summary

Learners build a **broad emotional vocabulary** by distinguishing **basic emotions**, **subtle
emotions** (fine-grained states such as envy, nostalgia, awe, and resentment), and **secondary
emotions** (feelings about feelings, such as shame about jealousy). They learn that naming a feeling
precisely — **emotional granularity** — is itself a skill that supports understanding and regulation.

## Objectives

- Distinguish basic, subtle, and secondary emotions and place feeling words into each group.
  _(D01.S1.09.01)_
- Use a broad emotional vocabulary to name a subtle or secondary emotion from your own life.
  _(D01.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

A friend shares amazing news, and you notice a small, sharp feeling that is *not* simple happiness
for them — but it is not exactly anger either. It is envy, and then a second feeling: a flush of
guilt or shame *about* the envy. Notice how the second feeling sits on top of the first, like a
reaction to a reaction. English calls that first one a *subtle* emotion and the second a *secondary*
emotion — a feeling about a feeling. The more words you have for these layers, the more clearly you
can see yourself, and the more choice you have about what to do next.

- Feeling vocabulary wheel

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the **first skill lesson** of the unit, so the
method is **explicit instruction with worked examples, then guided practice** (S-011), not free-form
discovery. Model each category yourself first, then have learners sort and generate words with a
partner before working alone. The key distinctions to teach: **(1) basic emotions** are broad,
early-developing states — joy, sadness, fear, anger (and, in some models, trust, disgust,
anticipation, surprise; Plutchik, S-402); **(2) subtle emotions** are finer-grained and often more
socially complex — envy, gratitude, nostalgia, awe, resentment, tenderness, longing; **(3) secondary
emotions** are *feelings about feelings* — shame about jealousy, guilt about anger, pride in courage,
relief after worry. The core claim to carry: having a richer feeling vocabulary — **emotional
granularity** — lets a person name their experience more precisely and is associated with better
emotion regulation (Barrett, S-401). This is a **description of how emotions work, offered as one
well-supported scientific account**, not a creed, and not a therapy; keep it that way (philosophy §5,
§11). The **global** lens: languages differ in which feelings have names — a feeling word in one
language may have no exact translation in another, which is a reminder that our vocabularies shape
(without fully deciding) what we notice. The **critical-thinking** lens: no single "wheel" of
emotions is settled fact; wheels are models, and learners should feel free to build their own.
Never rank feelings as "good" or "bad" — name them neutrally and honor them all (egalitarian spirit:
every self is a whole in the making, never a label).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Without looking, name the unit's three strands from Lesson 1. Which one are we
   beginning today?
2. **Worked example — basic and subtle (10 min).** Watch and listen as your facilitator models the
   two layers with a real example: "basic" anger at being cut in line, then the "subtle" feeling
   underneath — maybe resentment that this keeps happening, or hurt. What makes the second word more
   precise?
3. **Introduce secondary emotions (10 min).** Now the third layer: a *feeling about a feeling*.
   Model it: "I felt envy — and then I felt *ashamed* of feeling envious." The shame is the secondary
   emotion. In pairs, generate two more examples of a feeling about a feeling.
4. **Sort and add (15 min).** With a partner, sort the words on your word bank sheet into basic,
   subtle, and secondary. Add at least three new words of your own to each ring. If a word is hard to
   place, say why.
5. **Apply it to you (10 min).** Choose one subtle or secondary emotion you have felt recently. Write
   one sentence naming it precisely ("I felt ___ when ___, and then I felt ___ about that").
6. **Close (5 min).** Today you grew your feeling vocabulary. Next: we use it to read a complex
   moment.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter list and sentence frames ("I felt ___ when ___…").
- **Extension:** Collect feeling words from another language and share one that has no exact
  translation in yours, explaining what it captures.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner sort feeling words into basic, subtle, and secondary,
  and name a subtle or secondary emotion from their own life?
- **Artifact:** The completed emotion word bank (or the learner's "I felt ___ when ___" sentence),
  kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: is there a feeling you know but struggle to name? What do you call it? Add the
word to your word bank.
