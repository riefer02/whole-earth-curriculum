# Lesson 6 — My Story, Still Being Written: Past, Present, and Hoped-For Future

## Summary

Learners construct a **coherent personal narrative** linking their **past, present, and hoped-for
future** into one connected story. Using a past–present–future line and a worked example, they find a
single thread — a value, a theme, a turning point — that runs through all three, and tell their story
as one still being written.

## Objectives

- Construct a coherent personal narrative that connects your past, present, and hoped-for future.
  _(D01.S2.09.01)_
- Name the thread that links the three stages into one story. _(D01.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

If someone asked you right now, "So, who are you?", what would you say? Not your name — the story
under the name: where you come from, what matters to you, where you are headed. A scattered list of
facts is not an identity; a *story* is. And a story has a thread — one thing that shows up in the
past, is alive in the present, and reaches toward the future. Maybe it is curiosity, or loyalty, or
the pull of a certain place or craft. Today you find your thread and tell your story as one
connected life — not a finished book, but a story still being written.

## Materials

- Story-line sheet
- Worked-example narrative

## Preparation

- Copy the story-line sheet and the worked-example narrative.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the unit's second strand — identity and self-concept. Today we
  begin it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is a **skill lesson**: building a coherent
narrative is taught with a **worked example, then guided practice** (S-011), not left to blank-page
discovery. The psychological idea behind it is **narrative identity** — people make sense of who
they are by constructing an evolving story of the self that links the reconstructed past, the felt
present, and the imagined future, and this story gives life unity and purpose (McAdams, S-404). The
linked notion of **possible selves** — the selves a person imagines becoming, hoping to become, or
fearing to become — sits between the present self and future motivation (Markus & Nurius, S-399).
Hold these as **descriptive accounts, not a required genre or a verdict**: a learner's story may be
fragmented, uncertain, or multiple — that is human, and it is fine. The **ethics** lens: a narrative
is an interpretation, and the same life can be honestly told more than one way; teach learners to
tell their own story with honesty *and* kindness to themselves, not to build a flattering myth. The
**global** lens: cultures differ in how a life story is told — some center ancestors and lineage,
some center place, some center individual achievement — and no one form is the right one. **Safety
is paramount**: this can be tender material. Every sharing is optional; personal narratives are
shared only in small, trusted groups, never demanded aloud; a learner who has experienced harm or
loss may write only what feels safe, or write about a character instead. Connect difficulty to
agency and hope, never to shame (philosophy §12). The future is a direction, not a deadline — "I
don't know yet" is a complete and honest answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Name the unit's three strands. Today we begin identity and self-concept.
2. **Worked example (10 min).** Listen as your facilitator tells a short, *invented* narrative that
   runs one thread through a past, a present, and a hoped-for future. Notice how the same value shows
   up in all three.
3. **Map your line (15 min).** On your story-line sheet, note the people, places, and turning points
   of your **past**; what matters to you **now**; and who you **hope to become**. Keep it to what
   feels safe to write.
4. **Find the thread (10 min).** Look across your three columns. What appears in more than one — a
   value, a curiosity, a loyalty, a place, a craft? Write it in one sentence.
5. **Tell the story (15 min).** Write or speak a short narrative that carries that thread from past,
   through present, toward the future. It does not need to be long; it needs to be *yours* and
   *connected*.
6. **Close (5 min).** You did not write a finished book; you found the thread of a story still being
   written. Next: how the world shapes who you are.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the worked example's frame ("In the past I…; now I…; I hope to…; the thread is…").
- **Extension:** Identify a single theme that runs through all three stages and state it as a title
  for your story.

## Assessment

- **Formative (portfolio):** The personal narrative (or story line) linking past, present, and
  hoped-for future, with a named thread — kept in the portfolio. Sharing is optional; the artifact
  itself is the evidence.
- **Self:** The learner checks: does one thread appear in all three stages?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you one short story from their own past — and ask them what it taught
them that still matters now. Notice the thread.
