# Lesson 4 — Shaped by Where I Stand: Context, Belonging, and Emotion

## Summary

Learners analyze how **social context** and **group belonging** shape what they feel — how the same
event can feel different depending on where they stand and who is watching. They distinguish what a
situation amplifies from what is genuinely theirs, and practice noticing the pull without being ruled
by it.

## Objectives

- Analyze how social context and group belonging shape what and how strongly a person feels.
  _(D01.S1.09.02)_
- Distinguish the part of a feeling that comes from the situation from the part that is your own.
  _(D01.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

The same news — say, a mistake you made — can feel completely different depending on where you are.
Made it in front of a group you trust, it might feel like a small, almost funny thing, and they help
you fix it. Made it in front of people you are trying to impress, the very same slip can feel like a
weight on your chest. The event did not change; the *context* did. Add to that the pull of
*belonging* — how much we want to be inside a group, and how much it stings to be outside — and you
can see that feelings are real but never happen in a vacuum. Today you learn to see the situation's
part in what you feel.

## Materials

- Context cards
- Shapers-of-emotion sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the context cards and shapers-of-emotion sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 2–3, recall the four-layer frame. Today we ask *where the feeling comes
  from* — the situation's part.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It shifts from *naming* a feeling to *analyzing its
causes* — the second emotional-literacy goal (D01.S1.09.02). The core ideas to teach: **(1) social
context** — the same event evokes different feelings in different settings (a mistake among friends
versus among strangers); **(2) group belonging** — people are deeply sensitive to being inside or
outside a group, and this shapes emotion strongly (Tajfel's minimal-group work showed how readily
people favor their in-group; S-232); **(3) display rules** — cultures and groups differ in which
feelings are shown, hidden, or valued (Ekman & Friesen's account of nonverbal display; S-160). Hold
these as **descriptive observations, not as "you are a slave to your group."** The critical point for
agency: noticing the pull of context is what lets a person choose a response instead of being carried
by it — a feeling can be real *and* partly situational at the same time. The **global** lens is
central here: invite learners to compare, without ranking, how different cultures and groups shape
emotional life — which feelings are welcomed in a funeral, a celebration, a classroom, a market. The
**egalitarian** lens: belonging is not equally available to everyone — some people are excluded from
groups by prejudice or circumstance, and that exclusion is itself a source of feeling that society,
not the individual, created. Be careful and kind: no learner should be asked to confess feelings of
exclusion; keep examples invented or optional. Never claim a learner's feeling is "not real" because
it is shaped by context — the lesson is that context *adds* to feeling, not that feeling is fake.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, name the four layers of a feeling. Today we ask where the
   layers come from.
2. **Worked example (10 min).** Listen as your facilitator tells the *same* event — a mistake made in
   public — in two settings: among trusted friends, and among strangers you want to impress. Name the
   different feelings in each. What changed: the event or the context?
3. **Sort and compare (15 min).** In pairs, sort the context cards: the same event across different
   settings and groups. For each, name the feeling and then say *what part the context is playing*.
4. **Mine and the situation's (15 min).** On your sheet, take one real feeling you had this week.
   Separate the arrows: what did *social context* add? What did *belonging* add? What is still
   genuinely yours underneath? You may keep this private.
5. **Discuss the pull (7 min).** With your pair: once you notice a feeling is partly situational, what
   choices open up that did not exist before?
6. **Close (3 min).** Feelings are real — and shaped by where you stand. Next: the screen in between,
   how media shapes feeling.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames ("The context added…; underneath, my own part was…").
- **Extension:** Compare two groups you belong to and how each would receive the same event, and
  explain the difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name how context and belonging shaped a feeling, and
  distinguish the situational part from the part that is their own?
- **Artifact:** The completed shapers-of-emotion sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: is there a feeling you only feel in certain places or with certain people? How
does the place change it? Share one example with the class next time.
