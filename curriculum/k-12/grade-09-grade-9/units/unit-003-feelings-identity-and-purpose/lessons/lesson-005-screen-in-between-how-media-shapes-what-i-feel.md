# Lesson 5 — The Screen in Between: How Media Shapes What I Feel

## Summary

Learners analyze how **media — feeds, ads, and headlines — shapes emotion**, focusing on **social
comparison**, the **curated highlight reel**, and **emotional contagion**. They learn that a feed is a
window, not a mirror, and design a small **digital boundary** of their own, holding media as neither
a villain nor a master.

## Objectives

- Analyze how media shapes what and how strongly a person feels about themselves and others.
  _(D01.S1.09.02)_
- Design one small digital boundary that protects your own emotional measure. _(D01.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

You scroll for five minutes and come away feeling, somehow, a little less — less interesting, less
accomplished, less happy — without quite knowing why. What you saw was not anyone's whole life; it
was a **highlight reel**: the best angle, the best moment, chosen and edited and posted. Comparing
your messy, full, unedited life to a stack of highlight reels is a match no one can win. That quiet
"less" you feel is real — and it is also, in part, made by the screen. Today you learn to see the
machine behind the feeling, so you can keep your own measure.

## Materials

- Media examples
- Screen-in-between sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the media examples and screen-in-between sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall how context and belonging shape feeling. Today we zoom in on one
  shaper — media.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It completes the second emotional-literacy goal
(D01.S1.09.02) by focusing on **media**. The core ideas: **(1) curation** — feeds are chosen,
edited, and filtered, so they show a highlight reel, not a full life; **(2) social comparison** —
people naturally compare themselves to others, and curated content skews that comparison upward
(Festinger, S-221; Common Sense Media's research on media and adolescent development, S-226); **(3)
emotional contagion** — the feelings shown in media can spread to the viewer; **(4) the balanced
picture** — digital media can also connect, teach, and lift, and adolescence is a time when digital
habits are being formed (WHO adolescent mental-health framing, S-311). The stance is **neither
demonizing nor worshipping media** (philosophy §4, technology lens): the lesson's goal is *noticing
the pull and choosing*, not quitting screens. The **egalitarian** lens: the same feed is not equally
harmful to everyone — those already excluded or under pressure can feel the comparison more sharply,
and the platforms' incentives (keep you scrolling) fall unevenly. The **social-emotional** lens: a
boundary is self-care, not weakness. Keep it concrete and agency-filled: the lesson ends with a
*doable* boundary, never despair. Be sensitive: do not pressure learners to disclose their own
screen habits or feelings; make all sharing optional and invented examples welcome. Evidence is
distinguished from values: that feeds are curated is a fact; that one *should* limit them is a value
the learner weighs for themselves (philosophy §5).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4, name one thing that shapes feeling besides the event itself.
   Today: the screen.
2. **Worked example (10 min).** Look at one media example together. Name what is *shown*, what is
   *left out*, and what feeling each is designed to stir. Where does comparison sneak in?
3. **Analyze in pairs (15 min).** Take two more media examples. For each: what is curated? What is
   missing? What feeling does it aim to create — and how? Use the words *comparison*, *highlight
   reel*, *contagion*.
4. **Two panels (10 min).** On your sheet, draw or write the highlight reel on one side and a fuller,
   realer life on the other. What does the screen leave out?
5. **Design a boundary (10 min).** Write one small, doable digital boundary for yourself — a time
   limit, a muted notification, a feed you follow or unfollow, a moment you put the phone away. Make
   it specific enough to actually do.
6. **Close (5 min).** A feed is a window, not a mirror. Next: we turn to the story of who you are.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames ("The feed shows…; it leaves out…; it makes me feel…").
- **Extension:** Trace one feeling to its exact source in a feed and explain the mechanism — was it
  comparison, contagion, or curation?

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name how a media example shapes feeling, and write one
  specific, doable digital boundary?
- **Artifact:** The completed screen-in-between sheet (with the boundary), kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

With someone at home, compare one image you both saw on a screen with the fuller, realer version of
the same thing. What did the screen leave out?
