# Lesson 10 — Speaking Up, Standing With: Self-Advocacy and the Unit's Gathering

## Summary

Learners practice **self-advocacy** — stating their own needs and boundaries directly and
respectfully, using an **I-statement** frame and distinguishing it from passive and aggressive
styles. Then they gather the unit's **six goals into one whole self**, revisiting the Lesson 1 map
and their portfolio artifacts in a closing reflection.

## Objectives

- Advocate for your own needs and boundaries respectfully while considering others. _(D01.S3.09.02)_
- Gather the unit's six goals — naming subtle and secondary emotions, seeing context and media's
  shaping, telling a coherent story, seeing society and self shaping each other, setting a goal with
  milestones, and advocating for yourself — into one whole self. _(D01.S1.09.01, D01.S1.09.02,
  D01.S2.09.01, D01.S2.09.02, D01.S3.09.01, D01.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

There is a difference between being quiet when you are hurting, shouting to win, and *speaking up
for yourself while still seeing the other person*. The first two are easy and costly; the third is a
skill. Saying "I feel worried when plans change at the last minute, because I prepare carefully —
what I need is a heads-up sooner" names your need without attacking anyone. Self-advocacy is not
winning; it is being heard while staying fair. And it is the last piece of this unit: together with
naming your feelings, seeing what shapes them, telling your story, seeing how you and society make
each other, and setting a goal, it is how you become the author of your own life.

## Materials

- Advocacy scenario cards
- I-statement frame sheet
- Unit map sheet (from Lesson 1)

## Preparation

- Copy the advocacy scenario cards and I-statement frame sheet; have learners' Lesson 1 unit maps and
  their portfolio artifacts on hand.
- Retrieval: this whole lesson is a retrieval, synthesis, and application pass — the payoff of the
  unit (S-039).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the **final skill lesson and the unit's
capstone**. The skill — **self-advocacy** — is taught with a **worked example and guided practice**
(S-011). The core frame is **assertiveness**: expressing one's own needs and boundaries directly and
respectfully, distinct from **passive** (suppressing one's needs) and **aggressive** (trampling
others') styles (Alberti & Emmons, S-405). The **I-statement** pattern — "I feel… when… because…
what I need is…" — carries it (related to Rosenberg's nonviolent communication, S-148). The
**ethics** lens: advocacy that honors the other person is a moral skill, not just a social one. The
**egalitarian** lens: some people's needs are heard more readily than others' — because of status,
gender, disability, or prejudice — so teaching every learner to advocate is itself an act of
leveling; and advocacy can be collective, not only individual. The **global** lens: cultures differ
in how directly a need is stated; the lesson offers the I-statement as *one* respectful form, not
the only right one — a learner whose culture values indirectness should be honored, not "corrected."
**Safety:** role-plays are low-stakes and invented; no learner is made to disclose a real need. The
closing **gathering** retrieves all six goals (S-039) and celebrates mastery as collective, never a
ranking (S-007): a learner who has not yet got a goal simply revisits it — "not yet" is information.
Close with warmth: the unit has been inward work, and it lands in care and agency, not pressure.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Name the unit's six goals from memory. (Recalling is the learning.)
2. **Worked example (10 min).** Watch as your facilitator models the three styles for one scenario —
   passive, aggressive, assertive — and lands on the I-statement: "I feel… when… because… what I need
   is…". Notice how the assertive one honors both people.
3. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, take the advocacy scenario cards. For each, first say the
   need passively and aggressively (to feel the difference), then craft and say the assertive
   I-statement. Give each other one specific piece of feedback.
4. **Apply it to you (10 min).** Write one I-statement for a real (or invented) need or boundary of
   your own. Say it aloud to your pair if you wish.
5. **Gather the six (15 min).** Return to your Lesson 1 unit map and your portfolio artifacts. For
   each of the six goals, write one sentence of what you now know or can do. Connect them: how do
   feelings, identity, and purpose make one whole self?
6. **Close (5 min).** You began this unit wondering who you are. You end it as the author — still in
   the making, and that is exactly right.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice a single simple request with the I-statement frame before moving to full
  boundaries.
- **Extension:** Write an advocacy plan for a need bigger than your own — a change your community
  needs — and name whom to address and how.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** The six-goal gathering on the unit map — one sentence per goal, linked
  into one whole self — kept in the portfolio alongside the unit's artifacts.
- **Self/peer:** Learners self-check all six goals are present and give one specific "I noticed you
  honored both people when…" to a partner.
- **Mastery, not ranking:** A learner missing a goal revisits its artifact and completes it; the map
  is finished when it is finished.

## Home connection

Show someone at home your completed unit map and teach them one goal end to end — including the
I-statement. Teaching it is the surest sign it is yours.
