# Lesson 5 — Capstone: A Memorial You Can Defend

## Summary

The unit ends where public argument actually happens: on one page. Learners choose one
documented case from the unit and write a proposal for what should be done — keep,
change, remove, or add — then defend it with evidence, all three frameworks, the best
objection to their own view, and a condition under which they would change their mind.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences,
  duties, virtues) and compare what each shows. *(D09.S1.09.01)*
- Explain historical perspective — how people in the past saw their world differently —
  using one specific example. *(D08.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

Every group you belong to keeps some things and lets others go: a team's old photos, a
school's trophy case, the stories a family tells at dinner. Choosing is unavoidable.
Today you make one of those choices in public, in writing, with reasons another person
can check — which is the difference between having an opinion and holding a position.

## Materials

- One-page proposal sheet (six boxes)
- Model paragraph pair (weak and strong versions of the same paragraph)
- Peer-check card (four checks)

## Preparation

- Copy the proposal sheet, the model paragraphs, and the peer-check card.
- Choose the case you will model with, in advance. The amnesty question from Lesson 4
  works well because the evidence is short and the tension is real.
- Write the four checks where the room can see them.
- Retrieval: lessons 1–4. Do not re-teach. Ask for the lenses and the perspective
  questions from memory, and let a shaky answer be corrected by a peer.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Working-session calm: the room should sound like drafting, not debating.

**The facts to land.**

1. **A proposal answers five things:** what should be done, where, in whose words, at
   whose cost, and who was consulted.
2. **Every factual claim is cited or marked as a guess** (S-263, S-316).
3. **All three frameworks appear, each at its strongest:** what happens, what is owed,
   who we become (S-456, S-457, S-458).
4. **A reasoned position names its best objection** and says what would change the
   writer's mind.

**Don't overshoot.** A proposal is not a verdict on history but a decision about a
public act; it can be good without being agreeable. No proposal may rank one people's
suffering against another's.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Ethics:* reasons carry the proposal; feeling may be why you began.
- *Egalitarianism:* ask who was not in the room when you imagined it.
- *Global:* what fits one country's law may not travel; say what is local.
- *Critical-thinking:* your proposal is only as strong as the objection you state fairly.

**The skill.** One page: claim, evidence, three frameworks, objection, condition.

**Access.** Offer the six boxes with sentence starters; accept a spoken proposal or a
drawn plan with captions.

**Emotional safety.** Proposals address public memory, never a learner's family. Reading
aloud is optional.

**Safety floor.** If a proposal turns toward a living person or group in the room,
redirect it to the public case and follow your safeguarding path.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (6 min).** From memory, no notes: the three lenses; the four perspective
   questions; what a gap in the record does and does not prove; the excuse test. Correct
   each other before you ask me.
2. **The task (5 min).** Choose one case from this unit: the revised account of Great
   Zimbabwe, the paperwork of the slave trade, the Berlin Conference borders, or the
   amnesty trade. Not your own family, and not a case you have only heard about this
   morning. Write a one-page proposal for one public act of memory about it.
3. **Worked example (12 min).** Read both model paragraphs about the amnesty question.
   The weak one says remembering matters and lists feelings. The strong one names the
   decision, cites what is documented (S-392), runs each framework in a sentence, states
   the best objection plainly, and ends with a condition: "I would change my mind if
   ___." Watch what the second one does that the first does not (S-011).
4. **Draft (20 min).** Fill the six boxes:
   1. **The question** — in one sentence, as a choice.
   2. **What we know** — two or three facts, each cited or marked as uncertain.
   3. **What I propose** — keep, change, remove, or add; where; in whose words. If you
      show harm, show only as much as your point requires (S-495).
   4. **What each framework shows** — one honest sentence each, including the one you
      disagree with.
   5. **The best objection** — the strongest case against you, in your opponent's voice.
   6. **What would change my mind** — a condition someone could actually test.
5. **Peer check (11 min).** Swap. Run the four checks and write only on those: is every
   claim sourced or flagged? are there three frameworks, each at its strongest? is there
   a real objection? is the mind-change condition testable? Give back the page, not a
   verdict.
6. **Close (6 min).** Revise one sentence — the one the peer check hit hardest. Then, if
   you choose, read your framework row aloud. Hand in the page with your name and today's
   date. This page is your unit assessment; "not yet" means revise and resubmit, not
   fail.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the six boxes with starters ("The choice is whether…"; "This matters
  because…"), and write only one framework sentence, chosen with the facilitator.
- **Extension:** Write the objection so well that a reader cannot tell which side you are
  on until the last line — then say what that exercise taught you about your own reasons.

## Assessment

- **Summative (performance/portfolio):** the one-page proposal. Criteria: a decision
  rather than a theme; evidence labelled; three frameworks compared, not listed; a
  genuine objection; a testable mind-change condition; historical perspective present in
  at least one sentence.
- **Peer:** the four checks, recorded on the peer-check card.
- **Mastery framing:** a page that misses a criterion is revised against that criterion
  and resubmitted. The mark is the revision, not the first draft.

## Home connection

Give your proposal to one adult and ask them for the strongest objection they can make.
Write their objection down word for word, then answer it in one paragraph. That is what
public argument looks like when it is done honestly.
