# Lesson 3 — Seeing the World as They Saw It: Historical Perspective

## Summary

Understanding people in the past is a skill with steps, and it is not the same as
agreeing with them. Learners reconstruct how the people who drew Africa's colonial
borders saw their own world, notice that no era has only one view, and then apply a
three-part test that keeps understanding from sliding into excuse.

## Objectives

- Explain historical perspective — how people in the past saw their world differently —
  using one specific example. *(D08.S1.09.02)*
- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences,
  duties, virtues) and compare what each shows. *(D09.S1.09.01)*

## Connection

You have changed your mind about something you once thought was obviously true — a
food you hated, a rule you thought was fair, a person you had wrong. You can still
explain why you thought that, and explaining it does not mean you were right. That is
the move this lesson practices, at the scale of a whole society: first see the world
as they saw it, then judge what they did.

## Materials

- Perspective card (four questions and the three-test check)
- Two short case briefs (Berlin Conference 1884–85; abolition)
- Paper and pen

## Preparation

- Copy the perspective card and the two briefs. Keep both briefs to a few sentences:
  dates, decisions, no images of harm (S-495).
- Be ready to model the four questions aloud on the Berlin case before learners touch
  the second case. The modelling is the lesson; the second case is the practice.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, the five sourcing questions; from Grade 8, the difference
  between explaining a choice and justifying it.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Deliberate and unshocked: a method, not a trial.

**The facts to land.**

1. **Historical perspective reconstructs assumptions:** what people took for granted,
   feared losing, named, and could not see (S-477).
2. **An era never has only one view.** Abolitionists argued against slavery inside a
   world whose law treated it as normal, and their movement changed the law across much
   of the Atlantic world (S-479).
3. **Understanding is not excusing.** Explanation answers "how could they"; judgement
   answers "was it right". Both are required, in that order.

**Don't overshoot.** Never say "everyone thought that then" — it is false, and it
erases the people who resisted (S-384). Nor does understanding a perpetrator betray
victims: precision about how harm was possible is what makes judgement stick.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Ethics:* judging well requires knowing what was chosen and what was possible.
- *Egalitarianism:* whose view counts as "the" view of an era, and who is left out.
- *Global:* every society has blind spots, including ours; the method does not rank
  failures.
- *Critical-thinking:* an explanation that would excuse anything explains nothing.

**The skill.** Four perspective questions, then the three-test check.

**Access.** Give the four questions as a card; read both briefs aloud. A learner who
cannot write may answer aloud.

**Emotional safety.** Nobody is asked to imagine being a victim or a perpetrator; the
work stays on the page.

**Safety floor.** If a learner raises harm in their own family, do not probe in the
room; follow your safeguarding path.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From memory: the five sourcing questions from Lesson 2, and one
   silence you found. Today we turn from the record to the people who made it.
2. **Worked example: Berlin, 1884–85 (15 min).** In 1884–85, European powers met in
   Berlin and drew colonial borders in Africa. No Africans were in the room (S-477).
   Watch me think this through with four questions, out loud:
   1. **What did they take for granted?** That another continent's map was theirs to
      draw.
   2. **What did they fear losing?** Ask it, then find the evidence in the brief before
      you answer.
   3. **What words did they use?** The meeting is remembered as the "partition" of
      Africa: a word about land, not about peoples.
   4. **What could they not see?** The nations, agreements, and authorities already
      living on that land.
   Answering these does not make the borders right. It makes them intelligible.
3. **Name the method (5 min).** Those four questions are the skill. Copy them. Notice
   that all four ask about the *world they lived in*, not about whether they were good
   people.
4. **Same world, different sight (12 min).** No era has one view. Slavery was legal
   across much of the Atlantic world, and the transatlantic trade forced about 12.5
   million people aboard ships (S-322). Inside that same world, abolitionists argued it
   was wrong, and over the 1800s abolition became law across much of the Atlantic world
   (S-479). In Saint-Domingue the uprising that began in 1791 ended slavery and led to
   Haitian independence in 1804 (S-384). Run the four questions on the abolitionists
   too: what did they take for granted, what did they fear losing, what did they call
   the thing, what could they not see?
5. **The excuse test (12 min).** Three checks, in writing:
   1. Does the explanation remove the harm? (No — the harm is in the record.)
   2. Does any framework still judge it? Ask the **duties** lens: what did the delegates
      owe the people whose land they were dividing, regardless of what they believed
      (S-457)?
   3. Would the same explanation excuse anything at all? If it would, it is not
      understanding; it is an excuse.
6. **Close (6 min).** Write one sentence with two halves: "I understand ___ better, and
   I still judge ___ because ___." That sentence is the whole standard, and it is what
   you will need in the capstone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Two questions only, one case only, and a sentence frame that starts the
  judgement for them: "They could not see ___, but they still ___."
- **Extension:** Show that the same method can be turned on the present: name one thing
  your own society takes for granted that a historian in a century may find hard to
  explain, and say what evidence would show it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner answer the four perspective questions
  about one case with evidence rather than invention?
- **Performance:** The two-part closing sentence — understanding *and* judgement, with a
  reason — is the evidence that the standard has been met.

## Home connection

Ask an older person: what is one thing that was normal when they were young and is not
normal now? Listen for what people took for granted, and notice how they explain it.
