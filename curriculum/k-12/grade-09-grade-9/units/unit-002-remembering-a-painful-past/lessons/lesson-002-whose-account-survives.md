# Lesson 2 — Whose Account Survives? Archives, Silence & Power

## Summary

Every account of the past passed through hands. Someone wrote it down, someone kept
the paper, and someone later decided it was worth storing. This lesson teaches
learners to see that chain: the five questions to ask of any record, the difference
between "not written down" and "did not happen", and the way power decides not only
who wins an argument but which questions are asked at all.

## Objectives

- Explain historical perspective — how people in the past saw their world differently —
  using one specific example. *(D08.S1.09.02)*
- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences,
  duties, virtues) and compare what each shows. *(D09.S1.09.01)*

## Connection

A family photograph album has a gap where certain years should be. The album is not a
lie — it is a record of what someone chose to put in it, and of who owned a camera.
Nations keep albums too: tax registers, ships' logs, school lists, museum shelves. The
same question follows you from the kitchen table to the national archive: who held the
camera, and who was never in the room?

## Materials

- Archive card set (four short extracts)
- Five sourcing questions card
- Paper and pen

## Preparation

- Copy the four extracts and the five questions. Keep every extract to one or two
  sentences; use numbers where a number is needed, never images of harm
  (S-495).
- Choose extracts that differ in kind, so learners see that a written record and a
  spoken record are both records: a heritage description of Great Zimbabwe, a summary
  of one slave voyage, a line about griots, and one museum listing.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the three lenses; from Grade 6, the difference between a
  primary source made at the time and a secondary source made later (S-263).

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Steady and curious — detective work, not accusation.

**The facts to land.**

1. **An archive is not the past:** it is what someone wrote down and someone else
   kept (S-263).
2. **Five questions open any record:** who made it, when, for whom, why, and what it
   leaves out (S-316).
3. **A gap is not proof.** "Not written down" and "did not happen" are different
   claims.
4. **Power shapes the questions,** not only the answers — including which subjects are
   treated as worth recording (S-275).

**Don't overshoot.** The archive is not a conspiracy. Most records were made for
ordinary reasons — cargo, tax, land — and remain evidence. The skill is asking, not
suspecting.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Ethics:* a record that erases people does harm that outlives the event.
- *Egalitarianism:* whose words are archived and whose are only spoken decides who
  counts.
- *Global:* written archives and oral records are different technologies of memory,
  not a ranked pair (S-446).
- *Critical-thinking:* sourcing is a method with named questions, not a mood of doubt.

**The skill.** Five questions on one document, then one silence named and left unfilled.

**Access.** Read the extracts aloud and hand out the question card. A learner without
the shared language can still sort the cards by "written" and "spoken".

**Emotional safety.** Numbers, never images. Nobody is asked about their own family.

**Safety floor.** If a learner volunteers something personal, thank them, do not
probe, and follow your setting's safeguarding path.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From memory: name the three lenses from Lesson 1 and one thing
   a society does to remember. Today we look underneath all of them, at the record
   itself.
2. **An account that was revised (12 min).** Read the Great Zimbabwe extract. The stone
   city was built between about 1100 and 1450, with walls of dry stone and no mortar,
   and UNESCO describes it as a unique testimony to the Bantu civilization of the
   Shona between the 11th and 15th centuries (S-199). An older popular explanation credited the walls to a foreign
   queen — the Queen of Sheba — and that explanation is now discredited (S-199). Ask
   three things: why was the outsider's story easier to believe? Who is flattered by
   it? What had to be true about African people for that story to feel right? Nobody
   has to answer out loud. That is historical perspective: not what happened, but how
   the world looked to the people who found this account convincing.
3. **Five questions (8 min).** Take any record and ask: **who made it, when, for whom,
   why, and what does it leave out?** (S-316). This is the whole method. A record made
   for a tax office answers different questions than a letter written to a friend.
4. **The narrowing record (12 min).** Draw four bands, each narrower than the one
   above: **what happened → what was written down → what was kept → what reaches you.**
   Apply it to the transatlantic slave trade. Surviving voyage records are detailed
   enough that historians can reconstruct the crossings and count the people forced
   aboard: about 12.5 million embarked and about 10.7 million arrived (S-322). Now ask: what can this record tell you with confidence? And what does it
   never name?
5. **Another kind of record (10 min).** In West Africa, griots are oral
   troubadour-historians who carry genealogies and history by word (S-446). The
   *Sundiata* epic was recorded in writing from the griot Mamadou Kouyaté, which is how
   a spoken record entered the written one (S-269). UNESCO's Memory of the World
   programme preserves documentary heritage, including the Timbuktu manuscripts
   inscribed in 2017 (S-201). A record kept by word is still a record — and losing it is
   a loss, not a gap waiting to be filled.
6. **Close (8 min).** Write two lines. One: "The record I would go looking for is ___
   because ___." Two: one thing about this event that no archive can give back. Keep
   both; the capstone will use them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use three questions only — who made it, who is missing, what was it for
  — and one extract, read aloud.
- **Extension:** Take two records of the same event and argue which a historian should
  trust more, naming the evidence and the value behind the judgement.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner ask the five sourcing questions of a
  record and state one silence *without* asserting what was in it?
- **Self-assessment:** The learner's two closing lines — a record worth seeking and
  something unrecoverable — show whether the distinction between evidence and absence
  has landed.

## Home connection

Find one record in your home — a receipt, a certificate, a photo, a message — and ask
it the five questions. Then ask who in your household has no record at all, and why
that might be.

- On the *Sundiata* epic recorded from the griot Mamadou Kouyaté: Niane (1965),
  *Sundiata: An Epic of Old Mali* (S-269).
- On preserving documentary heritage, including the Timbuktu manuscripts: UNESCO,
  Memory of the World (S-201).
- On power as the capacity to shape what is asked and what is possible: Lukes (1974),
  *Power: A Radical View* (S-275).
- On teaching painful history without graphic material: USHMM, "Guidelines for Teaching
  about the Holocaust" (S-495).
