# Lesson 1 — How Should a Society Remember a Painful Past?

## Summary

Every society has a past that includes harm — harm it suffered and harm it caused.
This lesson does not tell learners what to conclude about that; it gives them tools to
think about it honestly: three ethical frameworks, and the habit of historical
perspective. The goal is not a "correct" answer but the ability to hold a hard question
without collapsing it.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences,
  duties, virtues) and compare what each shows. *(D09.S1.09.01)*
- Explain historical perspective — how people in the past saw their world differently —
  using one specific example. *(D08.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

What a society chooses to remember — and how it chooses to remember — shapes who that
society becomes. Whether a town keeps a statue, whether a nation apologizes, whether a
museum names what happened: these are decisions made by real people, often in
disagreement. You will inherit these decisions, and you will have to make some
yourself. This lesson is practice for that.

## Materials

- Short, non-graphic descriptions of three real cases of historical memory (from
  different world regions)
- Paper and pen

## Preparation

- Choose three cases of a society reckoning with its past — for example, a truth and
  reconciliation commission, a public memorial, a national apology, or a program of
  reparations — from different regions. Keep each description short and non-graphic.

## Facilitator note

This is a hard topic; hold it with care (`docs/facilitation.md`). Name feelings before
facts, and let learners step away if they need to. The lesson's stance is **not
relativism** — some ways of remembering are more honest or more just than others — but
it is also **not a single answer**. Do not impose a conclusion. The discipline is to
give every serious view its strongest version before arguing against it, and to
separate what is *known* (evidence) from what is *chosen* (values). Where a learner has
a personal connection to a case, let them share only if they choose.

## Procedure

1. **Meet the three cases (10 min).** Read or hear each case. For each, note two
   things: what is being remembered, and how the society has chosen to remember it.
2. **Three lenses (15 min).** For one case, ask it in three ways — write one sentence
   for each:
   - **Consequences:** what happens if we remember this way? if we forget?
   - **Duties:** what do we owe the people harmed, and the people who came after?
   - **Virtues:** what kind of person or society does this choice make us — honest,
     just, compassionate?
3. **Historical perspective (10 min).** Before judging, try to understand: how did
   people at the time see their world? What did they believe, fear, and value?
   Understanding is not excusing — name the difference in your own words.
4. **Hold two views (15 min).** Write the strongest argument you can for remembering
   *one* way, and the strongest argument for a *different* way. Which tension can't be
   resolved easily, and why?
5. **Close (5 min).** A mature mind can hold more than one true thing at once, and can
   still choose. What is one question you are leaving with, rather than one answer?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer sentence frames for each lens; work with one case only.
- **Extension:** Compare two cases and argue which society's approach was more just,
  using evidence and naming the values your judgment rests on.

## Assessment

- **Formative:** Can the learner apply at least two frameworks to a case and name a
  genuine tension?
- **Performance/self:** The written reflection — strongest view for each side — plus a
  one-line note on what they are still uncertain about.

Ask an elder in your family: what is one thing from our community's past that people
remember, and how do they remember it? Listen without arguing, then reflect in
writing.
