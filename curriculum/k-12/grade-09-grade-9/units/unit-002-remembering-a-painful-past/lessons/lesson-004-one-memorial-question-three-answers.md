# Lesson 4 — One Memorial Question, Three Answers

## Summary

A framework is only worth what it shows you. Learners take one documented public
question — was South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission right to trade
prosecution for truth? — and work it three times: as consequences, as duties, as
virtues. Then they compare, and find out that each lens shows something the other two
miss. The same three questions then go to a listed place, a school curriculum, and a
legal duty.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences,
  duties, virtues) and compare what each shows. *(D09.S1.09.01)*
- Explain historical perspective — how people in the past saw their world differently —
  using one specific example. *(D08.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

Three people watch the same thing happen — a friend is lied about in a group chat. One
asks what the lie will do to everyone involved. One asks what the liar owed their
friend. One asks what kind of person the liar is becoming. They are looking at one
event and seeing three different things, and all three are true. Public memory works
the same way, at the scale of a country.

## Materials

- Case brief — the amnesty trade
- Three-framework comparison sheet
- Three public-memory cards (a listed place, a school curriculum, a public apology)

## Preparation

- Copy the case brief, the comparison sheet, and the three cards. Keep the case to one
  page, non-graphic, numbers rather than images (S-495).
- Write the three framework questions where the room can see them for the whole hour:
  *what happens, to whom?* / *what is owed, and by whom?* / *who do we become?*
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the three lenses; from Lesson 3, the excuse test. Do not
  re-teach the lenses; ask learners to state them in one line each.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Even-handed and concrete: a comparison, not a verdict.

**The facts to land.**

1. **South Africa's TRC (1995), chaired by Desmond Tutu, gathered evidence rather than
   prosecuting:** more than 22,000 victim statements, 1,500 amnesties for full
   disclosure, and recommendations for reparations (S-392).
2. **Apartheid was legal racial segregation, ended in 1994** (S-480).
3. **Each lens asks a different question:** what happens (S-456), what is owed (S-457),
   who we become (S-458).
4. **Contested memory takes public forms:** a listed place (S-472), a curriculum
   (S-495), an apology with duties behind it (S-491).

**Don't overshoot.** The TRC is neither a failure nor a template. It is one documented
attempt with real strengths and limits; do not settle it in an hour.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Ethics:* three frameworks, three true things; the skill is comparison, not a winner.
- *Egalitarianism:* whose account a marker carries, and who was never asked, is Lesson
  2 in stone.
- *Global:* truth commissions, museum curricula, and treaty duties are different
  instruments; none is the default (S-463).
- *Critical-thinking:* a framework that shows nothing new has been named, not used.

**The skill.** Three frameworks over one question, then what each showed that the
others missed.

**Access.** Leave the three questions on the board and offer the sheet as a table; one
card is enough.

**Emotional safety.** Statistics, not images. Nobody is asked what they would have done
as a victim or an amnestied perpetrator.

**Safety floor.** If a learner connects this to their own life, do not ask for detail;
follow your safeguarding path.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In one line each, from memory: what does the consequences lens
   ask? the duties lens? the virtues lens? Then the excuse test from Lesson 3. Today we
   give all three the same question and see what happens.
2. **The case (8 min).** Read the brief. Apartheid ended in 1994 (S-480). The new
   government set up a Truth and Reconciliation Commission in 1995, chaired by
   Archbishop Desmond Tutu. It was built to gather evidence rather than to prosecute. It
   took more than 22,000 statements from victims, held public hearings, granted 1,500
   amnesties where a perpetrator fully disclosed what they had done, and recommended
   reparations (S-392). **The question: was it right to trade prosecution for truth?**
   You do not have to like either answer.
3. **Round one — consequences (5 min).** What did this produce, for whom? Truth on the
   record; victims heard in public; some accountability given up; what might follow
   (S-456). Be specific about *who* gains and *who* pays.
4. **Round two — duties (5 min).** What is owed to the people harmed, to their
   families, and to the rule of law itself? Does trading punishment for truth treat
   anyone as a means only (S-457)?
5. **Round three — virtues (5 min).** What kind of society does this choice make —
   honest, merciful, evasive, brave? What habit does it build in the institutions that
   come after (S-458)?
6. **Compare (7 min).** Fill the last row of your sheet: for this question, what does
   each lens *show*, and what does each *miss*? Name the one tension no framework
   resolves. Then one perspective question: in 1995, what did the commissioners believe
   a courtroom could not deliver? Understanding their answer is not agreeing with it.
7. **Transfer: three forms of contested memory (15 min).** In groups, take one card and
   run the same three questions.
   - **A listed place.** UNESCO lists Cahokia Mounds — the largest pre-Columbian
     settlement north of Mexico, built around about 120 earth mounds (S-472). Listing is
     a public claim that a place matters.
   - **A school curriculum.** A memorial museum publishes guidance on how a genocide
     should be taught: define terms, avoid simple answers, translate statistics into
     people, use graphic material only as far as the objective requires (S-495).
   - **A public apology.** A state can say sorry. What makes that more than words:
     money, a law, a changed practice? The Universal Declaration's first article holds
     that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights (S-149); the
     1948 Genocide Convention obliges states to prevent and punish genocide (S-491);
     SDG 16 names peace, justice, and strong institutions as a shared commitment
     (S-463).
8. **Close (5 min).** Write one sentence: which framework did most of your thinking, and
   what did the other two show that it missed? Keep it for the capstone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** One framework, one card, and a sentence frame: "The consequences lens
  shows ___ but misses ___."
- **Extension:** Argue which single framework a government should be required to use
  when it decides what to build or teach — and name what would go wrong if it did.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner apply a framework to the case with
  evidence, rather than naming the framework and stopping?
- **Performance:** The comparison row and the closing sentence — what each lens shows
  and misses — are the unit's core skill in one page.

## Home connection

Find a marker near you: a plaque, a statue, a street name, a museum label. Read its
wording closely. Ask what it tells you to feel, and what it does not mention.
