# Lesson 1 — Accurate Information & a Dignity-First Space

## Summary

Learners establish a **dignity-first, no-shame space** for the unit and learn the core skill
underneath all of it: telling **accurate information** from **misinformation** about the body and
health. They build shared ground rules and a three-question source-check they will use all unit.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health — anatomy, consent, and how to access
  accurate information and care. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last health question you were too shy — or too unsure — to ask out loud. Maybe it came
up in a chat, a video, or a joke you did not quite get. Everyone has questions about their body;
the problem is not the question. The problem is that so many of the answers floating around are
wrong, and wrong answers about your body can cost you your health and your peace. This unit begins
with the skill that unlocks everything else: **telling accurate information from misinformation**,
in a room where no question is shameful and every body is treated with dignity.

## Materials

- Class ground-rules chart
- Accurate-information checklist

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the source-check questions.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the difference between a claim and the evidence behind it, and
  the substance-awareness lesson's idea that accurate information supports safer choices.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **sexual and reproductive
health is a matter of accurate information, consent, and access to care — taught with dignity and
never shame — and the first skill is telling accurate information from misinformation.** This is a
norms-and-skills lesson, so use explicit instruction for the skill (the three-question source-check,
modeled as a worked example) and inquiry for the values (S-011). The ethics lens: how we speak about
bodies sets the temperature of the room — we speak with dignity, we do not mock, we do not shame. The
egalitarian lens is central and non-negotiable: **no body and no question is "better" or "worse";
every learner's body, family, culture, and pace is honored, and no one is ever pressured to share.**
The global lens: what counts as a trusted source of health information differs around the world —
a community health worker in one place, a clinic in another, an official health ministry or WHO
page in another — so "accurate" means "from a source that is accountable and checkable," not "from
the nearest screen." The technology lens: the internet carries both the best and the worst health
information ever made, so the skill is to *check*, not to trust blindly (UNESCO CSE, S-412; WHO,
S-261). Model the three questions on one concrete claim (worked example below), then let learners
practice. Establish the safety net now: **sharing is always optional; a learner may always pass;
and if a learner discloses something that suggests harm, you follow your setting's safeguarding
path — believe, do not promise secrecy, and get help** (WHO violence-prevention guidance, S-051).
Keep claims accurate and cite sources; distinguish evidence from values throughout.

## Procedure

1. **Open and ground rules (10 min).** This unit is about your body, your health, and your safety,
   and it is taught with dignity, never shame. Together, build the room's norms: **we use accurate
   words; we do not mock anyone's body or question; sharing is a choice, never a demand; and a
   question is never "stupid."** Write or say them; they are the floor we stand on all unit.
2. **Why accurate information matters (8 min).** Wrong answers about the body are everywhere — in
   rumors, jokes, and endless social posts. A wrong answer can lead a person to take a real risk or
   to miss real care. **Accurate information** is information that is true and checkable;
   **misinformation** is false or misleading information spread whether or not the sharer means harm.
3. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** _Worked example:_ claim — "You cannot get an infection if
   you are young and healthy." Ask: **(1) Who says it?** A random account or a health worker?
   **(2) Can I check it somewhere else?** Does an official source — a health ministry, WHO, a clinic —
   say the same? **(3) Does it respect me?** Or does it shame or frighten me into silence? For this
   claim: no one responsible says it, it fails every check, and it is false — infections do not care
   how young you are. That is the skill.
4. **Practice (12 min).** In pairs, take one health claim you have actually heard (or one from the
   facilitator's list) and run it through the three questions. Write or say your verdict: accurate,
   misleading, or "I need to check."
5. **Close (10 min).** Write down the three questions and one **trusted adult** you could ask a
   health question to without shame. You have just built the tool this whole unit runs on.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the two-question shortcut: "Who says it, and can a trusted adult check it?"
- **Extension:** Add a fourth question — "Who benefits if I believe this?" — and apply it to one
  claim that was designed to sell or scare.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner apply the three questions to a claim and say
  whether it is accurate, misleading, or needs checking?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The learner's written source-check questions and their named trusted
  adult, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Bring the three questions home: the next time a health claim crosses your screen or your
conversation, run it through the check and tell one person what you found.
