# Lesson 6 — Accessing Accurate Information & Care

## Summary

Learners explain **where and how to access accurate information and care** for sexual and
reproductive health — trusted adults, health workers, clinics, and official services — and the
**rights** a young person holds when they seek it: to accurate information, to privacy and
confidentiality (with safety limits), and to make their own choices with consent.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health, including how to access accurate information
  and care. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Knowing the facts is only half the skill; the other half is knowing **where to go when you need more
than a lesson can give** — a real question answered by a real person, a test, a method, a vaccine, a
check-up. In every community on Earth there are people whose job is exactly this: health workers,
clinics, counselors. And in most places a young person who seeks care has **rights** — to accurate
information, to privacy, and to decide what happens to their own body. Today you learn the pathway
from "I have a question" to "I got the care I needed," and the rights that protect you along the
way.

## Materials

- Care-pathway map
- Rights card

## Preparation

- Copy the pathway map and rights card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the three source-check questions; from Lesson 4, recall consent
  and autonomy.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **access to accurate information
and care is a right, and the pathway is knowable — a trusted adult, a health worker, or a clinic can
answer questions, provide methods, testing, vaccines, and referrals, and a young person's care is
generally confidential, with important exceptions where safety is at risk.** This is a
knowledge-and-skills lesson, so use explicit instruction (the worked-example pathway) and role-play
for asking (S-011). Teach the steps plainly: **(1) start with a trusted adult or a health worker you
can ask without shame; (2) find a service — a clinic, a school counselor, a youth-friendly service, a
pharmacist, a hotline — depending on what is available where you live; (3) get accurate information;
(4) decide for yourself, with consent, what you want; (5) follow up.** Teach the rights: to accurate
information, to privacy and confidentiality, to be treated without judgment, and to give or withhold
consent over your own body — grounded in the right to health and to decide freely over one's own body
(UNESCO CSE, S-412; WHO, S-261). Be honest about confidentiality: in most settings a young person's
health matters are private, **but privacy has limits when a person's safety is at risk — then a
trusted adult or service must act to protect them.** The ethics lens: seeking care and honoring a person's rights — to accurate information, to privacy, and to decide over their own body — is how we uphold their dignity and autonomy; access to care is a moral matter, not merely a practical one. The egalitarian lens: **access is not equal —
cost, distance, stigma, and law differ wildly around the world, and the same care that is easy for
one person is out of reach for another; that is an injustice to name, not a reason anyone is less
deserving.** The global lens: services take many forms — a community health worker under a tree, a
mobile clinic, a hospital, a phone hotline — and all are real parts of the pathway. The technology
lens: official websites and hotlines can reach people far from any clinic, but they must be checked
like any source. Keep it practical and hopeful; a learner may always pass; and if a learner names a
specific unmet need or risk, connect them to real local help rather than leaving it abstract.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what are the three questions to ask of any health claim? Today
   we learn where to go when a claim needs a *person*, not just a check.
2. **Meet the pathway (15 min).** Worked example, guided on the map: **(1)** a trusted adult or
   health worker; **(2)** a service — clinic, counselor, youth-friendly service, pharmacist, hotline;
   **(3)** accurate information; **(4)** your own choice, with consent; **(5)** follow-up. For each
   step, name what actually happens and who is there to help.
3. **Meet your rights (8 min).** You have a right to **accurate information**, to **privacy and
   confidentiality**, to be treated **without judgment**, and to **decide what happens to your own
   body**. One honest limit: confidentiality is not absolute — if your safety is at risk, a trusted
   adult or service must act to protect you.
4. **Practice asking (12 min).** In pairs, role-play (you may pass): ask a health worker a question
   calmly and clearly — "I have a question about ___; can you help me find accurate information?" —
   and hear how a good service answers.
5. **Close (10 min).** Write your own three-line pathway: one trusted adult, one kind of service you
   could reach, and one right you now know you have. You are not alone in this; the path is real.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use three steps — trusted adult, service, your choice — and name one real adult and
  one real place.
- **Extension:** Research one actual local or national service (by name) and add it to your map with
  what it offers and how to reach it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/performance):** Can the learner describe the pathway and at least two
  rights, and ask for help clearly in role-play?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The learner's three-line pathway (trusted adult, service, right), kept in
  the portfolio.

## Home connection

Find out — from a trusted adult or an official source — one real place near you where a young person
can get accurate sexual-health information or care. Write its name down; knowing the name is half
the journey.
