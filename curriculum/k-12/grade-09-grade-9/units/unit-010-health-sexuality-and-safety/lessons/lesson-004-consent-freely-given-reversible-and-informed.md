# Lesson 4 — Consent: Freely Given, Reversible & Informed

## Summary

Learners explain **consent** — an agreement that is **freely given, reversible, and informed**, never
assumed from silence or a previous yes — and practice asking, giving, and refusing with clarity in
everyday and intimate situations. Consent is framed as respect for a person's **autonomy** over their
own body, not as a hurdle or a game.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health, including consent. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

You already practice a form of consent every day. When a friend asks "can I borrow your jacket?" and
you say "sure," that is consent; when you say "not today," and they accept it without pushing, that
is consent being respected. Consent is not a special rule invented for one situation; it is the
everyday practice of **asking before acting on someone else's body or things, and honoring the answer
— yes or no**. What changes in intimate situations is only how much the stakes rise. The principle
never changes: your body is yours, and so is everyone else's.

## Materials

- Consent-principles card
- Scenario slips

## Preparation

- Copy the principles card and scenario slips.
- Retrieval: from Grade 5, recall the idea of consent for one's own body; from Grade 9 Relationships
  (Unit 5), recall healthy relationship skills including consent and trust.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **consent is an agreement that is
freely given, reversible, and informed — a real, unpressured "yes" — and it is never assumed from
silence, from a past yes, from what someone wears, or from a relationship.** This is a skills lesson
with a strong values core, so use explicit instruction for the principles (worked example) and
role-play for the practice (S-011). Teach the principles plainly: **freely given** (not pressured,
coerced, or guilted — a yes under pressure is not a yes); **reversible** (a person can change their
mind at any moment, even mid-way, and the new answer stands); **informed** (a person needs the real
facts to agree); **not assumed** (silence, sleep, intoxication, or an earlier yes do not equal yes —
only a clear, ongoing yes does). The ethics lens: consent is how we honor another person's autonomy —
their right to decide what happens to their own body — and autonomy is the floor of all dignity. The
egalitarian lens: **consent belongs to everyone equally — every gender, every body, every
relationship — and no one's "no" matters less because of who they are.** The global lens: cultures
express agreement and refusal differently — some are direct, some indirect, some rely on gesture —
but the underlying standard is the same: a real, unforced agreement (UNESCO CSE, S-412). The technology lens: phones and social media add new consent questions — a photo or message shared without consent is a violation of consent, and digital spaces follow the same freely-given, reversible, informed standard as every other space. Hold the
room safely: **role-play is voluntary; a learner may always observe instead; and if anything a
learner shares suggests coercion or harm, follow your safeguarding path — believe, do not promise
secrecy, get help** (WHO, S-051). Keep the language clear and calm, and never frame consent as a
burden — it is the opposite: it is what makes closeness safe and mutual.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Unit 5: what did we learn about consent in healthy relationships? Today
   we make the principle precise and practice it.
2. **Meet the principles (12 min).** Worked example, guided: consent is an agreement that is
   **freely given** (no pressure — a yes squeezed out is not a yes), **reversible** (a person can
   change their mind at any time), and **informed** (they know what they are agreeing to). And it is
   **never assumed**: silence, sleep, intoxication, or a yes given last week are not a yes now. Only
   a clear, ongoing yes means yes.
3. **Read the scenarios (10 min).** For each scenario, ask: Is there real consent here? How do you
   know? Some are everyday (borrowing, hugging, sharing a seat), some are intimate. Notice that the
   same test applies to both.
4. **Practice asking and refusing (15 min).** In pairs, role-play, with the chance to pass: (a) ask
   for something clearly and accept either answer; (b) give a clear yes; (c) give a clear no and have
   it respected; (d) change your mind and have the change honored. Model one round first.
5. **Close (8 min).** Write two lines: one way you can *ask* clearly, and one way you can *say no*
   clearly, that you would actually use. Consent is not a hurdle — it is what makes "yes" real.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only everyday consent (borrowing, hugging, sharing) and use the
  single-sentence frames "Can I ___?" and "No, not right now — thanks for asking."
- **Extension:** Analyze the difference between a genuine yes and a pressured yes in one intimate
  scenario, and write what "checking in" during the moment would look like.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/performance):** Can the learner apply the principles to a scenario and
  give, ask for, or refuse consent clearly and respectfully?
- **Self-assessment:** The learner writes one reflection sentence on why a pressured yes is not a
  yes, kept with their scripts.

## Home connection

Practice clear asking and clear no in low-stakes moments this week — "Can I borrow this?" and "No,
thank you," said plainly. Notice how much easier real agreements are when both people are honest.
