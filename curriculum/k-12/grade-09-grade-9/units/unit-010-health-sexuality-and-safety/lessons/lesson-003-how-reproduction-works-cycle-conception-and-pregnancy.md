# Lesson 3 — How Reproduction Works: Cycle, Conception & Pregnancy

## Summary

Learners explain how the **menstrual cycle** works and how **conception and pregnancy** happen: an
egg is released, can be met by sperm, and if fertilization and implantation occur, a pregnancy can
grow in the uterus. They order the steps and read the cycle as a normal, variable rhythm — not a
mystery and not a source of shame.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health, including how reproduction works. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

You have probably heard fragments — a "period," a "fertile window," a "nine months" — without ever
seeing how they fit into one story. Put together, they form a single, orderly process that has been
happening in human bodies for as long as there have been humans, in every culture on Earth. It is
not magic and it is not shameful; it is **biology you can understand in about one lesson**. Knowing
how it actually works — not the rumor version — is what lets you understand your own body, plan your
life, and sort accurate information from myth.

## Materials

- Menstrual-cycle wheel
- Conception sequence cards

## Preparation

- Copy the cycle wheel and sequence cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the accurate names of the parts; from Grade 6, recall the changes

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **the menstrual cycle is a
normal, recurring rhythm in bodies with a uterus — roughly a month long but widely variable — and
pregnancy can begin when a released egg is fertilized by sperm and implants in the uterus.** This is
foundational biology, so use explicit instruction and worked examples: model the cycle wheel and the
conception sequence once, in order, before learners work (S-011). Accurate, appropriately hedged
facts to teach: a typical cycle averages about **28 days but ranges widely (about 21 to 35 days is
common, and more variation is normal)**; **ovulation** (an egg's release) usually happens around the
middle of the cycle; the uterine **lining thickens** to prepare for a possible pregnancy and is shed
as **menstruation** (a period) if none occurs; if **sperm** meets the egg in the fallopian tube,
**fertilization** can occur, and the fertilized egg can **implant** in the uterus and grow — a
**pregnancy**, which typically lasts about **40 weeks** (about nine months). Sperm can survive in the
body for several days, and an egg lives about a day after release, which is why timing matters for
both conceiving and avoiding pregnancy (WHO, S-261; UNESCO CSE, S-412). The egalitarian lens: **the
cycle is a normal, healthy function, not an illness or a curse, and it varies — some bodies do not
have a cycle, and that does not make them any less whole; some people cannot become pregnant, and
that is a fact of biology, not a failing.** The ethics lens: accurate knowledge about reproduction is
what lets a person make their own informed choices; no one else's shame or silence should decide for
them. The global lens: cultures worldwide have named and made sense of menstruation in many ways —
some celebrate it, some keep it quiet, some attach rules to it — and all deserve respectful
curiosity, while the *biology* is the same everywhere. And where accurate information and services
are missing, unplanned and adolescent pregnancy is more common — which is why this knowledge matters
(WHO, S-455). The technology lens: medical technology — from the ultrasound that watches a pregnancy grow to assisted-reproduction techniques that help people conceive when they could not otherwise — has changed what people can know and choose about reproduction, and it is spread very unevenly across the world, which is itself a matter of justice. Keep it factual, calm, and free of fear; cite the sources; and hold the door open for
questions without ever requiring a learner to share anything personal.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: name the organs where eggs are stored and where a pregnancy
   grows. Today we watch the process those organs carry out.
2. **Meet the cycle (15 min).** Worked example, guided on the wheel: a typical cycle runs about a
   month. **Menstruation** (the period, roughly days 1–5) sheds the lining built the month before.
   The lining then **rebuilds**; around the middle, **ovulation** releases an egg from an ovary; and
   if no pregnancy occurs, the lining sheds again and the cycle repeats. Mark the phases. Real cycles
   vary — 21 to 35 days is common, and "typical" is not "required."
3. **Meet conception (10 min).** The sequence, in order: **(1)** an egg is released at ovulation;
   **(2)** if sperm are present in the fallopian tube, one may meet and **fertilize** the egg;
   **(3)** the fertilized egg travels to the uterus and **implants** in the lining; **(4)** a
   **pregnancy** grows there for about 40 weeks (about nine months). No sperm, no fertilization — no
   pregnancy. That one fact is the hinge for the next two lessons.
4. **Order the cards (10 min).** In pairs, order the conception steps and, on the wheel, point to
   the part of the cycle when pregnancy is possible. Self-check against the worked example.
5. **Close (10 min).** Write one accurate sentence connecting the cycle, ovulation, and pregnancy —
   e.g., "Pregnancy can begin when a released egg is fertilized by sperm and implants in the
   uterus." You now hold the real story, not the rumor version.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use three steps instead of four — period, egg release, pregnancy can begin — and match
  each to a picture.
- **Extension:** Explain, using the wheel, why a person who does not want to become pregnant would
  want to know when ovulation happens — and why cycle-timing alone is not a reliable method.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner order the conception steps and locate the
  fertile part of the cycle on the wheel?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The labeled cycle wheel and the ordered sequence, kept in the portfolio (or,
  for a learner who does not see the wheel: the sequence spoken or dictated in order, or a tactile
  model of the cycle built from found objects and described aloud).

## Home connection

The next time the topic comes up in media or conversation, notice whether the account matches what
you learned today. Correct one misunderstanding — kindly — if the chance arises, and notice how much
calmer the real story is than the rumor.
