# Lesson 5 — Contraception & Preventing Infections

## Summary

Learners explain how the main **methods of contraception** work — barrier, hormonal, IUD, and
emergency — and how **condoms also reduce the risk of many sexually transmitted infections**, the
idea of **dual protection**, and the value of **testing**. They practice matching accurate
information to a person's needs without judgment.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health, including how to access accurate information
  and care. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Knowing how pregnancy begins — from Lesson 3 — is the key to knowing how to prevent it or plan for
it when and if you choose to. And just as there are ways to prevent pregnancy, there are ways to
reduce the risk of infections that can pass between partners. None of this is about judging anyone's
choices; it is about making sure that when a person does choose, they choose **with accurate
information** rather than rumor. Today you learn the tools and how they actually work — so that
decisions about your own body are yours, and informed.

## Materials

- Method-comparison chart
- Scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison chart and scenario cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall that no sperm meeting the egg means no pregnancy; from Lesson 4,
  recall that consent is the floor of every sexual choice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **the main approaches to
contraception are barrier methods (like condoms), hormonal methods (pill, patch, ring, injection,
implant), the IUD, and emergency contraception — and condoms are the only method that also helps
protect against many sexually transmitted infections (STIs), which is why "dual protection"
(condoms plus another method) is the standard advice for anyone wanting both pregnancy prevention and
infection protection.** This is foundational knowledge, so use explicit instruction with the worked
example of the comparison chart (S-011). Accurate, appropriately hedged facts to teach: condoms
(external and internal) are barriers that also reduce the risk of many STIs; hormonal methods prevent
ovulation and are highly effective for pregnancy but do **not** protect against infections; the IUD
(copper or hormonal) is a long-acting, very effective method; emergency contraception can reduce the
chance of pregnancy after unprotected sex but is for emergencies, not routine use; **STIs** are
infections caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites that can pass during sex — some are curable
with medicine (like chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis), some are manageable with treatment (like
HIV), and
many can be present **without symptoms**, which is why **testing** matters and why **vaccines** (for
HPV and hepatitis B) are part of prevention (WHO family planning, S-453; WHO STIs, S-454). The ethics
lens: accurate information is what makes a person's consent and choices truly free — withholding or
distorting it is a form of harm. The egalitarian lens: **contraception and infection prevention are
for everyone, every gender and orientation; there is no shame in using them and no hierarchy among
people who do or do not.** The global lens: access to contraception is deeply unequal around the
world — many people cannot get the methods others take for granted — and widening that access is a
matter of health and justice (WHO, S-453). The technology lens: methods are a technology of care,
developed by science and now unevenly distributed — a fact worth noticing, not a reason to distrust
them. Teach factually and without fear; a learner may always pass; and never pressure anyone to
share personal choices. Distinguish the facts (what methods do) from the values (that everyone
deserves access and no one deserves shame).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what single fact decides whether pregnancy can begin? (Sperm
   meeting an egg.) Contraception works by interrupting that meeting — or by preventing the egg.
2. **Meet the methods (15 min).** Worked example, guided on the chart. **Barrier** (condoms) block
   sperm — and also reduce the risk of many **infections**. **Hormonal** methods (pill, patch, ring,
   injection, implant) stop the release of an egg. The **IUD** sits in the uterus and prevents
   pregnancy for years. **Emergency contraception** is a backup after unprotected sex, not a routine.
   Mark on the chart which methods also protect against infections — only the barrier ones do.
3. **Meet infections and testing (10 min).** **Sexually transmitted infections** can pass during sex
   and often cause **no symptoms**, so a person can have one and not know it. Some are curable with
   medicine; some are manageable with treatment. That is why **testing** matters, and why condoms and
   **vaccines** (HPV, hepatitis B) are part of prevention.
4. **Match the scenarios (12 min).** For each scenario, name the accurate information the person
   needs: What protects against both pregnancy and infections? What is long-acting? What if a condom
   fails? Answer with the chart, not with guesses.
5. **Close (8 min).** Write one accurate sentence you now know that you did not know last week —
   about a method, an infection, or testing. Accurate information is a right, not a privilege.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on two ideas — condoms protect against pregnancy *and* many infections, and
  testing matters because infections can be silent.
- **Extension:** Explain dual protection in your own words and why "condoms plus one more method" is
  the standard advice for people who want both kinds of protection.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner correctly match a method to its protection and
  state that only barrier methods also reduce infection risk?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed method-comparison chart, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Notice how contraception and sexual health are talked about — or avoided — in media and at home.
Practice naming one accurate fact calmly; the goal is comfort with truth, not shock.
