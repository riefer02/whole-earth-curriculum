# Lesson 8 — Seeking Help: A Plan That Works

## Summary

Learners build a **personal help-seeking plan**: two trusted adults, one service they could reach,
practiced words for asking, and the urgent step — **if someone is in immediate danger, tell a trusted
adult now or contact emergency services.** The lesson's spine: **reaching out is a strength, and no
one should face struggle alone.**

## Objectives

- Describe how and where to seek help for mental health. _(D03.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

Recognizing a struggle — your own or a friend's — is only the first half. The second half, the one
that actually changes things, is **telling the right person and getting the right support**. Most
people who feel better did not get there alone; they got there because someone was told. And the
strange, beautiful fact about asking for help is that it looks like weakness and is actually the
opposite: **it is the strongest, most self-respecting thing a person can do.** Today you build your
own plan, so that if you or someone you love ever needs it, the hardest part — the first step — is
already done.

## Materials

- Help-seeking plan template
- "What to say" sentence starters

## Preparation

- Copy the plan template and sentence starters.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall the signs of struggle and the "lasting-and-interfering" test;
  from Lesson 6, recall the pathway to care and the rights that protect it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **seeking help is a concrete,
learnable skill with a plan — name two trusted adults, name one service you could reach, practice
what you would say, and know the urgent step — and it is a strength, never a weakness.** This is a
skills lesson, so use explicit instruction (the worked-example plan) and role-play for the asking
(S-011). Teach the plan's parts plainly: **(1) two trusted adults** you could actually tell;
**(2) one service** — a school counselor, a health worker, a helpline, a crisis line — that you could
reach; **(3) the words** — a practiced first sentence like "I've been feeling really low for a while
and I think I need help"; **(4) the urgent step** — **if someone is in immediate danger of harming
themselves, do not keep it secret: tell a trusted adult now, stay with them, and contact emergency
services or a crisis line.** Be direct and safe about the urgent step without being graphic: name
that it exists, that it is not a secret to keep, and that trained help is the response (WHO
adolescent mental health, S-311). Teach supporting a friend: **listen, do not judge, encourage
professional help, do not promise to keep danger secret, and take care of yourself too — you cannot
be the only support.** The ethics lens: when a friend tells you they are struggling, your loyalty is
to their safety, not to keeping a dangerous secret. The egalitarian lens: **help is for everyone;
no one has to earn it, and a person's worth is never the question — access is the question, and
unequal access is an injustice to work against.** The global lens: support takes many forms
worldwide — family, elders, faith communities, peer groups, clinics, helplines — and all of them
count. The technology lens: helplines, crisis text and chat lines, and teletherapy mean help can now reach a person far from any clinic or counselor — technology as a bridge to support, never a replacement for a trusted human being. Hold the room with care: **sharing a plan's contents is optional and private; a learner may
write names or initials or keep the plan to themselves; and if a learner discloses real risk, follow
your safeguarding path immediately and warmly** (WHO, S-051; S-311). End on hope and agency: **no
one should face struggle alone, and reaching out is how that changes.**

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: what is the "lasting-and-interfering" test, and why does
   crossing it mean seeking help? Today we build the actual plan.
2. **Meet the plan (12 min).** Worked example, guided on the template: **(1)** two trusted adults you
   could tell; **(2)** one service you could reach — a counselor, a health worker, a helpline;
   **(3)** the words you would say; **(4)** the urgent step. Fill each box, using initials if you
   prefer.
3. **Learn the urgent step (8 min).** If someone is in **immediate danger** — thinking of harming
   themselves — this is **not a secret to keep**. Tell a trusted adult **now**, stay with the person,
   and get to emergency services or a crisis line. Trained help is the response; keeping a dangerous
   secret is not loyalty.
4. **Practice the words (15 min).** In pairs, role-play (you may pass): say the first sentence of
   asking for help; then practice being the friend who hears it — listen, do not judge, and encourage
   getting help. Model one round first.
5. **Close (10 min).** Finish your plan and read it once to yourself. Write one line underneath:
   "Reaching out is a strength." Keep the plan where you can find it. You have just done the hard
   part in advance.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Complete only two boxes — one trusted adult and one sentence you would say — and say
  them aloud.
- **Extension:** Write a short script for supporting a friend who is struggling, including exactly
  when and how you would involve an adult.

## Assessment

- **Formative (performance/self):** Can the learner state their plan's parts and say the first
  sentence of asking for help?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The personal help-seeking plan (kept private; shared only if the learner
  chooses), in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Tell one trusted adult, in your own words, that you are building this plan — and ask them to be one
of the people on it. A plan is stronger the moment it has a real person in it.
