# Lesson 7 — Recognizing Mental-Health Struggle

## Summary

Learners learn to **recognize the signs that a person — themselves included — may be struggling with
their mental health**: lasting low mood, lost interest, big changes in sleep or eating, withdrawal,
irritability, and hopelessness. They learn the practical test — **signs that last about two weeks or
more and get in the way of daily life are a reason to seek help, not a reason for shame.**

## Objectives

- Recognize signs of mental-health struggles in themselves or others. _(D03.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

Everyone knows what a sprained ankle looks like — swelling, pain, not being able to walk on it — and
no one is ashamed to rest it or see someone about it. Mental health is no different in kind: it can
be hurt, and when it is, there are **signs** — in mood, sleep, appetite, energy, and how much a
person pulls away from others. The difference is that we are taught to hide these signs instead of
read them. Today you learn to read them — in yourself and in people you care about — so that a
struggle can be met with help early, the way a sprain is, and never with shame.

## Materials

- Signs-of-struggle chart
- Vignette cards

## Preparation

- Copy the signs chart and vignette cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the signs of stress or distress and one self-care action; from
  Grade 7, recall how social media can affect mental health.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **mental health is something
everyone has, it moves along a spectrum, and a struggle shows up as a *pattern of change* — lasting
low mood, lost interest in things once enjoyed, big changes in sleep or eating, withdrawal from
people, irritability, trouble concentrating, and hopelessness — that lasts about two weeks or more
and gets in the way of daily life.** This is knowledge plus a compassionate skill, so use explicit
instruction for the signs (worked example) and discussion for the vignettes (S-011). Teach the
practical test plainly and repeatedly: **it is not any single feeling that matters; it is a *pattern*
— several signs, lasting, and interfering.** Make the anti-stigma point the spine of the lesson:
**struggling is not weakness and not a character flaw; mental-health conditions are real and
treatable, and naming a struggle is the first step to getting help** (WHO adolescent mental health,
S-311). The egalitarian lens: **struggle does not discriminate — it touches every gender, every
background, every culture — and no one deserves less care because of who they are; yet stigma and
unequal access mean some people suffer in silence longer, which is an injustice to name.** The global
lens: cultures understand and express mental distress differently — some name it in the body, some in
the spirit, some in the mind — and all are real ways of experiencing it; what is universal is that
support helps. The ethics lens: how we respond to a friend who is struggling — with care, not gossip,
not judgment — is a moral choice. The technology lens: digital life cuts both ways — social media and endless screens can deepen a struggle (comparison, disrupted sleep, withdrawal into a device), while the same technology — helplines, text and chat lines, teletherapy, support apps — can widen the path to help; the skill is to notice the effect and choose what supports you. Hold the room with care: **use the vignettes, never a learner's own
story, for public discussion; let any learner pass; and if a learner or vignette touches on
self-harm or suicide, handle it directly and safely — thank them for their honesty, do not leave
them alone with it, and connect them to a trusted adult or service now** (see the next lesson's
help-seeking paths; WHO, S-311). Do not teach methods, do not sensationalize, and always pair the
hard truth with agency: **help exists, and asking is a strength.**

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what are signs that stress is building, and what is one thing
   that helps? Today we learn to read the bigger signals.
2. **Meet the signs (12 min).** Worked example, guided on the chart: a person who is struggling may
   show **low mood that does not lift**, **lost interest** in things they loved, **big changes in
   sleep or eating**, **withdrawal** from people, **irritability**, **trouble concentrating**, or a
   sense of **hopelessness or worthlessness**. One sign on one day is a hard day — normal. The
   pattern is what matters.
3. **Learn the test (8 min).** The practical test: **several signs, lasting about two weeks or more,
   and getting in the way of daily life.** That is the line where "a hard day" becomes "a reason to
   seek help." There is no shame in crossing it — most people will, at some point in life.
4. **Read the vignettes (15 min).** For each short story, ask: What changed for this person? Which
   signs do you notice? Has it lasted and gotten in the way? Do they need help — and who could they
   tell? Discuss, but never diagnose; only trained professionals diagnose, and you do not need a
   diagnosis to deserve care.
5. **Close (10 min).** Write one sentence: a sign you now know to watch for, and one person you
   would tell if you saw it in yourself or a friend. Watching for a friend's struggle is an act of
   love; naming it is the beginning of help.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on three signs (lasting low mood, withdrawal, big sleep/eating changes) and one
  trusted adult to tell.
- **Extension:** Explain in your own words why "lasting two weeks or more and getting in the way" is
  a better test than any single feeling — and why a diagnosis is not required to seek help.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Can the learner name several signs and apply the
  "lasting-and-interfering" test to a vignette?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The learner's one-sentence reflection (a sign to watch for and a person to
  tell), kept privately in the portfolio.

## Home connection

This week, notice the difference between your own ordinary hard days and anything that lingers. If a
friend seems withdrawn for a while, one quiet, kind question — "you've seemed a bit low lately; want
to talk?" — can be the door that lets help in.
