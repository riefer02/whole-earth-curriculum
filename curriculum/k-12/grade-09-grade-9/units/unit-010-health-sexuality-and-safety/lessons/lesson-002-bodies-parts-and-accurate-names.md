# Lesson 2 — Bodies, Parts & Accurate Names

## Summary

Learners learn the **accurate names and basic jobs of the reproductive parts** — in bodies with a
uterus and ovaries and in bodies with testes — and why **knowing the real names** is a health skill
and a matter of dignity, not something to whisper or replace with slang.

## Objectives

- Explain the basics of sexual and reproductive health, including anatomy. _(D03.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Every part of your body already has a name — your elbow, your kneecap, your lungs. We say those
names plainly, without embarrassment, because we need them to explain where something hurts or how
something works. The parts of the reproductive system are just as real and just as nameable, yet
many people grow up knowing only whispered slang or no words at all. Knowing the accurate names is
not about being "grown-up"; it is about being able to **say clearly what you mean, understand your
own body, and get the right care**. Today you learn the real names and what each part does — with
the same calm dignity you would use for any other part of you.

## Materials

- Anatomy diagram sheet
- Word-bank cards

## Preparation

- Copy the diagram sheet and word-bank cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall the basic changes of puberty and that bodies change at different
  times; from Grade 3, recall the correct names for private body parts.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **every part of the body,
including the reproductive system, has an accurate name, and knowing and using those names — without
shame or giggling — is a health skill and an act of dignity.** This is foundational knowledge, so use
explicit instruction and worked examples: model labeling one diagram completely before learners work
independently (S-011). Use the accurate terms and say them calmly and matter-of-factly; your own
comfort sets the room's comfort. Key accurate terms to teach — **with a uterus and ovaries:** vulva
(the external parts: clitoris, inner and outer labia, the urethral and vaginal openings), vagina,
cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries; **with testes:** penis, scrotum, testes, epididymis, vas
deferens, urethra, prostate. Bodies vary, so describe parts by their presence ("people with a
uterus") rather than assuming parts equal gender; a person's parts do not define who they are. The
egalitarian lens: **there is no "normal" body — bodies vary in size, shape, and how they are made,
including intersex variations and differences of ability — and every one is a full, legitimate body.**
The ethics lens: accurate names protect people — a person who can name a part can report pain or
harm precisely and get care. The global lens: every language has words for these parts, and in many
cultures they are named plainly by parents, elders, and healers; the whisper-and-slang habit is one
cultural style, not a universal rule. The critical-thinking lens: slang and euphemism are not the
problem; the problem is *not knowing the real name when it matters.* The technology lens: medical imaging — from the labeled diagrams you hold today to ultrasound and other scans — is how we see inside a living body without opening it, and it has made reproductive health safer and more knowable; the same technology can also be turned to judge or police bodies, so we use it to inform, never to shame. Keep it factual and calm, cite
the anatomy/reproductive-health authority (WHO adolescent health, S-261; UNESCO CSE, S-412), and
remember: a learner may always pass; never require anyone to point at or name anything aloud if they
are not ready.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 6: what changes happen to bodies at puberty, and do they happen at
   the same time for everyone? Today we name the parts those changes involve.
2. **Meet the diagram (15 min).** Worked example, guided together: label the diagram of the body
   with a uterus and ovaries. **Vulva** — the external parts (clitoris, inner and outer labia, and
   the two openings). **Vagina** — the passage from the vulva to the uterus. **Cervix** — the neck of
   the uterus. **Uterus** — the muscular place where a pregnancy can grow. **Fallopian tubes** — the
   two paths an egg travels. **Ovaries** — where eggs are stored and released. Say each name aloud,
   once, plainly.
3. **Meet the second diagram (8 min).** Label the body with testes. **Penis** and **scrotum** — the
   external parts. **Testes** — where sperm are made. **Epididymis** and **vas deferens** — the path
   sperm travel. **Prostate** and **urethra** — the glands and the tube urine and semen leave
   through. Say each name aloud, once, plainly.
4. **Match and practice (12 min).** Match each name to its one-line job. Then, with a partner, say
   one sentence using an accurate name correctly — e.g., "The ovaries release an egg each cycle."
5. **Close (10 min).** Write one reason accurate names matter (for your health, for asking for help,
   for understanding your own body). There is nothing here to be ashamed of — these are just parts
   of a body, and your body deserves to be named with dignity.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on the five parts most relevant to understanding health and care, and match
  each name to its job with a picture cue.
- **Extension:** Trace the full path of an egg (ovary → fallopian tube → uterus) or of sperm
  (testes → vas deferens → urethra) and write the journey in one clear sentence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/peer):** Can the learner use the accurate names and match most parts to
  their jobs without confusion or embarrassment?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The labeled anatomy diagram, kept in the portfolio (or, for a learner who
  does not see the diagram or prefers not to keep it: a dictated or recorded verbal labeling of the
  parts, or a tactile model built from found objects and described aloud — so the knowledge is
  captured without requiring sight or writing).

## Home connection

Notice how calmly your family, community, or health materials name the parts of the body. If a
younger child in your life asks, practice answering with the accurate word, said plainly — the same
way you would name a knee or a lung.
