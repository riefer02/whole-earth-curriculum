# Lesson 1 — The Story I Am Writing

## Summary

Learners construct a **coherent personal narrative** that connects their **past, present,
and hoped-for future** — the "who I am becoming" reflection that opens the year-end
capstone. They gather three chapters, find the **thread** that links them, and name a
**theme**, holding the story as theirs to keep writing — never as a finished verdict.

## Objectives

- Construct a coherent personal narrative that connects my past, present, and hoped-for
  future. _(D01.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Every life is a story being told as it happens. The chapters you have already lived — the
place you came from, the people who shaped you, the hard and joyful turns — do not stay in
the past; they are the reasons you are who you are today and the material from which your
future will be built. Whether you grew up by a river, a sea, a mountain, a desert, or a
city street, you have a "then," a "now," and a "next." Pulling them into one story — not a
perfect story, a *true* one — is how you begin to author the next chapter instead of only
watching it happen.

## Materials

- Narrative map worksheet (three columns: past, present, hoped-for future, with a thread line)
- Writing journal or notebook
- Past artifacts (optional)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the narrative map worksheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8's "who I am becoming" work and this year's identity unit, recall
  the idea that identity is a *changing story*, not a fixed fact. Today we write the current
  page of it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a personal narrative is
coherent when its parts — past, present, future — are held together by a thread of meaning,
not just listed as events.** That thread is what McAdams calls *narrative identity*: an
internalized, evolving life story that links the reconstructed past, the experienced present,
and the imagined future to give a life unity and purpose (S-404). The future-facing part
draws on Markus and Nurius's *possible selves* — the selves a person hopes, and fears, to
become (S-399). Both are *descriptive theories*, not rules: a learner's story may be
fragmented, uncertain, or multiple, and **"I don't know yet" is a complete and honest
answer.** Do not rank stories; there is no "best" life to have lived (philosophy §8, §11).

Model the skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **ethics** lens: a truthful narrative includes the hard
chapters without turning a life into a verdict — the point is agency, not blame. The
**global** lens: every culture tells life stories, but in different shapes — some linear,
some cyclical, some carried in song, genealogy, or land (S-404 is one framework among many;
do not present the three-part arc as universal). The **egalitarian** lens: every learner's
story is worth telling and hearing; no life is more "interesting" than another. The
**technology & environment** lens: the feeds, platforms, and devices around us invite a
polished, edited version of a life, while the places we live — land, water, weather, city
or countryside — are part of the story too; a coherent narrative tells the *true* story of
both, not a filter-perfect one, and the medium (paper, voice, screen, soil) should serve the
truth rather than replace it. The
**social-emotional** lens: recalling the past can raise real feeling — invite, never force,
and let learners choose what to share (facilitation.md). Keep the tone gentle and
forward-looking: this is the capstone, and the goal is a learner who feels like the *author*
of their own life.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back: how did you describe yourself at the start of this year?
   What has changed since? You do not have to share aloud — just notice that the "you" of
   then and the "you" of now are connected but not identical.
2. **Meet the narrative map (8 min).** Look at the three columns: **past**, **present**,
   **hoped-for future**. Watch a worked example: a life with three chapters — *then*: a move
   to a new place and the fear of starting over; *now*: the friendships and skills built
   there; *next*: a hope to keep building things that help others. Notice the **thread**:
   "starting over taught me I can build." That one sentence is the coherence that turns a
   list into a story.
3. **Gather your chapters (15 min).** Fill your own three columns. Past: two or three real
   moments, people, or places that made you. Present: what you value, do, and care about
   now. Hoped-for future: the possible self you hope to become — and, if you wish, one you
   hope *not* to become. You may use an artifact (a photo, an old project, an object) to
   stand for a chapter.
4. **Find the thread (12 min).** Read across your three columns and ask: *what connects
   them?* Write one sentence — the thread. Then give it a name if you can: a **theme** such
   as "I keep going," "people matter to me," "I make things," or your own words. If no
   thread appears yet, write "still unfolding" — that is honest, and it is enough.
5. **Draft the story (12 min).** Write or record one short paragraph that moves from past to
   present to future along your thread. End with the next page you want to write.
6. **Close (3 min).** Remember: this is *your* story, and you hold the pen. It is not
   finished — you are still writing it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a two-column version (then, now) and a sentence starter for the thread
  ("What I learned from that is…").
- **Extension:** Add a *turning point* — the moment the story changed direction — and explain
  why it turned, connecting it to the theme.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state, in one sentence, the thread that links their
  past, present, and future — even if that thread is "still unfolding"?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The narrative map and the drafted
  narrative, added to the year-end portfolio as the "who I am becoming" reflection.

## Home connection

Ask an older family member or trusted adult: *what is one chapter of your life you are glad
you lived, and what did it teach you?* Listen for their thread. Then tell them one thing you
hope for in your own next chapter.
