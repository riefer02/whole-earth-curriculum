# Lesson 3 — My North Star and My People: A Goal and My Communities

## Summary

Learners set a **longer-term goal with a realistic plan and milestones** that will carry
them into Grade 10, and they **compare forms of community across cultures** — birth families,
chosen families, mutual-aid networks, and communal-work traditions such as *harambee*,
*gotong royong*, and the Andean *minga*. They close by naming the community they will lean on
as they pursue the goal, because no one reaches a north star alone.

## Objectives

- Set a longer-term goal with a realistic plan and milestones, and adjust it as needed.
  _(D01.S3.09.01)_
- Compare forms of community across cultures, including chosen families and mutual-aid
  networks. _(D02.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

A wish is not yet a plan. "I want to do well in Grade 10" becomes a *goal* only when you can
see the steps between here and there — the milestones you can check along the way, and the
willingness to change course when life changes. And no one walks that road alone. Everywhere
people belong to others — to the family they were born into, the family they *choose*, and to
networks of neighbors who help one another not for pay but because they belong to one another.
In a Kenyan town a *harambee* pulls a whole community together to build a school; in an
Andean village a *minga* brings neighbors to farm the terraces; in an Indonesian village
*gotong royong* shares the work of a shared need. Knowing your goal *and* your people is how
a year ahead becomes a road you can actually walk.

## Materials

- Goal-and-milestones worksheet
- Forms-of-community comparison chart
- Community example cards

## Preparation

- Copy the goal worksheet, the comparison chart, and the community example cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the "hoped-for future" column of your narrative map —
  that possible self is the seed of today's goal. From Lesson 2, recall the forces and people
  who shape you — some of them are the community you will lean on.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"), and it carries two objectives in one arc: a
**goal** (the "north star") and the **communities** a person stands with on the way. The
goal side rests on a real finding: *specific, challenging goals with feedback* improve
performance more than vague "do your best" intentions (Locke & Latham; S-223) — translate
"challenging" for age 14–15 as "meaningful and reachable," with milestones that give the
feedback. Purpose itself is found more by *trying and reflecting* than by sudden certainty,
and only a minority of young people report a clear sense of purpose — so "I am still
exploring" is a normal, healthy place to be (Damon; S-400). The community side draws on a
rich record: mutual aid — cooperation and reciprocity given freely out of belonging — is a
widespread human practice (Kropotkin; S-163), with named traditions across cultures:
*harambee* ("all pull together") in Kenya, *gotong royong* ("joint bearing of burdens") in
Indonesia, the *mink'a/minga* communal work of the Andes, and North American barn raisings
(S-164). Chosen families — the people we choose as kin — and mutual-aid networks are real,
honored forms of community, not substitutes for a "real" family (S-345 distinguishes mutual
aid from charity: in mutual aid, people give *and* decide together as equals).

Model the goal path once, then let learners build their own (S-011). The **global** lens is
the heart: treat every community form as a living tradition to understand, never a specimen
to rank — and remind learners to verify local details with community members rather than
freezing any tradition into a stereotype (S-164). The **egalitarian** lens: chosen families
and mutual-aid networks are often how people with fewer resources *create* the care no
institution provides — that is strength, not deficit. The **ethics** lens: ask *who decides*
in a community — the difference between mutual aid and charity is who holds the power
(S-345). The **technology & environment** lens: online communities and mutual-aid funds can
extend belonging across distance — and a goal may also be ecological (stewarding a shared
garden or waterway) — but ask who is included and who is left out. Hold all of this as
description and invitation, never as a single required life-path (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From your narrative map's "hoped-for future": what is one possible
   self you hope to become? Hold it in mind — it will become your north star.
2. **Meet the goal path (8 min).** Watch a worked example: the goal *"be ready to study the
   sciences I love in Grade 10"* becomes a plan — milestone 1, by month's end, list the two
   subjects and find one person who works in that field; milestone 2, build a study habit and
   a first project; milestone 3, check progress and adjust. Notice the path has room to
   **adjust**: if a milestone fails, you do not abandon the star — you re-route the road.
3. **Set your goal and milestones (12 min).** On your worksheet: write a longer-term goal
   that matters to you and is reachable in the coming year. Break it into **three milestones**
   with a "by when" for each. For one milestone, write one thing that could go wrong and how
   you would adjust.
4. **Compare forms of community (15 min).** Use the example cards and comparison chart. For
   each form — a birth family, a chosen family, a *harambee* self-help group, a *minga* work
   party, a *gotong royong* village effort, a mutual-aid fund, an online interest community, a
   neighborhood — record *what holds it together*, *what it gives*, and *where it is found*.
   Then ask: what do they share? How does each meet a need its members could not meet alone?
5. **Connect goal to people (10 min).** Answer in writing: *which community will you lean on
   as you pursue your goal — and what will you give back to it?*
6. **Close (5 min).** A north star and a people: the goal tells you where you are going; the
   community walks with you. Both are yours to keep building.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a goal with the first milestone pre-filled, and ask learners to add
  two more steps; compare two community forms instead of all eight.
- **Extension:** Add a "what could go wrong → how I adjust" plan for each milestone, and
  compare a mutual-aid network with a charity, explaining who decides in each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state a longer-term goal, three milestones with
  dates, and one planned adjustment — and name a community they will lean on?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed goal-and-milestones
  worksheet and the forms-of-community comparison chart, added to the year-end portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: *what goal did someone in our family or community once set — and who helped them
reach it?* Then share your own goal and one milestone with a person you trust, and ask them to
help you check on it.
