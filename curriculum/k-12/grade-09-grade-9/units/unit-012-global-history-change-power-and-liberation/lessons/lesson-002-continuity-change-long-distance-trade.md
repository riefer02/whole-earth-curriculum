# Lesson 2 — Continuity & Change: Long-Distance Trade

## Summary

Learners analyze **continuity and change** in one global process — long-distance trade —
across three networks and many centuries: the overland **Silk Roads**, the monsoon-driven
**Indian Ocean**, and the **trans-Saharan** routes. They learn the skill of a
continuity-and-change analysis through a worked example and guided practice.

## Objectives

- Analyze continuity and change in long-distance trade across several regions and
  centuries, naming what stayed the same and what changed. _(D08.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Picture a market near you — or one you have seen in a story or film. Some things about it
have not changed for a very long time: people still come to trade, and what one person
has is what another needs. Other things have changed completely: the goods, the money,
the speed. A market is a doorway into the oldest continuous human process on Earth:
**trade**. Today we trace how that process has stayed the same and changed across regions
and centuries.

## Materials

- Continuity-and-change sheet
- Trade-network cards

## Preparation

- Copy the sheet and the cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the unit's organizing idea (change and continuity, many
  voices). Today we apply it to trade.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a
continuity-and-change analysis names two things at once — what *stayed the same* over
time (the goods and ideas that kept moving; the fact that trade connected strangers
across faiths and lands) and what *changed* (the routes shifted, new goods joined old
ones, and new tools of travel spread the network).** The three networks: **(1) the Silk
Roads** — overland routes linking East Asia and the Mediterranean, carrying silk, spices,
paper, and ideas such as Buddhism (S-323); **(2) the Indian Ocean** — monsoon-driven
sailing in dhows linking East Africa, Arabia, India, and Southeast Asia, carrying gold,
cloth, rice, and religions, and famously reached by Zheng He's Ming fleets, 1405–1433
(S-323, S-265, S-207); **(3) the trans-Saharan** — camel caravans trading gold for salt
across the desert, feeding cities like Timbuktu (S-470). This is a *skill* lesson, so use
explicit instruction and a worked example first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011):
model a two-column analysis on one network, then guided practice on the other two, then
independent work. The **global** lens: trade is a shared human practice on every
continent. The **technology** lens: the tools of travel — ships, saddles, compasses,
caravanserai rest-stops — changed how far and fast trade could reach. The **egalitarian**
lens: ask who profited and who did the dangerous carrying. Keep questions open
(philosophy §11): the lesson offers evidence, not a single "winner" story.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what does "continuity and change" mean? Today we
   apply it to one process: trade.
2. **Worked example — the Silk Roads (10 min).** Watch the model. Over centuries, the
   Silk Roads **stayed the same** in this way: they kept carrying silk, spices, and ideas
   such as Buddhism between East Asia and the Mediterranean. They **changed** in this
   way: paper — invented in China — joined the goods, and rest-stops called caravanserai
   grew up along the routes. Two columns: *stayed the same*, *changed*.
3. **Guided practice — the Indian Ocean (12 min).** In a group, fill the sheet for the
   Indian Ocean. Clues: monsoon winds powered sailing for centuries (continuity); the
   arrival of Zheng He's great fleets in the early 1400s and the spread of new ports
   marked change. What stayed? What changed?
4. **Independent work — the trans-Saharan (12 min).** Now do it alone. Gold moved north
   and salt moved south for centuries by camel caravan (continuity); the rise of cities
   such as Timbuktu as centers of learning and books was a change. Fill both columns.
5. **Compare (8 min).** Trade networks all shared one thing: they connected strangers.
   What was the deepest *continuity* across all three? What was the deepest *change*?
6. **Close (5 min).** Trade changed the world without ever losing its oldest shape. Next:
   we trace a second global process — the growth of cities.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two columns pre-filled for one network and ask learners to
  match each fact to "stayed" or "changed."
- **Extension:** Trace one good (silk, gold, or paper) across two networks and describe
  how its meaning or value shifted from one place to another.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one continuity and one
  change for each network, with a fact to support each?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed continuity-and-change sheet, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: what is one thing your family or community has traded or exchanged in the
same way for a long time — and what has changed about how it is traded?
