# Lesson 7 — The Transatlantic Slave Trade & Forced Migration

## Summary

Learners analyze the **transatlantic slave trade** and other forced migrations as global
movements of people with lasting effects. They face the scale of the trade with care, and
they center the **resistance and humanity** of the people taken — maroon communities,
music, religion, and family.

## Objectives

- Analyze the transatlantic slave trade and other forced migrations as global movements
  of people, and their lasting effects on societies, with care and respect. _(D08.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

Every person has a story of how their family came to be where they are. Some journeys
were chosen; some were not. There is a kind of movement that is not migration by choice at
all, but **forced migration** — people taken against their will. The largest of these in
modern history was the transatlantic slave trade. It is a hard thing to face, and facing
it honestly is part of respecting the people it harmed. Today we do that, and we also
honor what they refused to lose.

## Materials

- Evidence cards
- Effects sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the evidence cards and the effects sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, recall that decline and change are multi-causal. Today we
  study a *forced* change — a movement of people, not a choice.

## Facilitator note

This is a hard topic; hold it with care (facilitation.md). **Name feelings before facts,
offer a pause, and let learners step away.** The idea to land: **the transatlantic slave
trade was a global system that forcibly moved an estimated 12.5 million African people
across the Atlantic (of whom roughly 10.7 million survived the crossing), and it was part
of a wider set of forced migrations — including the Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades —
whose effects last to this day** (S-322, S-274). Crucially, teach **agency alongside
atrocity**: enslaved people resisted — through revolt, sabotage, running away, and the
building of free communities — the **quilombos** and maroon settlements such as Palmares
in Brazil (S-476) — and they kept their cultures alive through music, religion, family,
and language, which became lasting world contributions (S-274). Lasting effects: the
demographic reshaping of the Americas, the African diaspora, and the cultural forms born
of it — from musical traditions to religions to cuisines. Do not use graphic images or
numbers as shock; state scale soberly and move to humanity. The **ethics** lens: the
trade was a system built on the denial of human dignity. The **egalitarian** lens: whose
labor built the wealth, and who was credited? The **global** lens: this was a movement
that connected three continents and reshaped all of them. The **critical-thinking** lens:
distinguish documented evidence from later myths. End on dignity and agency, never on
despair (philosophy §12).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6: change is rarely one cause. Today we study a change
   people were *forced* into — a movement of people, not a choice.
2. **Name the scale (10 min).** Read the evidence. The transatlantic slave trade forcibly
   moved an estimated 12.5 million African people across the Atlantic, over about four
   centuries. The ocean crossing — the Middle Passage — was brutal and deadly. The trade
   was part of a wider pattern of forced migrations, including the Saharan and Indian
   Ocean slave trades.
3. **Trace the effects (15 min).** Fill the effects sheet. *On people:* the African
   diaspora — communities of African descent across the Americas and beyond. *On places:*
   the demographic reshaping of the Americas. *On culture:* music, religion, language,
   and cuisine born of the meeting of African, Indigenous, and European peoples.
4. **Center resistance (12 min).** Read the card on resistance. Enslaved people resisted —
   through revolt, refusal, escape — and built free communities called quilombos or
   maroon settlements, such as Palmares in Brazil. Write one sentence on what they
   refused to lose, and how they kept it alive.
5. **Connect to now (8 min).** Where do you see the lasting effects of this history today —
   in music, food, language, or in the ongoing work for repair?
6. **Close (5 min).** The trade tried to deny people's humanity; they kept it. Next: how
   empire worked as a system of power.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the effects sheet with categories pre-filled and ask learners to
  add one example to each.
- **Extension:** Compare the transatlantic trade with another forced migration and name
  what they share and how they differ, using evidence.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state the scale of the trade soberly, name
  one lasting effect, and describe one form of resistance?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed effects sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an elder at home about a family or community story of migration — chosen or forced —
and how it shaped your family. Listen and reflect in writing (no one is required to share
anything private).
