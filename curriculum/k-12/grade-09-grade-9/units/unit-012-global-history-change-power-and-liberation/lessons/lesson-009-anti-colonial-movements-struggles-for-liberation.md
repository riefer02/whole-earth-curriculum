# Lesson 9 — Anti-Colonial Movements: Struggles for Liberation

## Summary

Learners analyze **anti-colonial movements** and the strategies they chose — armed
struggle, nonviolence, boycott, and organized protest — through five cases: the **Haitian
Revolution**, the **Aba Women's War**, India's **Salt March**, **Algeria**, and **Ghana**.
They see one shared goal: **self-rule and dignity**.

## Objectives

- Analyze colonialism and empire as systems of power and the anti-colonial movements that
  fought for liberation. _(D08.S4.09.01)_
- Explain how ideas of freedom and dignity fueled struggles for liberation across
  different continents. _(D08.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever seen people work together to change something unfair — a group that refused
to accept a rule, or stood together when standing alone was too hard? That is what a
movement is: ordinary people acting together for a common freedom. Across the world,
colonized peoples built exactly such movements — and they chose very different tools for
their different moments. Today we meet five of them.

## Materials

- Movement cards
- Strategy sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the movement cards and the strategy sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 8, recall empire as a system of actions, rules, and beliefs.
  Today we meet the movements that undid it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **anti-colonial movements
shared one goal — self-rule and dignity — but chose different strategies for their
different moments: armed struggle, nonviolence and civil disobedience, boycott, and
organized mass protest.** Five documented cases: **(1) the Haitian Revolution
(1791–1804)** — enslaved people rose against French colonial rule and built the first
nation founded by formerly enslaved people, with Toussaint Louverture a central leader
(S-384); **(2) the Aba Women's War (1929)** — thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women in
southeastern Nigeria organized against colonial taxation and the warrant-chief system
(S-209); **(3) India's Salt March (1930)** — Mahatma Gandhi and followers walked some 240
miles to the sea and peacefully made salt, breaking an unjust British monopoly, as civil
disobedience within the independence movement (S-390); **(4) Algeria's war of
independence (1954–1962)** — the FLN fought an armed struggle against French rule after
132 years (S-478); **(5) Ghana's independence (1957)** — Kwame Nkrumah's Convention
People's Party used boycotts, strikes, and nonviolent "Positive Action," and Ghana became
the first sub-Saharan African colony to win independence (S-276). Note the spread across
continents and centuries — the Haitian Revolution in the Caribbean, the Aba Women's War
and Ghana in West Africa, the Salt March in South Asia, Algeria in North Africa. The
**egalitarian** lens: center ordinary people's collective action, not only famous leaders.
The **ethics** lens: weigh strategies honestly — nonviolence and armed struggle each have
costs and contexts; the curriculum does not rank peoples' choices. The **global** lens:
this was one connected wave, not separate stories. The **critical-thinking** lens: ask
*why* a people chose the tool they chose — context, not temperament, usually decides. Keep
questions open (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 8: empire is a system of actions, rules, and beliefs.
   Today we meet the movements that undid it.
2. **Meet five movements (12 min).** Read the movement cards. For each, note the date,
   the place, and the strategy:
   - **Haiti (1791–1804):** armed struggle; the first nation founded by formerly enslaved
     people.
   - **Aba Women's War (1929):** organized mass protest against colonial tax.
   - **India's Salt March (1930):** nonviolent civil disobedience.
   - **Algeria (1954–62):** armed struggle after 132 years of French rule.
   - **Ghana (1957):** boycotts, strikes, and nonviolent "Positive Action."
3. **Build a timeline (8 min).** Place the five in order. What does the spread of dates and
   places tell you? (Liberation was a connected wave, not one story.)
4. **Compare strategies (15 min).** Fill the strategy sheet. Why might Haiti and Algeria
   have chosen armed struggle, while India and Ghana chose nonviolence and boycott? What
   did all five share?
5. **Center the people (10 min).** Each movement was made of ordinary people, not only
   famous leaders. Write one sentence naming the *people* who carried one movement.
6. **Close (5 min).** Self-rule was won by movements. Next: the ideas of freedom and
   dignity that traveled with them — and are still traveling.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-drawn timeline and ask learners to add each movement and one
  strategy word to it.
- **Extension:** Compare nonviolent and armed strategies and argue when each was chosen,
  using evidence from two cases and naming your reasoning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place the five movements in time, name each
  strategy, and explain why strategies differed?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed strategy sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: has our family or community ever been part of a movement — or refused an
unfair rule together? What did they do, and what happened?
