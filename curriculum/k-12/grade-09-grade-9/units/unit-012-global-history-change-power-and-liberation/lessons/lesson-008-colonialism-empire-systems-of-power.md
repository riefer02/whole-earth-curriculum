# Lesson 8 — Colonialism & Empire as Systems of Power

## Summary

Learners analyze colonialism and empire as **systems of power** built of three layers —
**individual actions**, **rules and laws**, and **structures and beliefs**. They see that
empire is a global phenomenon (not only European), and that undoing it took movements,
not just speeches.

## Objectives

- Analyze colonialism and empire as systems of power. _(D08.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Think of any unfair rule you have seen — a line some people cannot cross, a rule that
lands harder on some than others. A single unfair act is one thing; a *system* is many
acts, rules, and beliefs working together so that the unfairness keeps happening even
when no single person decides it each time. Empire worked that way. Today we take empire
apart to see how its power was built — so we can understand how it was finally undone.

## Materials

- Layer cards
- System sheet

## Preparation

- Copy the layer cards and the system sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall that forced migration was a *system*, not a series of
  accidents. Today we analyze the larger system of power behind it: empire.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **colonialism and empire
were systems of power, not just events — built of three layers working together:
(1) individual actions — taking land, forcing labor, punishing languages, extracting
gold, rubber, and people; (2) rules and laws — colonial borders drawn far away, taxes,
forced-labor codes, school rules against home languages; (3) structures and beliefs — the
idea that some peoples are "lesser," and an economy built to send wealth to the
colonizer.** Teach empire as a *global* phenomenon so the framing is not Eurocentric:
empires — the Ottoman, the Mughal, the Qing, the Inka, and many more — are found across
continents and centuries; the lesson focuses on European overseas colonialism from the
late 1400s because that is the one the unit's essential questions and objectives center,
while naming that empire itself is not a European invention (S-326). Specific documented
examples: the 1884–85 **Berlin Conference**, where European powers drew Africa's borders
without Africans present (S-477); **indirect rule** and the warrant-chief system, against
which the women of southeastern Nigeria rose in the **Aba Women's War of 1929** (S-209);
and the suppression of languages, analyzed by the Kenyan writer **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o**
(S-424). The **egalitarian** lens: who decided, and who was excluded from deciding? The
**ethics** lens: naming a system's cruelty is not the same as claiming its victims were
helpless — they were not. The **global** lens: empire is everywhere in the human story.
The **technology** lens: ships, weapons, and railroads were the tools that extended
empire's reach. Keep it analytical, not despairing — the next lesson is about the
movements that undid it (philosophy §12).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: why was forced migration a *system*, not a series
   of accidents? Today we analyze the larger system behind it: empire.
2. **Three layers (12 min).** Read the layer cards. Sort each colonial practice into its
   layer:
   - **Actions:** taking land, forcing labor, punishing languages, extracting resources.
   - **Rules and laws:** borders drawn far away, taxes, forced-labor codes, school rules
     against home languages.
   - **Structures and beliefs:** the idea that some peoples are "lesser"; an economy
     built to send wealth to the colonizer.
3. **See the system (15 min).** Fill the sheet with real examples. At the Berlin
   Conference of 1884–85, European powers drew Africa's borders without Africans present.
   Under indirect rule, colonial governments appointed warrant chiefs and imposed taxes —
   against which the women of southeastern Nigeria rose in the Aba Women's War of 1929.
   Schools punished children for speaking their home languages.
4. **Empire is global (8 min).** Empire is not one region's invention: the Ottoman,
   Mughal, Qing, and Inka empires all held power too. Why does it matter to name empire
   as a *global* human pattern, not one nation's story?
5. **Why systems are hard to undo (10 min).** Write one sentence: why is a system —
   actions, rules, and beliefs together — harder to change than a single cruel act?
6. **Close (5 min).** Systems can be undone — by movements. Next: the movements that did.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three-layer sheet with the layer names filled in, and ask
  learners to add one example to each layer.
- **Extension:** Trace one colonial system from a specific region through all three
  layers, using a source.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place colonial practices into the three
  layers and explain how the layers worked together?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed three-layer system sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: what is one *system* — not just one person's choice — that you can see
shaping who gets what in your community? How is it built of actions, rules, and beliefs?
