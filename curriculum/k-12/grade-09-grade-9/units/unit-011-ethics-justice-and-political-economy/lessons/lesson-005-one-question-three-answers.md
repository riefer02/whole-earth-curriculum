# Lesson 5 — One Question, Three Answers

## Summary

Learners put the three lenses side by side on one moral question, producing the unit's first
portfolio artifact: a **multi-framework analysis**. They analyze the same question through
consequences, duties, and virtues, then **compare** what each shows and what each misses, and they
**weigh** the answers without being required to collapse them into one.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question using more than one ethical framework (consequences, duties, and
  virtues) and compare what each shows. _(D09.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

When a real decision matters, one lens is rarely enough. The friend who needs the truth, the
promise that must be kept, the scarce resource that must be shared — read any of these through
*one* lens and you see part of the picture; read it through *three* and the picture sharpens. Today
you do what thoughtful people everywhere do: **reason across more than one framework and compare
what each shows.**

## Materials

- Three-lens comparison sheet
- A moral question of the learner's choice

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison sheet and peer-review checklist.
- Prepare one question to model whole-group, drawn from the unit's question bank or from real
  shared life (a scarce resource, a hard truth, a promise).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) consolidate the three lenses into a
single skill — analyze one question three ways, then compare; (2) model once, then guide: work one
question whole-group across all three columns, then let each learner choose their own question and
work it (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011); (3) hold the outcome open — the goal is a
*reasoned* comparison, not a single "right" answer, and learners may legitimately weigh the three
answers differently (philosophy §11).** The ethics lens: more than one framework, held together,
is what honest moral reasoning looks like. The egalitarian lens: in every column, keep asking who
benefits and who is left out. The global lens: the frameworks themselves travel across cultures and
are argued about within each — present them as tools, not as one tradition's property. Distinguish
evidence from values: what a framework *shows* is a matter of careful reasoning; what we *value*
more is a choice we defend, not prove. This is the first portfolio artifact — keep it.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** From memory, write one sentence each for what the consequences, duties,
   and virtues lenses ask. Check against a partner.
2. **Model the comparison (15 min).** As a group, take one question. Fill the three columns
   together: consequences ("what happens, to whom?"), duties ("what do I owe?"), virtues ("what
   kind of person would I become?"). Then compare aloud: where do the answers agree? Where do they
   pull in different directions? What does each lens see that the others miss?
3. **Choose your own question (5 min).** Pick a moral question from the bank or your own life — one
   you genuinely find hard, and that is fair to discuss in this space.
4. **Analyze it three ways (20 min).** Fill all three columns for your question. Then write one
   short paragraph: *what does each framework show, and where do they differ?*
5. **Peer check (5 min).** Trade with a partner. Did each lens get a real answer? Was there an
   actual comparison, not just a list? Give one specific suggestion, kindly.
6. **Close (5 min).** Next we turn inward — to conscience and self-criticism, and how our own
   values stay honest.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each column ("The consequences lens shows…", "The
  duties lens shows…", "The virtues lens shows…").
- **Extension:** Weigh the three answers and defend a reasoned judgment, naming what each framework
  contributes and what you would want to know to decide better.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner give each lens a genuine, distinct answer and then
  compare them, rather than merely listing?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed three-lens comparison — the unit's multi-framework moral
  analysis.

## Home connection

Show someone at home your three columns and ask: which lens would *you* reach for first, and why?
Bring their answer next time.
