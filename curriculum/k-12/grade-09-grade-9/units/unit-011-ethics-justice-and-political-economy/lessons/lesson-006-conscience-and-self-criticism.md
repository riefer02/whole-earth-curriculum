# Lesson 6 — Conscience & Self-Criticism: Keeping My Values Honest

## Summary

Learners turn from analyzing questions to **forming their own values**. They meet conscience as a
loop — **act, reflect, self-criticize, revise** — and practice honest self-criticism that corrects
values without collapsing into self-condemnation, producing a **values reflection** as a portfolio
artifact.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the role of conscience and self-criticism in forming one's own values. _(D09.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

After a choice you regret, a quiet voice asks: *was that really who I want to be?* That voice — and
the habit of listening to it, questioning yourself, and changing course — is how a person's values
stop being inherited slogans and become their own. Every tradition has a name for this inner
correction. Today you learn to do it **honestly and kindly**, because the point is growth, not
punishment.

## Materials

- Conscience loop sheet
- Values reflection journal page

## Preparation

- Copy the loop sheet and journal page.
- Prepare to model the loop with a *mild, low-stakes* example of your own (never a learner's), so
  learners see honest self-correction done without shame.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) define conscience not as a fixed,
infallible voice but as a *loop* — act, reflect, self-criticize, revise — that keeps values honest;
(2) distinguish self-criticism (honest, specific, corrective) from self-condemnation (global,
destructive: "I am bad"), and insist on the former; (3) hold this as private, voluntary work — a
learner's values are their own, and no one is required to disclose (philosophy §6, §8).** The ethics
lens: forming values is a discipline, not a feeling. The egalitarian lens: self-criticism should ask
whether my values quietly favor people like me over others. The social-emotional lens: keep the tone
kind — this is about growth, never shame, and a learner may always pass or step away (facilitation
"holding difficult topics safely"). The global lens: conscience and self-examination are named
across traditions — in philosophy, faith, and Indigenous practice — and none owns the idea.
Distinguish evidence from values: *that* self-examination improves judgment is supported by
reflective-practice evidence; *which* values one holds is a personal matter the lesson never grades.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** Last time we compared three lenses. Now, name from memory: what is the
   difference between a value and a fact? (A value is what we hold to be good; a fact is how things
   are.)
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Read the conscience loop sheet. Conscience is not a fixed voice — it
   is a loop: **act**, **reflect** on what happened, **self-criticize** honestly, **revise** your
   values and next choices, then act again.
3. **Model it (10 min).** Watch the facilitator work the loop on a mild example of their own —
   naming what happened, what the choice revealed about a value, and what they will revise. Notice:
   no self-condemnation, only honest correction.
4. **Two kinds of inner criticism (5 min).** Tell the difference: **self-criticism** says "that
   choice was unfair; I can do better" (specific, corrective); **self-condemnation** says "I am
   worthless" (global, destructive). Which one builds a person?
5. **Reflect on your own values (15 min).** Privately, choose one value you hold. On your journal
   page, write: (a) what it is; (b) where it came from — a family, a tradition, a teacher, an
   experience; (c) one time it was tested; (d) whether the test changed it, and how. This is your
   own; you share only what you choose.
6. **Close (5 min).** Values are formed, not found — and self-criticism is how they stay honest.
   Next: we turn from the inner life to the wider world — systemic injustice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for the journal ("I hold the value of… because…"; "It was
  tested when…").
- **Extension:** In writing, distinguish self-criticism from self-condemnation and give one example
  of each in a character from a story or history.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one value, its origin, and how it was tested and
  revised — and do so without self-condemnation?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The values reflection journal page (shared only as the learner
  chooses).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: what value did you hold when you were my age that you later revised, and what
changed it? Bring one sentence next time.
