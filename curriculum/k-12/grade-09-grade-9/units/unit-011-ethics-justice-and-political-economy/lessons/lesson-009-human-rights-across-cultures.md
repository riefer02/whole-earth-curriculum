# Lesson 9 — Human Rights Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners evaluate the idea of **human rights across cultures**. They read the Universal Declaration
of Human Rights alongside the **African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights**, which adds collective
and people's rights, and they weigh critiques that human rights reflect some traditions more than
others — holding the idea as both **shared** and **genuinely contested**.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the idea of human rights across cultures, including critiques that they reflect some
  traditions more than others. _(D09.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

The claim "every person has dignity and rights" is one of the most powerful ideas humanity has
produced — yet it was written down in a specific time and place, and thoughtful people have argued
ever since about whether it fits every culture, and whether it leaves out duties, community, and
peoples' rights. To honor the idea, you must also hear its critics. Today you do both: **affirm the
idea and question it honestly.**

## Materials

- Human rights map sheet
- Document excerpts

## Preparation

- Copy the map sheet and excerpt sets.
- Prepare short, accurate excerpts: from the UDHR (Articles 1 and 2 on dignity and equality); from
  the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (its addition of duties and peoples'/collective
  rights); and one clearly worded critique that human rights documents reflect Western, individualist
  assumptions. Keep each brief and fair.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) teach the human-rights idea
accurately — the UDHR (1948) grounds rights in inherent dignity (Article 1), and is a *declaration*
adopted by states, not a claim that every person already enjoys these rights; (2) show that the
idea has been *extended and amended*, not merely accepted — the African Charter on Human and
Peoples' Rights (the "Banjul Charter," 1981) adds duties and peoples'/collective rights, and other
regional and Indigenous traditions do similar work; (3) hold the critique openly — critics argue
human-rights documents reflect some traditions more than others (individualism over community,
rights over duties), and this critique deserves a fair hearing, not dismissal or endorsement
(philosophy §11).** The ethics lens: affirming dignity and hearing its critics are both ethical
acts. The egalitarian lens: ask whose voice wrote the documents and whose was added later — and who
still lacks the rights on paper. The global lens: present the UDHR, the African Charter, and the
critique side by side, none as the default. Distinguish evidence from values: *that* these
documents exist and differ is a fact (cite them); *that* human rights are good, or that one
document is better, is a value claim learners weigh and defend. This is a portfolio artifact — keep
the evaluation.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** From memory: what is a right, and where did you first meet the idea
   (Grade 5: the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child)? Say it in one sentence.
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** Read the human rights map sheet. At the center is **human dignity**.
   Around it: **individual rights**, **peoples' and collective rights**, **duties and community**,
   and **critique**. The idea is shared — and it is also contested.
3. **Read the documents (15 min).** In groups, read the excerpts. What does the UDHR ground rights
   in? What does the African Charter add that the UDHR does not emphasize — duties, peoples' rights?
   What does the critique say the documents leave out?
4. **Evaluate (15 min).** Write one paragraph: *is the idea of human rights one every culture can
   share, or does it quietly favor some traditions over others?* Use at least one specific line from
   a document and one from the critique. You may land anywhere on this — but you must argue.
5. **Share carefully (5 min).** In pairs, share one sentence of your evaluation. Disagree with
   respect; the goal is reasoning, not agreement.
6. **Close (5 min).** The idea of rights is a work in progress, built by many hands and still being
   argued. Next: power — who has it, and how people hold it to account.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("The UDHR grounds rights in…"; "The African Charter adds…";
  "The critique argues that…").
- **Extension:** Weigh universalism against cultural relativism and defend a middle position that
  honors both dignity and cultural difference.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner cite a document and a critique, and argue rather
  than merely assert?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The written cross-cultural human-rights evaluation.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: what does "rights" mean in our family's or community's tradition — and what
duties come with them? Bring one sentence next time.
