# Lesson 3 — Duties: What We Owe

## Summary

Learners read the same everyday moral question through the **duties lens** — the traditions that
judge an act by **what we owe**, not by its results. They distinguish **duties owed to every
person** from **duties that grow out of relationships and roles**, and they name what this lens
captures and what it misses.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question through the duties lens, distinguishing universal duties from
  relationship-based duties, and compare what this lens shows with the consequences lens.
  _(D09.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

A promise you made, a confidence a friend shared with you, a stranger who needs help and no one is
watching — some things feel like they are required of you no matter what the result is. The duties
lens asks: **what do I owe, simply because we share a life?** It is a second, very different way
people everywhere have reasoned about right and wrong.

## Materials

- Duties branches sheet
- Worked-example dilemma card

## Preparation

- Copy the branches sheet and dilemma card (use the *same* dilemma as Lesson 2, so learners can
  feel the difference between the lenses).
- Prepare to model: "Would I want this to be a rule for everyone?" as the universal-duty test.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) introduce the duties lens honestly
— its modern form is deontology, most associated with Immanuel Kant (the idea of a universal rule
and treating persons as ends, never merely as means), but duty-based reasoning appears in many
traditions, including Confucian role ethics, Hindu and Buddhist accounts of dharma, and Indigenous traditions that ground duty in relationship, role, and reciprocity (S-468; S-469; S-427); (2) model
once, then guide: work the *same* dilemma as Lesson 2 but ask the duty question — what do I owe, no
matter what the result? — and test it with "would I want everyone to act this way?"; (3) name the
lens's limits — duty can seem rigid when consequences are severe, and it can conflict with care for
specific people.** The ethics lens: some things are owed regardless of outcome. The egalitarian
lens: a universal rule protects everyone equally — that is its power, and we must check that it
does not ignore real inequality. The global lens: duty is grounded differently across traditions —
in law, in kinship, in faith, in reciprocity — and none is the default. Keep the lens as *one* way
of seeing; virtues arrive next (philosophy §11). Distinguish evidence from values: that people feel
bound by duty is a fact; that a particular duty is binding is a value claim we argue about.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Without looking: what did the consequences lens ask, and what did it miss?
2. **Meet the lens (10 min).** Read the duties branches sheet. The lens asks: **what do I owe?**
   One branch is *duties to every person* (do not deceive; do not treat a person as a mere tool);
   the other is *duties from relationship and role* (a parent to a child, a promise kept, a role
   honored).
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Take the *same* dilemma as last lesson. Step by step: (a) name the
   act; (b) ask — would I want this to be a rule for everyone? (c) list duties to every person;
   (d) list duties from relationships and roles. Notice how the answer differs from the
   consequences lens.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In pairs, read a second dilemma through the duties lens and fill
   your own branches. Then say aloud: *what do I owe, no matter what the result?*
5. **Compare the two lenses (5 min).** Side by side — consequences asked "what happens?", duties
   ask "what do I owe?". Where do the two answers disagree? What does each see that the other
   misses?
6. **Close (5 min).** Two lenses down, one to go. Next: the virtues lens — what kind of person do I
   want to become?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two branches pre-labeled and one worked duty in each.
- **Extension:** Argue which account of duty — universal rule or relationship — better captures a
  duty to care for a distant stranger.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a universal duty and a relationship-based
  duty for a new dilemma, and contrast the duties lens with the consequences lens?
- **Artifact:** The completed branches sheet (or written duties), kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: is there a promise or duty you keep even when it costs you? What does it come
from — a rule, a relationship, or a role? Bring one sentence next time.
