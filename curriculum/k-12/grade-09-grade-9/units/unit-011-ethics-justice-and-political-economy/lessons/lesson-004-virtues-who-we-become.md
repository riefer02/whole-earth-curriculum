# Lesson 4 — Virtues: Who We Become

## Summary

Learners read the same moral question through the **virtues lens** — the traditions that judge an
act by **the kind of person it builds**. They trace how practice becomes habit, habit becomes
character, and character becomes a flourishing life, and they compare **virtues named across
cultures** without ranking them.

## Objectives

- Analyze a moral question through the virtues lens — what kind of person the act builds — and
  compare virtues named across more than one culture. _(D09.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Every repeated choice shapes you. A person who practices honesty becomes honest; a person who
practices care becomes caring; a person who practices courage becomes the kind of person who can
stand up when it costs something. The virtues lens asks: **what kind of person would I become by
doing this?** Cultures everywhere have named the traits of a life well lived — courage, honesty,
care, generosity — though they name and shade them differently.

## Materials

- Virtue loop sheet
- Virtue cards

## Preparation

- Copy the loop sheet and virtue cards.
- Prepare a few one-line meanings for virtues from more than one tradition (e.g., the Southern
  African *ubuntu* — "I am because we are"; the Diné *hózhó* — balance, harmony, beauty) — offered
  respectfully as living ideas, not specimens.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) introduce the virtues lens honestly
— virtue ethics is most associated with Aristotle (a virtue is a settled habit of character, built
by practice, aimed at flourishing, *eudaimonia*), but the idea of character and a good life runs
through many traditions, including Confucian, Buddhist, Islamic, and Indigenous frameworks; (2)
model once, then guide: trace the loop — practice → habit → character → flourishing — for one
virtue, then let learners trace another; (3) name the lens's limits — it says less about which
specific act to choose right now, and "virtue" can be used to judge others harshly or to privilege
one culture's list over another's.** The ethics lens: who we become matters as much as what we do.
The egalitarian lens: ask whose list of virtues gets treated as normal, and whether the virtues
named include care for the least powerful. The global lens: virtues are named and shaded
differently across cultures — hold them side by side, never ranked (philosophy §11). Distinguish
evidence from values: that cultures name virtues is a fact; that a particular virtue is good is a
value we argue about, and there is room for more than one answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Name the two lenses so far (consequences, duties) and one thing each misses.
2. **Meet the lens (10 min).** Read the virtue loop sheet. The lens asks: **what kind of person
   would I become by doing this?** Practice a virtue; it becomes a habit; habits form character;
   character makes a flourishing life.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Take the *same* dilemma as the last two lessons. Step by step:
   (a) name the act; (b) ask — which virtue would this practice, and which would it erode? (c) what
   kind of person does a lifetime of this build? Notice how the answer differs from the other two
   lenses.
4. **Virtues across cultures (10 min).** Look at the virtue cards. Which virtues appear in more
   than one tradition? How is each shaded differently by its own culture? Treat each as a living
   idea — ask before you assume, and rank none above another.
5. **Guided practice (10 min).** In pairs, choose one virtue and trace its loop in your own life:
   one small practice → the habit it forms → the character it builds → the life it makes possible.
6. **Close (5 min).** Three lenses, three answers. Next: we put them side by side on one question
   and compare what each shows.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the loop pre-labeled and one worked virtue (honesty) filled in.
- **Extension:** Compare one virtue across two traditions in writing, and say what each shading
  teaches that the other does not.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner trace a virtue through the loop and name one virtue
  that appears in more than one culture?
- **Artifact:** The completed loop sheet (or written virtue trace), kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: what trait does our family or culture most try to teach a child, and how is it
practiced in daily life? Bring one sentence next time.
