# Lesson 12 — The Roots of Conflict: Inequality, Fear, Scarcity

## Summary

Learners analyze the **causes of conflict and violence** — inequality, fear, and scarcity — and how
they can be addressed **at the root** rather than only at the surface. They trace a case from root
causes through conflict to possible remedies, producing a **roots-of-conflict analysis** as a
portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Analyze the causes of conflict and violence, including inequality, fear, and scarcity, and how
  they can be addressed at the root. _(D09.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Most conflict does not start with a single sudden insult; it grows from deeper ground — when some
have far more than others (**inequality**), when people feel unsafe or mistrust one another
(**fear**), and when there is not enough to go around and no fair way to share it (**scarcity**).
If you want peace, you cannot only cut the branches; you must tend the roots. Today you learn to
see the roots — and the remedies that match them.

## Materials

- Roots-of-conflict tree sheet
- Case study

## Preparation

- Copy the tree sheet and case studies.
- Choose a short, sourced case of conflict with traceable root causes (a dispute over land, water,
  wages, or belonging), at a depth the age can hold, and always paired with what people did to
  address it — hope attached (development "9–12"; facilitation "holding difficult topics safely").

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) teach the root causes of conflict
and violence — inequality (unequal share and unequal voice), fear (threat, mistrust, rumor), and
scarcity (genuine shortage or unfair division) — as *causes*, distinct from the surface *trigger*
that ignites them; (2) model once, then guide: trace one case from roots to conflict to remedy,
then let learners trace another (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011); (3) keep it
non-dogmatic — these are among the best-supported causes, not the only ones, and "addressing the
root" is offered as a strategy to evaluate, not a formula to accept (philosophy §11).** The ethics
lens: understanding causes is not excusing violence — it is the precondition for ending it. The
egalitarian lens: ask whose fear and whose scarcity are centered, and whether the remedies would
share power, not just resources. The environment lens: scarcity is often *environmental* — water,
land, a changing climate — so peace and stewardship are linked. The global lens: conflict looks
different across societies; present each case as one place's story. Distinguish evidence from
values: *that* inequality and scarcity correlate with conflict is a documented finding (cite a
source); *that* we should reduce them is a value we argue for. This is a portfolio artifact — keep
the analysis.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** From memory: what is the difference between a *conflict* (a disagreement)
   and *violence* (harm)? (One sentence — you met this in Grade 5.)
2. **Meet the tree (10 min).** Read the roots-of-conflict sheet. Three roots feed the trunk of
   conflict and violence: **inequality**, **fear**, **scarcity**. The branches are the responses —
   competition, exclusion, blame. The roots can be tended.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Together, trace one case: (a) name the conflict; (b) find the
   roots — which are inequality, fear, scarcity? (c) name the surface trigger; (d) propose one
   remedy for each root, and one way to prevent the next conflict.
4. **Trace a second case (15 min).** In groups, work a second case through the same steps. End by
   naming the *root* your remedy addresses — and why addressing the root differs from punishing the
   trigger.
5. **Weigh (5 min).** Which root is hardest to address, and why? What would it take — sharing
   resources, building trust, or sharing power?
6. **Close (5 min).** Peace is grown at the roots. Next: reconciliation and restorative justice —
   what happens *after* serious harm.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the tree pre-labeled and one worked root and remedy to model.
- **Extension:** Distinguish the root from the trigger in your case and argue which root is most
  consequential — and which remedy is most likely to last.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify inequality, fear, or scarcity as a root
  cause in a case and propose a remedy aimed at the root rather than the surface?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed roots-of-conflict analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: have you ever seen a conflict where the real cause was not enough to go
around, or fear, or unfairness — and what finally helped? Bring one sentence next time.
