# Lesson 13 — Reconciliation & Restorative Justice

## Summary

Learners evaluate **reconciliation and restorative justice** as responses to serious harm. They
learn the three restorative questions — **what happened, who was harmed, how do we make it right** —
and weigh both the **possibilities** (repair, truth, restored relationship) and the **limits** (when
harm is too great, when power is too unequal, when repair is refused), producing an **evaluation**
as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Evaluate reconciliation and restorative justice as responses to serious harm, including their
  possibilities and limits. _(D09.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

After something is broken — a trust, a community, a life — punishment alone asks only "who is to
blame, and what should they suffer?" Restorative justice asks three different questions: **what
happened, who was harmed, and how do we make it right?** It is not softness; it is a demanding
practice with real power and real limits. Today you learn to judge it honestly, not to admire or
dismiss it.

## Materials

- Restorative circle sheet
- Case study

## Preparation

- Copy the circle sheet and case studies.
- Choose short, sourced cases of reconciliation or restorative justice after serious harm, including
  at least one that shows its limits or failure, so learners weigh both sides. Hold heavy content
  with care and always connect to agency (facilitation "holding difficult topics safely").

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) teach restorative justice
accurately — it centers the three questions (what happened; who was harmed and what do they need;
who is responsible, and how is the harm repaired and the relationship restored), and its modern
frame traces to Howard Zehr and the International Institute for Restorative Practices, with older
roots in Indigenous peacemaking (e.g., Diné *hózhó* and peacemaking circles); (2) hold both sides —
its possibilities (truth, repair, restored relationship, accountability without mere vengeance) and
its limits (some harms may be too great; it can fail if power is unequal or if the responsible
person refuses; it is not a substitute for justice where impunity would result); (3) avoid dogma —
present restorative justice as *one* defensible response, compared with others, never as the only
right answer (philosophy §11).** The ethics lens: repair is a moral achievement, not a formula.
The egalitarian lens: ask whether the process shares power or reproduces the original inequality.
The global lens: reconciliation looks different across cultures — truth commissions, peacemaking
circles, community courts — none is the default. The community lens: serious harm breaks
relationships, and repair must include the community. Distinguish evidence from values: *that*
restorative practices exist and are documented is a fact (cite sources); *that* they are preferable
in a given case is a value learners argue. This is a portfolio artifact — keep the evaluation.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** From memory: what are the three roots of conflict (inequality, fear,
   scarcity)? Name them.
2. **Meet the circle (10 min).** Read the restorative circle sheet. Instead of "who is to blame,"
   restorative justice asks: **1. What happened? 2. Who was harmed? 3. How do we make it right?**
   Around the circle sit the person harmed, the person responsible, supporters, and a facilitator.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Together, evaluate one case: (a) what happened; (b) who was harmed,
   and what did they need; (c) who took responsibility, and how; (d) how was repair made — or not;
   (e) where did the process work, and where did it reach its limit?
4. **Evaluate a second case (15 min).** In groups, work a contrasting case — ideally one where
   reconciliation fell short. Answer the same five questions, and end by naming one possibility and
   one limit.
5. **Weigh (5 min).** For what kinds of harm does repair work best? When is it not enough? Is it
   ever a *replacement* for justice, or only a *form* of it?
6. **Close (5 min).** Repair is possible — and it is hard, and it has limits. Next: our capstone,
   where every thread of this unit is woven together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the five evaluation questions pre-printed and one worked answer.
- **Extension:** In writing, weigh restorative justice against punishment for one specific harm and
  defend a reasoned position, naming the conditions under which you would choose each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three restorative questions and state one
  possibility and one limit, grounded in a case?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed reconciliation-and-restorative-justice evaluation.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: after a serious falling-out, what actually repaired it — an apology, time, an
honest talk, a change in behavior? Bring one sentence next time.
