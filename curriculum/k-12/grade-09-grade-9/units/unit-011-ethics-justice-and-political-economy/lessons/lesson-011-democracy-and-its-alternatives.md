# Lesson 11 — Democracy & Its Alternatives

## Summary

Learners evaluate the **strengths and limits of democratic participation** and compare it with
**alternatives** across societies. They place ways of deciding on a spectrum from one-person rule to
broad participation, weigh what democracy protects and what it still excludes, and produce a
**comparison** as a portfolio artifact.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the strengths and limits of democratic participation, and compare with alternatives
  across different societies. _(D09.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

How should a group decide anything — by one person's word, by a few elders or owners, by a vote, or
by talking until everyone can live with it? Your school, your town, and every country answer this
differently. Democracy — rule by the many — is one answer with real strengths and real limits, and
honest citizens weigh it against the alternatives rather than praising it automatically. Today you
compare them with open eyes.

## Materials

- Ways-of-deciding spectrum sheet
- Comparison chart

## Preparation

- Copy the spectrum sheet and comparison chart.
- Prepare accurate, non-ranking examples of each way of deciding from more than one region,
  including consensus-based councils (e.g., the Haudenosaunee) as a living form of broad
  participation, not a "primitive" stage.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) teach the ways societies decide,
placed on a participation spectrum — rule by one (autocracy, monarchy), by a few (oligarchy,
aristocracy), by many through voting and parties (democracy), and broad participation through
deliberation and consensus (including Indigenous and other council traditions); (2) evaluate
democracy honestly — its strengths (equal voice, peaceful transfer of power, accountability) and
its limits (majorities can overlook minorities, wealth can capture voice, participation is unequal);
(3) avoid dogma — democracy is *compared*, not preached; alternatives are described accurately, not
caricatured, and learners may reach different conclusions about which arrangement best serves
justice in a given place (philosophy §11).** The ethics lens: how a group decides is itself a moral
question. The egalitarian lens: in every arrangement ask *who gets a real voice, and who is
excluded.* The global lens: democracy and its alternatives appear in many cultural forms across
societies — none is the default, and consensus traditions are living, not relics. Distinguish
evidence from values: *that* participation is uneven is a documented fact (cite a dataset); *that*
broader participation is better is a value we argue for. This is a portfolio artifact — keep the
comparison.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** From memory: name two accountability tools from last lesson (e.g., a free
   press, independent courts, elections, protest, organizing).
2. **Meet the spectrum (10 min).** Read the ways-of-deciding sheet. From left to right, participation
   grows: **one rules** → **a few rule** → **many rule (democracy)** → **broad participation**
   (deliberation, consensus). Each is a real way real societies decide.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Together, fill the comparison chart for democracy: who decides
   (citizens through voting and parties), strengths (equal voice, peaceful transfer of power,
   accountability), limits (majorities can overlook minorities; wealth and media can capture voice;
   participation is unequal).
4. **Compare the alternatives (15 min).** In groups, take one alternative — rule by one, rule by a
   few, or broad participation by consensus — and fill the same chart. Be fair: name its strengths
   as well as its limits, and give a real example.
5. **Weigh (5 min).** Where does democracy protect what the alternatives risk — and where does it
   still fall short of broad participation? What would a *more* democratic arrangement add?
6. **Close (5 min).** Democracy is a practice to improve, not a prize to display. Next: the roots
   of conflict — inequality, fear, scarcity.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the comparison chart pre-filled for democracy and one worked row for an
  alternative.
- **Extension:** Argue whether democracy's value lies in better decisions, in equal voice, or in
  both — and defend your answer with an example.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a strength and a limit of democracy and fairly
  describe one alternative, grounded in a real example?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The completed democracy-versus-alternatives comparison.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: how did our family or community make its last big decision — one person, a few,
a vote, or talking it through? What worked and what didn't? Bring one sentence next time.
