# Lesson 7 — Systemic Injustice: Actions, Rules, Structures

## Summary

Learners meet the idea of **systemic injustice**: unfair outcomes that no single person intended,
produced when **individual actions**, **rules and norms**, and **durable structures** combine. They
learn to trace a case across all three layers, distinguishing "someone did wrong" from "a system is
arranged unfairly."

## Objectives

- Analyze a systemic injustice, tracing how individual actions, rules, and structures combine to
  produce unfair outcomes. _(D09.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Why do some neighborhoods have clean water, shade trees, and safe paths, while others — a short
walk away — do not? No one person decided "let us keep water from those people." Instead, many
small choices, written and unwritten rules, and long-standing arrangements **combined** to produce
a pattern no individual chose. That is what "systemic" means: the unfairness lives in the *system*
— the actions, rules, and structures working together — not only in one person's intent.

## Materials

- Three-layers sheet
- Worked-example case

## Preparation

- Copy the layers sheet.
- Choose a worked-example case that is real and documented but not overwhelming — unequal access to
  clean water, safe housing, or a fair school start — and prepare the three layers to model.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goals: **(1) introduce "systemic injustice"
precisely — an unfair outcome produced by the interaction of (a) individual actions, (b) rules and
norms, and (c) durable structures/institutions, often with no single bad actor; (2) model once,
then guide: trace one case through the three nested layers, then let learners trace another
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, S-011); (3) hold a crucial distinction — systemic analysis
explains a *pattern*, not every individual case, and it assigns responsibility to arrangements,
not blame to whole groups.** The ethics lens: unfairness can be real even when no one intends it.
The egalitarian lens: always ask who the arrangement benefits and who it leaves out, and who has
the power to change it. The global lens: systemic injustice takes different forms in different
societies — do not assume one country's pattern is the world's pattern. Distinguish evidence from
values: *that* unequal access is documented is a factual claim (cite a source); *that* it is unjust
is a value claim we argue for, and there is room for more than one answer. Keep hope attached:
systems are human-made, so they can be re-made (philosophy §12).

## Procedure

1. **Retrieval (5 min).** Last time we formed values. Now: what is the difference between an
   individual wrong and a pattern that keeps repeating? Say it in one sentence.
2. **Meet the three layers (10 min).** Read the three-layers sheet. **Individual actions** sit
   inside **rules and norms** (policies, customs, unwritten expectations), which sit inside
   **structures and institutions** (laws, markets, schools, media, history). Unfair outcomes often
   come from all three working together.
3. **Worked example (15 min).** Together, trace the worked-example case. Step by step: (a) name the
   unfair outcome; (b) list individual actions that feed it; (c) name the rules and norms; (d) name
   the structures. Notice: no single person intended the whole thing.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** In groups, trace a second case through the three layers. For each
   layer, name one thing, and end by saying: *the unfairness lives in the arrangement, not in any
   one hand.*
5. **Name the distinction (5 min).** "Someone did wrong" (an individual wrong) is different from "a
   system is arranged unfairly" (a systemic wrong). Why does the difference matter for how we fix
   it?
6. **Close (5 min).** Next: we trace one injustice in full — cause to outcome — and ask where the
   loop could be broken.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the three layers pre-labeled and one worked entry in each.
- **Extension:** Identify the single change — to a rule, a structure, or a repeated action — most
  likely to interrupt the unfair outcome, and defend your choice.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner trace a case across all three layers and explain how
  they combine, rather than blaming a single actor?
- **Artifact:** The completed layers trace, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: what is one thing our neighborhood or town has that another nearby place
lacks? Whose choices and rules do they think made it that way? Bring one sentence next time.
