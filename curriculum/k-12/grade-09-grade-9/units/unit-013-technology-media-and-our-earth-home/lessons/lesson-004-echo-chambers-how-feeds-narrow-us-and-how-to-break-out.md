# Lesson 4 — Echo Chambers: How Feeds Narrow Us — and How to Break Out

## Summary

Learners explain how an algorithmic feed can become an **echo chamber**: software keeps
showing what a person already engages with, so the same views repeat and opposing views
stay out of sight. They name and plan **concrete ways to break out** — seeking different
sources on purpose.

## Objectives

- Explain how algorithmic feeds and "echo chambers" can narrow what people see, and name
  a way to break out of them. _(D10.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever stood in a small room where your own voice comes back at you, again and
again, so that soon it is all you hear? A feed can do that to your thinking. If it keeps
showing you only what you already click on, the world quietly shrinks to your own
reflection. Breaking out is a skill: stepping toward voices that are not your own.

## Materials

- Echo chamber diagram sheet
- Break-out card set

## Preparation

- Copy the echo chamber diagram sheet and the break-out cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall how a feed ranks by engagement. Today we see what that
  ranking does to a *whole worldview* over time — and how to push back.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a feed that ranks by
engagement tends to show a person more of what they already respond to, which can narrow
what they see into an "echo chamber" — the same views repeated, opposing views out of
sight — and the way to break out is to seek different sources on purpose.** The "filter
bubble" term is from Pariser (S-482); hold it as a *concept to examine*, and note honestly
that scholars debate how strong the effect really is — it is a tendency, not an iron law.
The **technology** lens: personalization is the mechanism. The **ethics** lens: is it fair
to others — and to one's own judgment — to hear only one side? The **egalitarian** lens:
whose voices get left out of a personalized feed, and whose get amplified? The **global**
lens: break-out moves include reading outlets from other countries and languages — the
world is wider than one feed. The **critical-thinking** lens: "my feed is not the world"
is a habit, not a one-time lesson. Model one break-out move explicitly, then let learners
plan their own (S-011). Keep it non-judgmental — everyone's feed narrows; the question is
what we do about it (facilitation.md).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: a feed ranks by engagement. What does it show you
   more of? Today: what that does to your whole picture of the world.
2. **Meet the echo chamber (10 min).** Look at the diagram. Follow the loop: you click
   what you already agree with → the feed shows more of it → you see the same view
   repeated → other views stay out of sight. That narrowing is an **echo chamber**; the
   invisible wall of personalization is sometimes called a **filter bubble**.
3. **Audit your own feed (10 min).** Honestly: how much of what you see repeats what you
   already think? Name one topic where you have *never* seen a view opposed to your own.
4. **Sort the break-out cards (12 min).** In a small group, sort the moves from easiest
   to hardest: follow a source you disagree with; read an outlet from another country;
   ask *who is absent* from this story; check the original source yourself.
5. **Write a break-out plan (8 min).** Choose one move and write a plan: what you will
   read or follow, and how you will notice when your view of the world has widened.
6. **Close (5 min).** A feed shows what it thinks you like — not all there is. Next: how
   to trace a single viral claim back to where it really came from.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-sorted list of break-out moves and ask learners to pick one
  and say why.
- **Extension:** Discuss the limits of the echo-chamber idea itself — what evidence would
  show the effect is strong or weak — and write a short, balanced note.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain the narrowing loop and name one
  concrete break-out move?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The break-out plan, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

This week, find one source that disagrees with what you already think about something you
care about, and read it through. Write one sentence about what it said and one about how
it felt to read.
