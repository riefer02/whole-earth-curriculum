# Lesson 5 — Trace It to Its Source: Evaluating a Viral Claim

## Summary

Learners practice a **four-step method** for evaluating a viral claim: **state the claim,
trace it to its source, check it against independent reporting, and decide with
evidence.** The facilitator models the method once; learners then work it on further
claims, distinguishing evidence from the conclusion it supports.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a viral claim by tracing it to its source and checking it against independent
  reporting. _(D10.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

A story reaches you in seconds — a photo, a headline, a number someone swears is true.
Before you pass it on, you have one job: find out whether it is real. That job has steps,
like following a rumor in a village back to the person who first said it and asking a
neighbor who was there. Tracing a claim to its source and checking it against someone
independent is the same skill, done with care.

## Materials

- Trace-the-claim flowchart sheet
- Claim card set

## Preparation

- Copy the flowchart sheet and the claim cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, recall how a feed narrows and one way to break out. Today we
  practice the break-out skill on a *single claim*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is a **skill lesson**, so use explicit
instruction: **model the four steps once on a worked example, then guide practice, then
independent practice** (S-011). The four steps: **(1) state the claim exactly; (2) trace
it to its source — who posted it, where did it first appear, what is the original
evidence; (3) check it against independent reporting — separate outlets or a fact-checker
that did not come from the same source; (4) decide with evidence — does the claim hold,
or is it distorted, out of context, or false?** This is "lateral reading" — leaving the
claim to check *other* sources rather than only reading the claim's own page (S-394). The
**technology** lens: viral spread is engineered by the same ranking we studied in Lesson 2.
The **ethics** lens: passing on a false claim does real harm; checking before sharing is a
duty to others. The **global** lens: the same claim may be reported differently in
different countries and languages — compare. The **critical-thinking** lens: separate
*what the evidence shows* from *what the headline says* — evidence and interpretation are
different things (philosophy §5). Prepare claim cards that are neutral, age-appropriate,
and genuinely checkable; avoid partisan or traumatic content (facilitation.md).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4: what narrows a feed, and what is one break-out move?
   Today we apply that move to a single claim.
2. **Watch a worked example (12 min).** Follow as the claim "a photo shows a rare animal
   in a big city" is traced: **(a)** state it; **(b)** trace it — where did it first
   appear, who posted it; **(c)** check independent reporting — does a separate outlet or
   fact-checker confirm it; **(d)** decide — holds, distorted, or false. Notice: the
   *source* and the *independent check* matter more than how the claim feels.
3. **Trace a claim with your group (18 min).** Take a claim card. Work the four steps
   together. For each step, write what you found. If you cannot find the original source,
   that itself is evidence — say so.
4. **Decide with evidence (10 min).** Write a one-paragraph verdict: what the claim says,
   what the evidence shows, and your decision — with the reason stated plainly.
5. **Self-check (5 min).** Reread your verdict. Did you *trace* and *corroborate*, or did
   you stop at what looked convincing? One honest sentence.
6. **Close (5 min).** One source is a start; independent sources are the test. Next: we
   turn from the screen to the planet — the greenhouse effect.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-traced first claim with the source and independent check
  filled in; ask learners to state only the decision and reason.
- **Extension:** Trace a claim to its *original* primary source (a study, a document, the
  full video) and evaluate whether the viral version distorted it, citing what you found.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner work the four steps on a new claim and
  state a decision backed by a reason?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The traced claim and the evidence-and-decision paragraph, kept
  in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Choose one claim you saw online this week. Trace it to its source and check it against one
independent outlet. Bring your one-paragraph verdict to the next lesson.
