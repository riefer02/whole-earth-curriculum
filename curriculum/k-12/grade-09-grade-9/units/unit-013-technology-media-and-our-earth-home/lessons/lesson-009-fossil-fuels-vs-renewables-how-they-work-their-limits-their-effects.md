# Lesson 9 — Fossil Fuels vs. Renewables: How They Work, Their Limits, Their Effects

## Summary

Learners compare **fossil fuels and renewable energy sources** across three dimensions —
**how they work, their limits, and their effects on people and the planet.** They see
that each is a **trade-off, not a simple good or bad**, and that the choice shapes the
climate, the air, and people's lives.

## Objectives

- Compare fossil fuels and renewable energy sources in how they work, their limits, and
  their effects on people and the planet. _(D10.S4.09.01)_

## Connection

Every light, every ride, every cooked meal runs on some source of energy — coal dug from
the ground, oil pumped from deep rock, sunlight falling on a panel, wind turning a blade.
Where you live decides which of these you can reach most easily, and each choice carries
costs someone must pay. Comparing them honestly is the first step toward choosing well.

## Materials

- Compare sheet
- Energy source cards

## Preparation

- Copy the compare sheet and the energy source cards.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 6–8, recall the greenhouse effect, the carbon cycle, and the
  ice-albedo feedback loop. Today we see which energy choices feed those systems and which
  do not.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The comparison to land, in three rows: **(1)
How it works —** fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) are ancient buried plant and animal
matter, burned to release stored chemical energy; renewables (solar, wind, hydro,
geothermal) capture energy that is naturally and continuously replenished. **(2) Limits —**
fossil fuels are finite (made over millions of years) and unevenly distributed; renewables
are "flow-limited" and often **intermittent** (the sun sets, the wind stops), so they need
storage or backup. **(3) Effects —** burning fossil fuels emits carbon dioxide (the
greenhouse effect) and local pollutants that harm health; renewables emit far less in
operation but have their own effects (land use, materials, mining for minerals) (S-095;
S-438; S-219). Hold the honest, non-dogmatic frame: **this is a comparison of trade-offs,
not a morality play** — people's energy choices are shaped by cost, geography, and history.
The **environment** and **technology** lenses are central. The **egalitarian** lens: who
bears each source's costs — the pollution and extraction usually land on the poorest and
least powerful, a bridge to Lesson 10. The **ethics** lens: choosing energy is choosing who
and what to protect. The **global** lens: energy mixes differ wildly by country; no single
answer fits everyone. The **critical-thinking** lens: require learners to *state a limit*
for every source they praise — the mark of honest comparison.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 6–8: how does burning fossil fuels change the carbon
   cycle and the climate? Today: what are the choices, exactly?
2. **Meet the sources (12 min).** Use the energy source cards. **Fossil fuels** — coal,
   oil, natural gas — are ancient buried life, burned for heat. **Renewables** — solar,
   wind, hydro, geothermal — capture energy that keeps coming.
3. **Fill the three rows (15 min).** On the compare sheet, fill three rows for each
   column: **how it works**, **its limits**, and **its effects**. For every source you
   praise, write one honest limit; for every source you criticize, write one real benefit.
4. **Weigh the trade-offs (10 min).** Which source is most reliable where you live? Which
   has the lightest effect on air and climate? Which is cheapest? Why is there no single
   "right" answer for the whole world?
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap sheets with a partner. Did each of you state a limit and a
   benefit for every source? Fill in what the other missed.
6. **Close (5 min).** Every source is a trade-off; the choice shapes people's lives. Next:
   who bears the burdens and who reaps the benefits — environmental justice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the compare sheet pre-filled for one source and ask learners to
  complete the rest.
- **Extension:** Evaluate a proposed energy mix for a fictional region against a standard
  of cost, reliability, and fairness, and defend it in one paragraph.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner compare two sources across all three rows,
  stating at least one limit and one effect for each?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed compare sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: where does our energy come from — the grid, a generator, wood, the sun? Write
down the source and one thing it costs, in money or in air, to use.
