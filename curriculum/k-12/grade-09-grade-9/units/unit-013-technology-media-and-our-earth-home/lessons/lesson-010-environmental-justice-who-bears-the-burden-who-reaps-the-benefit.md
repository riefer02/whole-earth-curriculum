# Lesson 10 — Environmental Justice: Who Bears the Burden, Who Reaps the Benefit

## Summary

Learners analyze **environmental justice**: the pattern by which the burdens of
environmental change — pollution, extraction, floods, heat — fall **disproportionately on
low-income, Indigenous, and marginalized communities and on the Global South**, while the
benefits accrue elsewhere. They argue what fairness would require, and **close the unit**
by gathering all eight goals.

## Objectives

- Analyze how the burdens and benefits of environmental change fall unevenly on different
  people (environmental justice). _(D10.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

Where does the smoke from a factory drift, and who lives there? Who enjoys the electricity,
and who breathes the ash? Who loses a home to rising water, and who sold the fuel that
warmed it? The same change can give comfort to some and take safety from others. Seeing
that unevenness clearly — and asking whether it is fair — is environmental justice.

## Materials

- Burden–benefit balance sheet
- Case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the burden–benefit balance sheet and the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, recall the trade-offs of each energy source. Today we ask who
  pays each source's price, and who enjoys its gain — and we gather the whole unit.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **environmental change is
not felt evenly. Its burdens — pollution, resource extraction, floods, heat — fall
disproportionately on low-income, Indigenous, and marginalized communities and on the
Global South, while its benefits (energy, profit, comfort) often accrue to others.
Environmental justice is the analysis of that unevenness and the demand that burdens and
benefits be shared fairly.** The term's formal definition comes from the U.S. EPA
(S-483); the field's scholarship was founded in part by Robert Bullard's documentation of
hazardous-waste siting in Black communities (S-484); the *global* unevenness is documented
by the IPCC (S-005). Use at least one **non-U.S. / global** case (e.g., a small island
state facing sea-level rise it did little to cause; a community near extraction in the
Global South) so the lens is planetary, not one nation's story. The **egalitarian** lens is
the heart here. The **ethics** lens: fairness is a *values* question — distinguish the
documented *facts* of unevenness from the *judgment* that it is unjust (philosophy §5). The
**environment** lens: the burdens are real, measurable harms. The **global** lens: the
pattern repeats worldwide. End on agency, not despair — stewardship is something learners
can practice (facilitation.md). Close with the unit's retrieval pass: all eight goals
recalled in one breath (S-039).

**Indigenous peoples as stewards and defenders, not only victims.** Always teach the
Standing Rock case (S-485) alongside the burden cases: the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe —
Lakota and Dakota people of what is now North and South Dakota — defended the Missouri
River against the Dakota Access Pipeline, grounding the 2016–2017 "water protector"
gatherings in Mní wičhóni ("water is life"). Frame it as a case of *agency and
stewardship*: here Indigenous peoples are **knowledge-holders** (their relationship to the
river, its water, and treaty lands), **stewards** (protecting water for future
generations), and **defenders** (organizing and acting) — not passive victims. This
corrects the flattening that would otherwise leave Indigenous peoples appearing only as
burden-bearers, and it models stewardship as something learners can themselves practice.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9: name one trade-off of fossil fuels and one of
   renewables. Today: who pays each price, and who gains.
2. **Meet environmental justice (10 min).** Read the definition: **environmental justice**
   is the fair treatment of all people in environmental decisions — and the honest study of
   how burdens and benefits actually fall. They do not fall evenly: those who contributed
   least to a problem often carry the most of it.
3. **Analyze cases (18 min).** Use the case cards. Analyze at least the Indigenous
   stewardship case — the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe defending the Missouri River (Mní
   wičhóni, "water is life") against the Dakota Access Pipeline — and one other (a
   polluting site near a low-income community, sea-level rise on a small island state, or
   a clean-energy project's local costs). For each, fill the balance sheet: *who bears the
   burden*, *who reaps the benefit*, and *who is acting to protect what — and how*.
4. **Argue fairness (12 min).** For one case, write one paragraph: what is unfair about the
   pattern, and what would a fairer arrangement require — and who would have to give
   something up for it?
5. **Gather the unit (8 min).** In one breath, recall all eight goals: algorithms and
   feeds; who controls the platform; echo chambers and breaking out; tracing a viral claim;
   the greenhouse effect; the carbon cycle; feedback loops; energy and environmental
   justice. Which one will you carry with you?
6. **Close (5 min).** The screen and the Earth are read with the same habit: critical
   judgment, neither worship nor fear — and a fairer sharing of burdens and benefits.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the balance sheet pre-filled for one case and ask learners to fill
  the second.
- **Extension:** Compare a local and a global case, and propose one fairer arrangement,
  naming its real costs and who would bear them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify who bears the burden and who reaps
  the benefit in one case, and state what fairness would require?
- **Portfolio artifact (summative for the unit):** The completed balance sheet and fairness
  paragraph, added to the unit's portfolio of eight artifacts.

## Home connection

Ask at home: in our community, who lives nearest the road, the factory, the waste site, or
the flood path — and who does not? Write down one thing your family might do, however
small, to share a burden or a benefit more fairly.
