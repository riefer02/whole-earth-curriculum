# Lesson 3 — Who Controls the Platform? Power Over Users

## Summary

Learners analyze **who controls a digital platform** by naming three levers the
controller holds — **setting the rules, tuning the ranking, and collecting the data** —
and trace what that power lets them decide about users. They see that control is power,
and that **ownership shapes who holds it.**

## Objectives

- Analyze who controls a digital platform and what power that control gives them over
  users. _(D10.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

Every shared space has someone who decides its rules — who may enter a market, how a
well is shared, what may be said in a meeting hall. A digital platform is a shared space
too, but its rules, its ordering, and its records are all set by whoever controls the
software. Knowing who that is — and what they can decide without asking you — is knowing
where the power sits.

## Materials

- Platform power sheet
- Platform case cards

## Preparation

- Copy the platform power sheet and the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, recall the algorithm's steps (signals → rank → slice). Today
  we ask who sets those steps — and what else they control.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **whoever controls a
platform holds three levers — the rules (terms of service and moderation), the ranking
(the algorithm), and the data (what is collected and how it is used) — and those levers
add up to power over users: what they see, what they may post, and what is known about
them.** This is an *analysis* of how power works, not a verdict that all platforms are
bad. The **technology** lens: control is built into the software and the business model.
The **egalitarian** lens: ask who holds power and who has none — users rarely set the
rules they live under. The **ethics** lens: is power this concentrated fair, and what
would make it accountable? The **critical-thinking** lens: "who controls this?" is a
question to ask of every tool, not only screens. Contrast at least one **community-run or
cooperatively owned** platform with a for-profit one so learners see that ownership is a
choice, not a law of nature (S-281). Keep the treatment balanced — platforms also give
real value (connection, voice, knowledge) — and hold questions open (philosophy §11).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what are the algorithm's three steps? Today we ask
   *who sets them* — and what else they control.
2. **Meet the three levers (10 min).** Look at the platform power sheet. Whoever controls
   a platform holds three levers: **rules** (what is allowed, who is removed), **ranking**
   (what rises, what is buried), and **data** (what is collected, what it is used for).
3. **Analyze two platforms (15 min).** Read the case cards — one global for-profit social
   network and one community-run or cooperative service. For each, fill the sheet: *who*
   holds each lever, and *what can they decide without asking users?*
4. **Trace the power (12 min).** For one platform, write one sentence on each: what the
   controller can decide about *what users see*, *what users may post*, and *what is known
   about users*.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap sheets with a partner. Did you both find the same three
   levers? Where did you differ?
6. **Close (5 min).** Control is power, and ownership decides who holds it. Next: what
   happens when that ranking narrows what any one person sees — the echo chamber.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the power sheet pre-filled for one lever and ask learners to fill
  the other two.
- **Extension:** Compare a for-profit platform with a cooperative one and argue how the
  change of ownership changes who is accountable, naming your reasoning.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the three levers and state one power
  each gives the controller over users?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed platform power sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask at home: who owns the apps and platforms our family uses most, and have we ever read
their rules? Write down one rule you discover that surprised you.
