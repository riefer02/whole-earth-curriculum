# Lesson 1 — How Do We Know the Climate Is Changing?

## Summary

Learners do what scientists actually do: look at data, build a claim that the data can
support, and say honestly what the data cannot tell us. Then they turn the same
critical eye on the *sources* of climate information, learning to separate evidence
from assertion and reliability from bias.

## Objectives

- Construct an evidence-based explanation that links data to a claim and states its
  limits and uncertainties. *(D06.S1.09.01)*
- Critically evaluate a source of scientific information for reliability, bias, and
  conflicts of interest. *(D06.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

Every day you hear claims about the climate — from the news, from people you know,
from social media. Some are careful and some are not, and it is not always obvious
which is which. This lesson is not about telling you what to believe; it is about
giving you the two skills that let you decide for yourself: *reading evidence* and
*judging sources*. Those two skills transfer to every important question you will
ever face.

## Materials

- A graph of global average temperature over time (1880–present)
- A graph of atmospheric CO₂ over time (ice-core + direct measurement)
- Two short articles about climate from different sources

## Preparation

- Print the two graphs (or draw them), large enough to read together.
- Choose two real articles that differ clearly in source reliability or sponsorship.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is about **method, not conclusion**. Do not lead learners to a foregone
answer; let the data and the source questions do the work. The goal is that a learner
can *state the claim the data supports* and *name the limits* ("this shows a trend; it
does not, by itself, prove every cause"). When sources are discussed, stay neutral —
the point is to ask "how do you know?", never to mock any source. Model saying "I
don't know that" and "the evidence is uncertain there." For learners distressed by
climate content, keep the focus on tools and agency; see `docs/facilitation.md`.

## Procedure

1. **Look at the data (10 min).** Study the two graphs. Describe what you actually
   see: what is the overall direction of each line? (Not "why" — just "what.")
2. **Build a claim (15 min).** Write one sentence the data can support: "The data show
   that global average temperature has ___ since ___, and that atmospheric CO₂ has ___
   over the same period." Then write one thing the data does *not* show on its own.
3. **State the limits (10 min).** What is uncertain here? Why does a graph of
   temperature not automatically tell you the *cause*? What else would you need to
   connect the two trends?
4. **Judge the sources (15 min).** For each article, answer three questions: *Who made
   it? How do they know? What do they stand to gain or lose?* Which article would you
   trust more, and why?
5. **Close (5 min).** Science earns trust by showing its evidence and its limits. A
   claim without evidence is an assertion; a claim without limits is a red flag. Bring
   these two habits to everything you read.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence frames for the claim and the three source questions.
- **Extension:** Find the original dataset behind one article and check whether the
  article represents it faithfully.

## Assessment

- **Formative:** Can the learner state a claim the data supports and name one limit?
- **Summative/performance:** The written claim–data–limits note, and a one-paragraph
  source evaluation naming reliability, bias, and any conflict of interest.

## Home connection

Show someone at home one of the graphs and your claim. Ask where they get their
information about the climate, and practice the three source questions together.
