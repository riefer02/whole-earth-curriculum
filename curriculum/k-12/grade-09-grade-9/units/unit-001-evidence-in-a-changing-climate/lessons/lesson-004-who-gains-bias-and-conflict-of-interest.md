# Lesson 4 — Who Gains? Bias and Conflict of Interest in a Source

## Summary

Learners distinguish bias from conflict of interest, then apply four questions — who
made it, who paid, what is left out, who gains — to two real sources with disclosed
interests. They learn that an interest does not make a claim false; it tells a reader
what to check.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate a source of scientific information for reliability, bias, and
  conflicts of interest. *(D06.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

A friend tells you the phone repair shop on the corner is the best in town — and
mentions, halfway through, that their cousin owns it. The recommendation may still be
true, but you now know where to check: the reviews, the guarantee, the price. A
source's interest is not a lie detector; it is a map of where to look, and it works
the same on a stallholder's sign, a political flyer, and a climate report.

## Materials

- **Standard:** the two source cards below, the four-question frame, and one real
  article that states its funding.
- **Low-tech:** the quotations copied onto a board, and a hand-drawn four-question
  grid.
- **Enriched:** internet access to trace a claim to its original report, and a full
  competing-interests statement.

## Preparation

- Print the two cards and the four-question frame.
- Choose one real article whose funding or competing-interests line is visible and
  short. Do not choose one that attacks a named local person or group.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, what a range means; from Lesson 3, who measured the
  sea-level numbers and who paid.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Curious and unshockable. You are teaching a habit of checking, not a
scandal.

**The facts to land.**

1. **Bias** is a slant that pushes a claim one way; science guards against it with
   sampling, blinding, and repetition (S-250, S-242).
2. A **conflict of interest** is when the people making a claim stand to gain if it is
   believed. WHO names one: "There is a fundamental and irreconcilable conflict
   between the tobacco industry's interests and public health interests" (S-374).
3. A conflict of interest **does not prove a claim false**. It tells you what to
   check; peer review and replication are filters, not guarantees (S-489).
4. **No source is interest-free** — not WHO, and not the IPCC, whose summary
   governments approve. The test is disclosure, method, and scrutiny.

**Don't overshoot.** Never let "has a conflict of interest" become "is lying"; never
imply a public-interest mandate makes a source right.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical-thinking:* an interest is a question, not a verdict.
- *Ethics:* disclosing an interest is honest; hiding one removes the reader's chance
  to judge.
- *Egalitarianism:* funding is unequal, and a well-resourced source is not truer for
  it.
- *Global:* the four questions work on a ministry, a journal, or a village notice.

**The skill.** The four-question frame plus lateral reading — leave the document and
see what independent sources say (S-151, S-394).

**Access.** Print the cards large, read them aloud, accept an oral evaluation.

**Safety floor.** Keep critique on documents and institutions. Never put a learner's
family, job, faith, or news source on trial.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: who measured the sea-level numbers, and how
   would you find out who paid? From Lesson 2: what does a range tell you that a single
   number does not? Today you judge what those interests mean — and add a fourth
   question: *who gains?*

2. **Two words, precisely (10 min).** Write both definitions in your own words.
   **Bias** is a slant that pushes a claim one way — often unintentionally, through
   who was sampled, what was measured, or which results were reported (S-250, S-242).
   A **conflict of interest** is when the people making a claim stand to gain if you
   believe it. Then answer: does a conflict of interest prove the claim is false? (No.
   It raises the bar for evidence.)

3. **Card A — read a disclosed conflict (10 min).** Read the quotation.

   > "There is a fundamental and irreconcilable conflict between the tobacco
   > industry's interests and public health interests. … The industry is working for
   > profit." — WHO, *Tobacco and nicotine* fact sheet (S-374)

   Answer the four questions. Who made it? What is WHO's mandate? What does WHO stand
   to gain or lose? Does the fact sheet show evidence a reader could check? Then say
   the hard part out loud: WHO has an interest too. Naming it does not dismiss the
   claim; it tells you what to verify.

4. **Card B — the interest you might not notice (10 min).** Read the second quotation.

   > "Global mean sea level increased by 0.20 [0.15 to 0.25] m between 1901 and
   > 2018." — IPCC, *Sixth Assessment Report*, Working Group I Summary for
   > Policymakers (S-005)

   The IPCC is not a single author. Its summaries are approved line by line by member
   governments, which is a check and also a place where interests press (S-005). Who
   gains if a summary sounds stronger? Who gains if it sounds weaker? What would you
   read to find out? (The underlying chapters and the review comments.)

5. **Lateral reading (10 min).** Take one climate claim you have met this week — in a
   video, a conversation, a headline. Do not read further down the page. Instead,
   leave it: search for the original report or study, and for independent coverage
   (S-394). Write the trail: claim → where it appeared → the original → who funded
   the original.

6. **Write the evaluation (10 min).** One paragraph, four sentences: the strongest
   reason to trust this source; the interest at stake; one thing it leaves out or
   does not check; the next thing you would check. End with one line a reader could
   act on.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** answer two questions instead of four, with sentence starters — "The
  interest at stake is ___ because ___."
- **Extension:** find a source whose funding line is missing, and explain what a
  reader loses when that line is absent. Then find the same claim in a source with
  the opposite interest and compare what each omits.

## Assessment

- **Formative:** the learner names the interest at stake in each card and states
  clearly that an interest is not proof of falsehood.
- **Portfolio artifact:** the four-sentence evaluation, kept for the capstone in
  Lesson 5.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home where they get their news about a topic they care about, and who
pays for that source. Ask the four questions together, about a source you both use —
not to score a point, but to see what changes when you know who is paying.
