# Lesson 2 — Uncertainty Is Not Noise: Best Estimates and Error Bars

## Summary

Learners read the brackets in a real climate table — a best estimate and a likely
range — and draw them as error bars. They learn that a range is information, not a
flaw, and they use overlap to decide which differences are bigger than the
uncertainty and which are not.

## Objectives

- Construct an evidence-based explanation that links data to a claim and states its
  limits and uncertainties. *(D06.S1.09.01)*
- Read a best estimate with its range, and judge whether a difference is larger than
  the uncertainty. *(D06.S1.09.01)*

## Connection

Three friends time the same race and get 12.4, 12.6, and 12.5 seconds, so you say
"about 12.5" — the spread is part of the answer. A tailor who measures your sleeve
twice and gets 61 cm and 62 cm does not throw the tape away; the two readings show how
precisely the sleeve can be cut. A warming of 1.09 °C comes with the same kind of
bracket, and it tells you how firmly the evidence pins the number down.

## Materials

- **Standard:** the three-row table, the error-bar chart, a ruler, and grid paper.
- **Low-tech:** one drawn axis on a board; crosses and lines for estimates and
  ranges.
- **Enriched:** a spreadsheet that plots the three bars, or the IPCC summary itself.

## Preparation

- Print the table and chart, or draw the axis large enough for the group to read.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, ask for one claim the graphs supported and one limit the
  learner named.
- Decide how you will say the bracket aloud: "about 1.09, plausibly between 0.95 and
  1.20."

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Plain and reassuring. A range is a sign of care, not of confusion.

**The facts to land.**

1. **Every measurement carries uncertainty** — from the instrument, the method, and
   the averaging (NIST, S-249).
2. A climate figure is reported as a **best estimate** and a **likely range**: 1.09
   [0.95–1.20] °C for 2011–2020 against 1850–1900 (IPCC, S-005).
3. When two ranges **do not overlap**, the difference is larger than the stated
   uncertainty. Land (1.34–1.83) and ocean (0.68–1.01) do not overlap.
4. A range is **not a guarantee**, and it does not cover every possible mistake — only
   the uncertainty the analysts could quantify.

**Don't overshoot.** Do not present the bracket as "the true number is certainly in
here", and do not let "uncertain" slide into "we know nothing". The estimate is the
finding; the range is its precision.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical-thinking:* overlap is a test you can run, not a feeling.
- *Technology:* better instruments and more stations narrow a range.
- *Global:* the global figure pools measurements from many countries.
- *Environment:* a global range can hide places changing much faster.

**The skill.** Model one bar, guide the second, release the third (S-011).

**Access.** Offer a pre-drawn axis and enlarged type; accept a spoken answer.

**Safety floor.** If the numbers feel frightening, say so plainly, then return to what
the range lets a person do: judge a claim, and ask a sharper question.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what claim did the temperature and CO₂ graphs
   support, and what limit did you name? Today you ask a second question of every
   number: *how sure is it?*

2. **Read the brackets (8 min).** Look at the table. The first number is the best
   estimate; the bracket is the likely range. Say the first row aloud in a full
   sentence. Then answer: is the bracket a mistake? Is the true value *certain* to be
   inside it? (No — it is the analysts' stated confidence, not a guarantee. Repeated
   readings can shrink random error, but they do not remove a shared bias — S-242.)

   | Measure (2011–2020 vs 1850–1900) | Best estimate (°C) | Likely range (°C) |
   |---|---|---|
   | Global surface | 1.09 | 0.95 to 1.20 |
   | Land | 1.59 | 1.34 to 1.83 |
   | Ocean | 0.88 | 0.68 to 1.01 |

   Source: IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I Summary for Policymakers
   (S-005).

3. **Worked example — draw one bar (12 min).** Watch the global row being drawn.
   Rule an axis from 0 to 2 °C. Mark 1.09 with a short vertical line. Draw a thick
   horizontal bar from 0.95 to 1.20 through it. Label it. Your bar now carries both
   pieces of information at once: where the estimate sits, and how wide the
   uncertainty is.

4. **Draw the other two (8 min).** Add land and ocean to the same axis, with their
   numbers. Check your drawing against the supplied chart: are your bars in the right
   places and the right widths?

5. **Interrogate (10 min).** Answer in writing, then compare with a partner:
   - Do the land and ocean bars overlap? If not, what does that let you say?
   - Does the global range come anywhere near zero? What does that make very
     unlikely?
   - Which claim is safer: "warming is 1.09 °C" or "warming is about 1.1 °C, plausibly
     between 0.95 and 1.2 °C"?
   - What does the range *not* cover? (A thermometer that is biased the same way
     everywhere; a place changing faster than the global mean.)

6. **Write it (7 min).** Two sentences: (a) one claim with its range; (b) one
   sentence beginning "This range does not mean…".

7. **Close (5 min).** Tell a partner why "1.09" without the bracket overstates the
   precision of what we know.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** supply the axis and the first bar; ask for one claim and one limit
  rather than four answers.
- **Extension:** the report also gives observed warming for 2010–2019 as 1.06
  [0.88–1.21] °C and human-caused warming as 1.07 °C with a likely range of 0.8–1.3
  °C. Why are these three numbers close but not identical? What is each one trying to
  measure?

## Assessment

- **Formative:** the learner places an estimate and its range correctly on an axis,
  and says whether two ranges overlap.
- **Portfolio artifact:** the two-sentence claim with its range, and the sentence
  naming what the range does not mean, kept for Lesson 5.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for a measurement they make often — a recipe, a journey time, a
sleeve length. Ask how much it varies. Agree on a best estimate and a range, and say
which is more useful for planning.
