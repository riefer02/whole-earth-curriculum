# Lesson 5 — The Explanation You Can Defend: Claim, Data, Limits

## Summary

Learners write a complete evidence-based explanation — claim, data, reasoning, limits,
and a source note — using the datasets they have already interrogated. A structured
peer check and one revision turn a first draft into something they can defend.

## Objectives

- Construct an evidence-based explanation that links data to a claim and states its
  limits and uncertainties. *(D06.S1.09.01)*
- Critically evaluate a source of scientific information for reliability, bias, and
  conflicts of interest. *(D06.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

When someone asks "how do you know?", a strong answer has a shape: what you claim,
which numbers carry the claim, why they bear on it, where the answer stops being
reliable, and who produced the evidence. A mechanic who says "the tyre is worn — here
is the tread depth, it is below the safe line, but I cannot tell you how long the
other three will last" is easier to trust than one who says "you need new tyres".
The same five parts make a climate explanation checkable by someone who was not in
the room.

## Materials

- **Standard:** the capstone frame sheet, the data sheet below, and the peer-check
  checklist.
- **Low-tech:** paper folded into five labelled bands, and a spoken explanation.
- **Enriched:** a shared document for drafting and revising, or a second dataset for
  the same question.

## Preparation

- Print the frame and the data sheet.
- Retrieval: have Lessons 2–4 notes to hand; learners will reuse them.
- Decide which prompt (A or B) you will model aloud.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Serious and encouraging — this is the unit's performance, not a quiz.

**The facts to land.**

1. An evidence-based explanation has five parts: **claim, data, reasoning, limits,
   source**.
2. The **data** must be the numbers actually used, with units and period — not a
   gesture at "the graph".
3. The **reasoning** is the link: why these numbers support this claim. Here, rate ×
   years, and ranges that do not overlap.
4. The **limits** are part of the claim's strength, not a disclaimer bolted on. A
   range is not a guarantee, and a global mean hides local change.
5. The **source note** says who produced the evidence and what interest they carry
   (S-489, S-151).

**Don't overshoot.** Do not require a verdict on whether climate change is "real".
The task is whether *this explanation* earns trust. A cautious explanation is not a
weak one.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical-thinking:* a stated limit is evidence of care, not a hole.
- *Egalitarianism:* the same evidence must serve people in very different places.
- *Environment:* every global number stands in for many local worlds.
- *Global:* the data are pooled from many countries; the reasoning must respect that.

**The skill.** Retrieval first, then a modelled example, then independent writing and
peer feedback (S-039, S-011).

**Access.** Offer sentence starters, a scribe, or a recording; accept a labelled
diagram in place of prose.

**Safety floor.** If an explanation turns to despair ("nothing can be done"),
acknowledge it and redirect to a question the evidence can answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall, without notes first (6 min).** Write down three things from this unit:
   the difference between a rate and a total; what a likely range means; one question
   to ask about a source's interest. Then check your notes. Retrieving before
   rereading is what makes the ideas stick (S-039).

2. **See one built (12 min).** Watch the frame being filled for the sea-level
   question. *Claim:* sea level rose about 0.20 m from 1901 to 2018, and the rate
   roughly tripled. *Data:* 1.3, 1.9, and 3.7 mm per year over 71, 35, and 12 years;
   total 0.20 m, likely 0.15–0.25 m. *Reasoning:* rate × years recovers the total, and
   the later rate is outside the earlier range. *Limits:* a global mean hides local
   change; a range is not a guarantee; the table alone does not name a cause.
   *Source:* IPCC, an intergovernmental assessment whose summary governments approve.
   Tell the class which part is hardest to write, and why.

3. **Choose your question and draft (18 min).** Pick **A** or **B** and fill all five
   parts. Write in full sentences, one part at a time. Every number you use carries a
   range, and the range is part of the evidence, not a footnote (NIST, S-249). Use
   only numbers from the data sheet; if you need a number that is not there, say so in
   your limits.

   **A. How much has the climate warmed, and how sure are we?**
   Global surface 1.09 °C, likely 0.95–1.20; land 1.59 °C, likely 1.34–1.83; ocean
   0.88 °C, likely 0.68–1.01 (2011–2020 vs 1850–1900).

   **B. Is sea-level rise speeding up?**
   1.3 mm/yr (1901–1971), 1.9 mm/yr (1971–2006), 3.7 mm/yr (2006–2018); total 0.20 m,
   likely 0.15–0.25 m (1901–2018).

   Both rows are from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group I Summary for
   Policymakers (S-005).

4. **Peer check (8 min).** Swap with a partner and answer five yes-or-no questions:
   Is there a claim? Are the numbers named with units and period? Is the reasoning a
   link rather than a repeat? Is at least one limit specific to this claim? Does the
   source note name who made the evidence and one interest at stake? Give one
   concrete suggestion, not a grade.

5. **Revise once (6 min).** Make the single improvement that would most strengthen the
   explanation. Usually it is a sharper limit or a more precise data sentence.

6. **Submit and close (5 min).** Hand in the explanation with its source note. Then
   answer in one line: what makes this explanation worth trusting? Keep the answer
   with your work.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** provide sentence starters for each part and a partly completed frame;
  accept a labelled diagram with a spoken explanation.
- **Extension:** add a paragraph answering a counterclaim ("someone says the rise
  paused after 1998 — what does your data say, and what would you need to check?"),
  and name the evidence that would weaken your explanation.

## Assessment

- **Summative/performance:** the written explanation is scored against the five
  parts, with the limits and source note weighted as heavily as the claim.
- **Portfolio:** the final explanation, the peer-check sheet, and the revision are
  kept together so growth is visible.

## Home connection

Read your explanation to someone at home and ask them one question: "What would you
want to check before you believed this?" Add their question to your limits if it is
not already there.
