# Lesson 3 — Where the Numbers Come From: Who Measured, Who Funded

## Summary

Learners trace a published sea-level number back through its chain — instrument,
station network, dataset, assessment, summary — and ask who worked at each link and
who paid. They discover that a rate is a derived number, not something an instrument
reads off, and that a gap in the chain is a place to look, not a verdict.

## Objectives

- Critically evaluate a source of scientific information for reliability, bias, and
  conflicts of interest. *(D06.S1.09.02)*
- Trace a published number to the instruments, people, and funding behind it.
  *(D06.S1.09.02)*

## Connection

A price of 3.70 a kilo at a market is not a fact about the world; it is the end of a
chain — a farmer, a scale, a wholesaler, a stallholder — and each link can be asked
who, how, and who paid. A number in a climate report has the same kind of chain behind
it, only longer: an instrument, a station, a dataset, an assessment, a summary. Once
you can see the chain, you know which link to inspect when a number surprises you.

## Materials

- **Standard:** the sea-level table, the chain diagram, and one article with a funding
  line.
- **Low-tech:** the chain drawn on a board and copied by hand.
- **Enriched:** a dataset's methods page, or a local gauge or weather-station record.

## Preparation

- Print the table and the chain diagram.
- Choose one real article whose funding line is short and visible.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, what a likely range means; from Lesson 1, the three
  source questions.

## Facilitator note

**Tone.** Detective-like and unhurried. This is tracing, not accusing.

**The facts to land.**

1. A published number is the end of a chain: **instrument → station network →
   dataset → assessment → summary**.
2. Sea-level rates come from **tide gauges** (water against a coast, where the land
   can also move) and **satellite altimetry** (height from orbit, since the 1990s);
   the IPCC record combines them (S-005).
3. A **rate is derived**: measurements over time, divided by time. Multiplying back
   recovers the total — 1.3 × 71, 1.9 × 35, 3.7 × 12 ≈ 203 mm, the reported 0.20 m
   (S-005).
4. **Funding is part of provenance.** Ask who services a gauge for a century, who
   employs the analysts, and who approves the summary — governments, for the IPCC
   (S-005). Peer review is one link, not a guarantee (S-489).

**Don't overshoot.** A gap in the chain is not evidence of a conspiracy. Most gaps are
boring: the detail lives in a chapter or a methods page.

**The lenses, briefly.**

- *Critical-thinking:* every number has an address.
- *Technology:* a gauge and an altimeter see different things.
- *Global:* the record is assembled from many countries' instruments.
- *Egalitarianism:* ask which coasts have long records and which do not.

**The skill.** A five-link provenance trail, modelled once, then traced independently
(S-011).

**Access.** Read each link aloud; accept a spoken trail.

**Safety floor.** Keep "who paid" a question about institutions, never an accusation
against a named scientist or a learner's family.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 2: what does a likely range tell you that a single
   number does not? From Lesson 1: name the three source questions. Today you add the
   step that comes before all of them: *where did this number come from?*

2. **A rate is made, not found (12 min).** Read the table. The 3.7 mm per year is not
   a reading on a dial; it is change divided by time. Check that for yourself: multiply
   each rate by its period, then add the three totals. Do you get about 203 mm, which
   is the reported 0.20 m (S-005)? Write one sentence saying what was measured and what
   was calculated.

   | Period | Rate (mm per year) | Likely range (mm per year) | Length (years) |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 1901–1971 | 1.3 | 0.6 to 2.1 | 71 |
   | 1971–2006 | 1.9 | 0.8 to 2.9 | 35 |
   | 2006–2018 | 3.7 | 3.2 to 4.2 | 12 |

   Total for 1901–2018: **0.20 m**, likely 0.15 to 0.25 m (S-005).

3. **Follow the chain (12 min).** Look at the diagram. Each box is a link. Annotate
   each one with two things: **who** works there, and **how** the number changes at
   that link. At the instrument link, name what a tide gauge measures at a coast and
   what a satellite altimeter measures from orbit; the IPCC's assessment describes the
   record as built from both (S-005). At the dataset link, note that this is a global
   record built from many stations, not one reading — averaging reduces random error
   but not a bias shared by every instrument (S-242).

4. **Who paid (10 min).** For each link, ask who pays. A gauge needs someone to
   service it for a century; a dataset needs analysts; a summary needs reviewers and a
   secretariat. Then find the funding line in your article. Does knowing who paid
   change what you would check? Does it, on its own, tell you the claim is false?
   (No — it tells you which link to inspect.)

5. **Trace one number (10 min).** Choose either the 0.20 m total or one warming figure
   from Lesson 2. Write its trail as five arrows, from instrument to summary. Mark one
   place where the summary does not show you enough, and name what you would open next
   — the underlying chapter, the dataset page, or the methods note.

6. **Close (6 min).** Write one sentence in the form: "This number exists because ___
   measured ___, ___ combined it, and ___ paid." Then say why a gap in the trail is a
   place to look rather than a reason to stop.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** give the five links pre-labelled and ask for who at two of them; accept
  the trail as a labelled diagram.
- **Extension:** compare a tide-gauge record with a satellite record for the same
  coast. What can each see, and what can neither see? Which would you trust for a
  hundred-year trend, and why?

## Assessment

- **Formative:** the learner names at least three links in the chain, one funder, and
  one gap worth opening.
- **Portfolio artifact:** the five-arrow provenance trail, kept for the capstone in
  Lesson 5.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home who pays for a number they rely on — a water meter, a shop scale,
a phone's step count. Trace it back one link: who calibrates it, and who would notice
if it drifted?
