# Lesson 5 — Empathy Across Distance, Media, and Technology

## Summary

Learners examine how **empathy is narrowed or widened** by distance, numbers, media, and technology — the
identifiable-victim effect, psychic numbing, framing, and compassion fatigue — and choose one practical
counter-move for their own media habits.

## Objectives

- Examine how empathy can be limited or expanded by distance, media, and technology. _(D02.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

Here is a strange, ordinary fact: one child's face on a screen can move us to tears, while a headline about a
million people affected by a flood scrolls past and barely registers. It is not that we are bad people; it is
that empathy is a near-sighted tool. It works best up close, on one named person with a story. Distance,
numbers, and an endless feed all dim it. Understanding this — and how media and technology steer it — is the
first step to choosing where your care actually goes. Today you take that step.

## Materials

- Empathy-radius diagram
- Media-framing examples

## Preparation

- Copy the diagram and framing examples.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall how stereotypes can block empathy (D02.S2.08.02); from Grade 7, recall how
  online and offline communities differ.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) empathy is naturally "parochial" — it
runs strongest toward people near us, who look like us, and toward single identifiable victims, not crowds;
this is the "identifiable victim effect" (S-414); (2) as the number of victims grows, feeling can actually
numb — "psychic numbing" — one death is a tragedy, a million is a statistic; (3) media and technology can
widen empathy (a single story, a face, a shared place can make a faraway person real) or narrow it (endless
feeds, dehumanizing frames, and echo chambers that only show us "us"); (4) sustained exposure to others'
suffering can produce "compassion fatigue" — not because we are cold, but because we are tired; (5) because
we know this, we can choose counter-moves.** This is a *concept*, so use inquiry and discussion more than
drill (philosophy §13). The critical-thinking lens: the same event can be framed as a person or a number, and
the frame changes how we feel — so ask who chose the frame. The egalitarian lens: whose suffering is shown as
a face and whose is shown as a statistic is often about power and proximity, not about whose suffering
matters more. The global lens: media from different places frame faraway crises differently, and a global
citizen learns to notice the frame. The technology lens is the heart here: feeds and platforms shape what we
see, and therefore what we feel (S-226). Hold this honestly but not hopelessly — end with agency, the
counter-move the learner can choose.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: how can a stereotype block empathy? Today we widen the lens — how does
   *distance* and *media* shape what we feel?
2. **Meet the near-sightedness (12 min).** Empathy runs strongest up close. Study the diagram: near circles
   (me, my people) feel strongest; far circles (strangers, millions) feel faint. Two effects explain it: the
   **identifiable victim** effect — one named person with a face and story moves us far more than a crowd —
   and **psychic numbing** — as the number grows, feeling flattens (S-414).
3. **Meet the media lens (12 min).** Media and technology can widen empathy — one story, one face can make a
   faraway person real — or narrow it — a dehumanizing frame, a statistic, or an echo chamber that shows only
   "us" (S-226). Compare the two framing examples: the same event told as one named person versus as a
   number. How does each make you feel, and why?
4. **Name the tiredness (5 min).** Constant exposure to others' suffering can bring **compassion fatigue** —
   not coldness, but exhaustion. It is a signal to rest and to choose, not a reason to give up.
5. **Choose a counter-move (12 min).** Pick one: seek one name and story when a big number goes numb; vary
   your feed to include voices unlike your own; take a break when compassion fatigue hits; or check who framed
   a story and why. Write your counter-move and when you will use it.
6. **Close (4 min).** Empathy is near-sighted — but knowing that lets us aim it on purpose.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort a list of items into "widens empathy" and "narrows empathy" using the diagram.
- **Extension:** Research the identifiable-victim effect or psychic numbing, and design one change to a real
  feed or news habit that would counter it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain how distance, numbers, and media shape empathy, and
  name a counter-move?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The annotated empathy-radius diagram and the written counter-move, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

For one day, notice when a big number goes numb or a face moves you. Try your counter-move once, and note what
changed.
