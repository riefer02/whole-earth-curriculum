# Lesson 7 — Nationalism, Exclusion, and In-Group Loyalty

## Summary

Learners analyze how **in-group loyalty and nationalism** can both **build** community (belonging, shared
purpose, liberation) and **limit** it (exclusion, dehumanization, conflict), and practice asking where the
line between pride and exclusion lies.

## Objectives

- Analyze how nationalism, exclusion, and in-group loyalty can both build and limit community.
  _(D02.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

You know the warmth of "us." It might be your team, your neighborhood, your language community, or your
country — the feeling of being on the inside with people who share something with you. That warmth is real
and good; it is how people have fed one another through famines and won their freedom. But "us" always has an
edge: somewhere, a line is drawn, and someone is left on the other side of it. The same loyalty that builds
can also exclude, dehumanize, and burn. Today you look honestly at both faces of "us."

## Materials

- In-group/out-group diagram
- Case-study cards

## Preparation

- Copy the diagram and case-study cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall how belonging can include some and exclude others (D02.S3.06.01); from
  Grade 8, recall how power and resources are shared in a community.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **(1) in-group loyalty is a deep human
tendency — even arbitrary group labels produce favoritism toward "us" (the minimal-group experiments,
S-232); (2) this loyalty *builds* community: belonging, trust, shared purpose, and the solidarity that wins
liberation; (3) it also *limits* community: an out-group is excluded, stereotyped, and sometimes dehumanized;
(4) nationalism is a modern form of in-group loyalty built around an "imagined community" — a nation whose
members will never meet but imagine themselves as one (S-415); (5) the same nationalism can liberate a
colonized people *and* become a new exclusion — so the honest question is not "is loyalty good or bad?" but
"who does this 'us' include, who does it leave out, and at what cost?"** This is a *concept* with real moral
weight, so use inquiry and careful discussion, not drill (philosophy §13). The global/anthropological lens:
nationalism is historically recent and culturally specific — for most of human history people belonged by
kinship, place, faith, or empire, not nation-state. The ethics lens: loyalty to one's own is good, but it
does not license harm to outsiders. The egalitarian lens: every person outside the line is as fully human as
everyone inside it. The technology lens: online platforms can harden "us versus them" by showing us only our
side and feeding outrage against the out-group. Hold this topic with care — learners may hold deep national
or ethnic loyalties; honor them while inviting honest reflection, and keep the posture of questions, not
verdicts.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 6: how can belonging include some people and exclude others? Today we
   examine the biggest "us" of all — the nation.
2. **Meet the warmth (10 min).** Draw the circle: "us" inside, "them" outside. In-group loyalty is real and
   early — even people given a *meaningless* label (blue team vs red team) begin to favor their own side
   (S-232). Name what "us" gives people: belonging, trust, shared purpose.
3. **Meet the edge (10 min).** The same line that holds "us" together leaves "them" out. An out-group can be
   stereotyped, mistrusted, and — at its worst — dehumanized. A **nation** is an "imagined community": its
   members will never meet, yet they imagine themselves as one (S-415).
4. **Analyze the double face (15 min).** Work the case-study cards: a liberation movement that united a
   people; a national pride that became a campaign against a minority; a sports loyalty that turned violent.
   For each, answer: what did this "us" build, and what did it limit or destroy?
5. **Ask the honest question (10 min).** The question is not "is loyalty good or bad?" but "who does this
   'us' include, who does it leave out, and at what cost?" Write your answer for one case — and redraw the
   circle wider where you think it should be.
6. **Close (5 min).** Loyalty can be love; exclusion is where love stops. The skill is holding the first
   without slipping into the second.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort the case-study cards into "mostly builds," "mostly limits," and "both" using the diagram.
- **Extension:** Compare a liberating nationalism (a people winning independence) with an oppressive one (a
  nation dominating others), and name two things that distinguish them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner explain how in-group loyalty both builds and limits community,
  and apply the "who is left out?" question?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The annotated diagram with one case analysis and the redrawn circle, kept in the
  portfolio.

## Home connection

Notice one "us" you belong to this week — a team, a group chat, a nation, a faith. Ask yourself honestly: who
does this "us" include, and who does it leave out?

## Resources

- On minimal-group favoritism and social identity: Henri Tajfel (1970) (S-232).
- On nationalism as an "imagined community": Benedict Anderson (1983), *Imagined Communities* (S-415).
- On hidden bias and how groups shape perception: Banaji & Greenwald (2013), *Blindspot* (S-344).
