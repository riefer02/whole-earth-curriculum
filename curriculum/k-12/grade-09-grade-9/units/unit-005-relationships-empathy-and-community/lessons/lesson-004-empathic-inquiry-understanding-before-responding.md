# Lesson 4 — Empathic Inquiry: Understanding Before Responding

## Summary

Learners practice **empathic inquiry** — a four-step loop of **observe, ask, reflect feelings and needs, and
check** — to understand another person before responding, resisting the urge to fix, judge, or argue first.

## Objectives

- Use empathic inquiry to understand another's feelings and needs before responding. _(D02.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Someone you know is upset — a friend who just failed something they studied for, a sibling who was left out,
a classmate who seems angry for no reason. Your first impulse is often to fix it ("you should just…"), to
minimize it ("it's not a big deal"), or to argue ("you're overreacting"). But people rarely feel helped by
being fixed before they are heard. There is a different first move, practiced by counselors, mediators, and
good friends everywhere: understand first, respond second. Today you learn the loop.

## Materials

- Empathic-inquiry loop card
- Listening scenarios

## Preparation

- Copy the loop card and scenario set.
- Retrieval: from Grade 6, recall the difference between empathy (feeling with) and sympathy (feeling for)
  (D02.S2.06.01); from Grade 8, recall how bias and stereotypes can block empathy.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **empathic inquiry is a four-step loop —
(1) observe: notice the person's face, voice, body, and situation; (2) ask: an open question that invites
them to say more ("what's that been like for you?"); (3) reflect: name the feeling and the need you hear
("you sound frustrated because you wanted this to go differently"); (4) check: confirm you got it right
("is that close?"), and loop back if not.** This is a *skill*; use a worked example first, then guided
practice (S-011). The ethics lens: understanding someone is not endorsing them — and it is the honest
starting point before any judgment or advice. The egalitarian lens: whose feelings get listened to, and
whose get talked over, is often about power; empathic inquiry is a way of making room for quieter voices.
The global lens: deep listening is honored in traditions across cultures — from the talking circle to the
mediator's questions — though its forms differ. The technology lens: in text there is no face or voice to
observe, so the "ask" and "check" steps matter even more, and a kind check can prevent a misread. Emphasize
that reflecting is a guess offered humbly, not a verdict.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 6: what is the difference between feeling *with* someone and feeling *for*
   someone? Today we build the skill of the first one.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Empathic inquiry has four steps: **observe** (notice their face, voice, body,
   and situation), **ask** (an open question), **reflect** (name the feeling and need you hear), and **check**
   (confirm, and loop back if you missed).
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** Scenario: a friend says, "I can't believe I practiced all week and
   still didn't get the part." _Observe:_ voice flat, shoulders low. _Ask:_ "What was that like for you?"
   _Reflect:_ "You sound disappointed — like you put in all that work and wanted it to pay off." _Check:_ "Is
   that close?" Trace each step and say why this is different from "you should just try again."
4. **Practice in pairs (15 min).** Use the scenarios. Person A shares something that is bothering them (real
   or from the set). Person B runs the loop — and does *not* fix, judge, or argue. Then switch roles.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Ask your partner: did I observe and ask before I reflected? Did I check my guess?
   Did I resist the urge to fix it? Adjust what was missing.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one step you found hard. Understanding first is a skill, and it gets easier with
   practice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a bank of feeling and need words and open-question stems ("what's it been like…?",
  "how did that land for you?").
- **Extension:** While listening, also notice your own inner reaction (the urge to fix or judge) and name it
  silently — then return to the loop.
- **Deaf / hard of hearing:** "Observe" means seeing — sign, gesture, and visible expression — not hearing a
  voice; reflect and check can be signed, written, or pointed to the feeling and need bank.
- **Neurodivergent / non-speaking / AAC:** Reading a face is not required. When it is hard, the "ask" and
  "check" steps carry the load — ask how the person feels rather than guessing from expression, and always
  check your guess. Reflection may be written, signed, or pointed, never only spoken.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner run the loop — observe, ask, reflect, check — before
  responding?
- **Peer check:** Partner confirms the listener reflected feelings and needs and checked the guess.
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed loop card with one worked example, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

This week, when someone shares a difficulty, try *not* to fix it for one full minute — just ask one question
and reflect what you hear. Notice what changes.
