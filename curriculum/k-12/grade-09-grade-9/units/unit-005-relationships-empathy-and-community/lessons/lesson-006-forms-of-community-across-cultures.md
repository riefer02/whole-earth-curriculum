# Lesson 6 — Forms of Community Across Cultures

## Summary

Learners compare **forms of community** across cultures — kinship, chosen family, mutual-aid networks, faith
communities, diaspora, and online communities — identifying what people join, share, and give in each, and
what all of them have in common: belonging and care.

## Objectives

- Compare forms of community across cultures, including chosen families and mutual-aid networks.
  _(D02.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

Ask yourself how many "we's" you belong to. Your family is one — but maybe not the only one. There may be the
friends who feel like family, the people you worship or celebrate with, the neighbors who help when someone
is sick, the online group that shares your language or hobby, the relatives scattered across countries who
still call. Community is not one shape; it is many, and different cultures have built strikingly different
ones. Today you map the variety — and find what they all share.

## Materials

- Forms-of-community chart
- Community example cards

## Preparation

- Copy the chart and example cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 7, recall how online and offline communities differ (D02.S3.07.01); from Grade 8,
  recall how power and resources are shared in a community and who is left out.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **community takes many forms — (1) kinship
(family by birth or marriage), (2) chosen family (people who become family by choice, not blood), (3)
mutual-aid networks (people who meet one another's needs together, as equals), (4) faith and spiritual
communities, (5) diaspora (a people scattered across countries who keep a shared identity and care across
distance), and (6) online communities. What all forms share: people belong to something, share something
(stories, resources, identity, care), and give to one another.** This is a *concept*, so use inquiry and
comparison (philosophy §13). The global/anthropological lens is the heart here: communities differ across
cultures and times — the communal-work traditions of harambee in Kenya, gotong royong in Indonesia, mink'a in
the Andes, and barn-raising in North America are all named forms of mutual aid (S-164); the term "mutual aid"
was popularized by Kropotkin (S-163); and mutual aid as solidarity — not charity — is articulated by Dean
Spade (S-345). The egalitarian lens: there is no single "real" family or "real" community — a chosen family
is as real as a birth family, and honoring that is honoring people's lives. The ethics lens: communities
carry obligations — to care for members and to ask who is left out. The technology lens: online community is
real belonging, not a lesser copy, even though it works differently. Approach other cultures' communities as
living traditions to honor with respectful questions, never as specimens.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 7: how is belonging online different from belonging offline? Today we look
   at the many shapes belonging takes.
2. **Meet the forms (12 min).** Read the six forms on the chart: **kinship, chosen family, mutual-aid
   network, faith community, diaspora, online community.** For each, use the example cards to answer: how do
   people join? what do they share? how do they care for one another?
3. **Compare (15 min).** Fill the chart. Then compare: how does a chosen family meet the same needs a kinship
   family does? How is a mutual-aid network different from a charity — and why does that difference matter
   (S-345)?
4. **Find the shared center (10 min).** Across all six forms, name what they share: people **belong** to
   something, **share** something, and **give** to one another. Write your own list of the communities you
   belong to, and mark which form each is.
5. **Close (8 min).** Share one community of yours that does not fit a single box. That overlap is not a
   problem; it is how belonging really works.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the chart partially filled; learners add their own example under each form.
- **Extension:** Compare two forms in depth — a kinship family and a chosen family — and write where each is
  stronger or weaker at meeting the needs of belonging, support, and care.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe several forms of community and what they share?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed forms-of-community chart with the learner's own communities marked,
  kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an older person in your family or neighborhood: "What communities have you belonged to in your life?" Add
what they name to your chart.

- On mutual aid as a widespread human practice: Peter Kropotkin (1902), *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*
  (S-163).
- On mutual aid as solidarity, distinct from charity: Dean Spade (2020), *Mutual Aid* (S-345).
