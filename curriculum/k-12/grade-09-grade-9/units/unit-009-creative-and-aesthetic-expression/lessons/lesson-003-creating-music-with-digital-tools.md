# Lesson 3 — Creating Music with Digital Tools

## Summary

Learners **build a short piece of music** in a free digital tool: they choose a loop or make a beat,
add a melody, layer at least one more track, and apply one **effect** such as reverb or echo. They
close by **reflecting on how the tool shaped the sound and the process** — the loop's repetition, the
grid's perfect timing, the undo that erases a mistake — and how that differs from singing or playing
live, where the body and the moment cannot be clicked away.

## Objectives

- Create music using digital or recording tools, and reflect on how the technology shaped the sound
  and the process. _(D07.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Every song you have ever loved was built the same way a wall is built — one brick at a time. A beat
is laid down, then a bass line, then a melody, then a voice, each one a **track** stacked on the
last. Long before computers, musicians layered parts by playing together; a digital tool lets one
person be the whole band, laying each part down alone and listening back. Today you become that
one-person band and hear how the tool changes what music can be.

## Materials

- A device with a free music tool (BandLab, GarageBand, Audacity, or a browser sequencer)
- Headphones or a shared speaker

## Preparation

- Open the tool once yourself and confirm a loop library or simple instrument is available; find where
  the tempo and one effect (reverb/echo) live.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall that a *style* or *tradition* is a set of choices — instrumentation,
  rhythm, and form. Today we make choices with a tool.

## Facilitator note

The goal is one short piece (30–60 seconds) plus a reflection, not virtuosity. Keep tools free so no
learner is left out (the **egalitarian** lens): BandLab runs in a browser and Audacity is a free,
open-source recorder/editor (S-443, S-444). The **worked example** matters here — building a track is
a new skill, so model the exact steps (choose loop → add melody → add track → add effect) before
independent work (S-011). The **technology** lens is the heart: ask *what did the tool let you do that
a live body could not* (loop forever, undo, quantize to a perfect grid, stretch a note) *and what did
it take away* (breath, the wobble of a real hand, the room's echo). The **ethics** lens: if learners
borrow a loop or sample, credit its creator and respect its license — sampling with credit is a
tradition; sampling without credit is taking. The **global** lens: invite learners to begin from any
rhythm or scale they know — a djembe pattern, a salsa clave, a hip-hop break — as a respectful
starting point, not a costume.

## Procedure

1. **Set up (5 min).** Open the tool and set a **tempo**. Look at the timeline diagram: tracks are
   rows, and the clips you place on them are the "bricks."
2. **Make the first track (10 min).** Worked example, follow along: choose or make a **loop** — a
   drum beat or a short rhythm that repeats. Place it on the first track and press play. This
   repetition is the floor of your piece.
3. **Add a melody (15 min).** On a second track, add a melody — click notes or play them in. Keep it
   short; a melody is a sentence, not a speech.
4. **Layer and shape (15 min).** Add one more track (a bass line, a pad, a voice, or a field
   recording). Then apply one **effect** to any track — reverb makes it sound far away, echo repeats
   it, EQ brightens or darkens it. Change the tempo once and notice what happens.
5. **Reflect (10 min).** Write three sentences: what tracks you built, one thing the tool let you do
   that a live body could not, and one thing live music has that your piece does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Start from a provided two-loop template and add only a melody.
- **Extension:** Record a live voice or instrument over the loops and describe in one sentence how it
  changed the feeling.
- **Access (Deaf / hard of hearing):** Build the piece as a **visible rhythm** — place clips on the
  timeline and follow the moving playhead, watch a waveform, feel the pulse by resting a hand on the
  speaker, or sign/gesture each part. The track grid and effect labels are the music here, not only
  the sound.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the piece have at least two layered parts and one
  effect? Does the reflection name a specific way the tool shaped the sound (loop, undo, timing grid,
  effect) rather than just "it was on a computer"?
- **Self-check:** Can you point to each track in your piece and say what it adds?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how music was made when they were young — a radio, a record, a live singer, a
drum. How is your piece different, and what is the same?
