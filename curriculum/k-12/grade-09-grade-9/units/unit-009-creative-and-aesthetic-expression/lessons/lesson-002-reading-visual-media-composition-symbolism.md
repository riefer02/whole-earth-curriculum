# Lesson 2 — Reading Visual Media: Composition & Symbolism

## Summary

Learners use a **composition checklist** — rule of thirds, leading line, framing, color, scale, and
gaze — and hunt for **symbols** to analyze one advertisement, one news photograph, and one
social-media post. They practice the critical habit of separating **observation** (what is actually
there) from **judgment** (what it makes me feel or believe) and asking **who benefits** and **who is
left out**. Persuasion is not automatically bad; this lesson equips learners to tell honest
persuasion from manipulation.

## Objectives

- Analyze how visual media — advertising, news, and social media — use composition and symbolism to
  influence viewers. _(D07.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

Before you read a word of a page, you have already read its images. The bright button you are meant
to tap, the photo that makes a story feel urgent, the smiling face beside a product — none of these
are accidents. Someone **placed** them, **framed** them, and **colored** them so your eye would land
exactly where they want. You already feel the pull of composition and symbolism every day; today you
learn the names for the moves, so you can decide for yourself whether to go along.

## Materials

- Three images per group: one advertisement, one news photograph, one social-media post
- A composition checklist sheet

## Preparation

- Select or print the three images; pick ones with clear composition and at least one obvious symbol.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the critique habit — **observation is different from judgment**.
  Today we turn that into a checklist and apply it to media that is trying to *influence* us.

## Facilitator note

This lesson builds media literacy, not cynicism. The goal is that a learner can *name* the tools of
visual persuasion (composition, symbolism) and *ask* what they are for — not that they distrust every
image. Use the **worked example**: analyze the first image together, out loud, with the checklist,
before learners work in groups (S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the core: evidence is what
I can point to; judgment is what I value. The **ethics** lens: persuasion and manipulation both use
the same tools — the difference is honesty about what is being asked and who gains. The
**egalitarian** lens: always ask *who is shown, who is missing, and why that matters* — advertising
has a long history of leaving whole groups out or casting them in narrow roles. The **technology**
lens: composition tools are now in everyone's pocket; the same grid a designer uses is on your phone
camera. Keep sources honest: the Center for Media Literacy's core questions (who created this, what
techniques attract attention, what is omitted, who benefits) frame the analysis (S-442).

## Procedure

1. **Meet the checklist (5 min).** Look at the annotated diagram. The moves: **rule of thirds**
   (the four sweet spots where lines cross), **leading line** (a real or implied line that pulls the
   eye), **framing** (what is inside and outside the edges), **color** (bright draws the eye), and
   **gaze** (where a pictured person looks). A **symbol** is an object or color that stands for
   something more — a crown for power, a dove for peace, a green leaf for "natural."
2. **Worked example: one ad, together (10 min).** As a group, find in one advertisement: the focal
   point, one leading line, one symbol, and one thing the maker wants you to feel. Say each as an
   *observation* ("the product is at a rule-of-thirds crossing") before any *judgment* ("this makes
   me want it").
3. **Analyze in groups (20 min).** In your group, run the checklist over the news photograph and the
   social-media post. For each, write: one composition move, one symbol, what it makes you notice,
   and what it makes you feel.
4. **Ask who benefits (10 min).** For each image: who made it, who is shown, who is missing, and who
   gains if you are persuaded? Say which answers are evidence and which are your values.
5. **Close (10 min).** Share one finding. How will you look at the next ad or post differently?

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("The eye goes first to ___ because ___.").
- **Extension:** Trace one image to its source and add the question "does the source match the
  message?" — the bridge to Grade 9 media verification.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Analyze each image through a **spoken description** — a partner or
  the facilitator describes placement, color, and symbol aloud, and the learner names the composition
  move and the symbol from what they hear. The checklist works entirely by listening and asking.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the learner name at least one composition move and one
  symbol for each image, and keep observation separate from judgment?
- **Peer-check:** Trade findings with a partner; can your partner see the move you described?

## Home connection

For one day, notice every image that tries to get your attention — signs, screens, packaging. Pick one
and ask at dinner: what is it trying to make you do, and how?
