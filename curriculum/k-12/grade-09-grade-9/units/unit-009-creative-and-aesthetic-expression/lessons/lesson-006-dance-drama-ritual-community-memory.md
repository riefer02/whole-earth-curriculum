# Lesson 6 — Dance, Drama & Ritual: Building Community & Carrying Memory

## Summary

Learners analyze how dance, drama, and ritual build **community** and carry **memory** across
cultures, using three well-documented examples: the Māori **haka**, Mexico's Indigenous **Day of the
Dead** festivity, and the West African **griot** tradition. They separate what they *observe* from
what they *value*, and they meet each tradition as a living practice — a way people still gather and
remember today — approached with respectful questions, never as a specimen to dissect.

## Objectives

- Analyze how dance, drama, and ritual build community or carry memory across cultures.
  _(D07.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

Think of how your own family marks the big things — a birth, a death, a harvest, a new year. There is
usually a way of *doing* it: the same food, the same song, the same motions, done again each time.
That repetition is not empty; it is how memory is kept when no one writes it down, and how a group
reminds itself that it is one group. All over the world, people have used the moving body and the
spoken word to hold their history and their belonging. Today you look at how three peoples do it.

## Materials

- Two or three short case-study notes with images (haka, Day of the Dead, griot tradition)
- An analysis organizer with two columns: community / memory

## Preparation

- Prepare the case-study notes; if showing video, choose recordings made by or with the community.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the critique habit — **observation is different from judgment**.
  Today we apply it to traditions that are still alive.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is about respect, not spectacle. The **worked example** models the analysis on one
tradition before learners analyze the others (S-011). The **global** and **community** lenses carry
it. The Māori **haka** is a living tradition of posture and chant that expresses identity, strength,
welcome, and remembrance — explained authoritatively by Te Ara, the Encyclopedia of New Zealand
(S-447). Mexico's **Day of the Dead** ("Indigenous festivity dedicated to the dead," inscribed by
UNESCO in 2008) commemorates the temporary return of deceased loved ones, linking the living to
ancestors and the maize harvest — a fusion of Indigenous and Catholic traditions (S-448). The West
African **griot** (also *jeli*/*jali*) is a troubadour-historian who carries genealogies and histories
in an oral tradition across generations (S-446). Speak of all three in the *present tense* — these
are living peoples. The **ethics** lens: rituals can also be sites of belonging *and* exclusion (who
is allowed to perform, who is remembered); name this honestly. The **environment** lens: many rituals
are tied to seasons, harvests, and land — memory and place are one. If a learner brings a tradition
of their own, honor it first and let them teach it.

## Procedure

1. **Meet the organizer (5 min).** Look at the ritual cycle: **gather → remember → perform →
   renew**. A ritual does at least two things — it **builds community** (people gather, belong, and
   participate) and **carries memory** (it holds what a people must not forget).
2. **Worked example: the haka (15 min).** Read or watch the haka case study. Together, fill the
   organizer: what you *observe* (unison posture, chant, stamping, facing the group) and what it
   *does* (expresses identity, welcomes, remembers, unifies). Say each as an observation before any
   judgment.
3. **Analyze: Day of the Dead and the griot (20 min).** In pairs, fill the organizer for each. Day of
   the Dead: how does it build community (families prepare together, the living and dead meet) and
   carry memory (altars, offerings, the maize cycle)? The griot: how does the spoken word carry
   genealogy and history across generations?
4. **Compare (10 min).** What do the three share? What is different because their places, seasons,
   and histories differ?
5. **Close (5 min).** Name one ritual in your own life — a holiday, a game day, a family habit — and
   what it builds or remembers.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("This ritual remembers ___ by ___.").
- **Extension:** Add a tradition from your own community and analyze it with the same
  observation-before-judgment discipline.
- **Access:** No movement is required — the analysis runs entirely from the case studies and the
  organizer. When showing video, pair it with a **spoken description** of the movement, and give a
  **written transcription** of any chant so rhythm and words remain evidence for learners who cannot
  hear them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the learner cite *observed* evidence (movement, chant,
  object, repetition, call-and-response) for how each tradition builds community or carries memory,
  and keep observation separate from judgment?
- **Peer-check:** Can your partner see or hear the evidence you named?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a ritual or repeated tradition in your family. What does it remember, and
who does it gather?
