# Lesson 8 — Design, Technology & the Environment

## Summary

Learners trace two contrasting designed objects — one handmade and local, one mass-produced — through
their full **life cycle** (materials, making, use, disposal) and analyze how design and technology
shape everyday life and the environment. They weigh **trade-offs** — cost, convenience, durability,
waste, energy, and accessibility — and compare at least two contexts, asking **who designs, who is
left out, and where the burden of harm falls**. This closes the unit by turning the maker's hand and
the critical eye onto the made world itself.

## Objectives

- Analyze how technology and design shape everyday life and the environment, using examples from more
  than one context. _(D07.S4.09.02)_

## Connection

The objects around you are not neutral. The plastic bottle cools nothing by itself and lasts for
centuries after you throw it away; a clay pot cools water by evaporation, breaks down into earth, and
is made by a neighbor's hands. A concrete building soaks up and radiates heat and pours carbon into
the air as it is made; an earthen wall breathes and returns to soil. The same need — to hold water, to
shelter a family — can be answered by designs that leave very different marks on the living world.
Today you learn to see that difference, because design is a decision someone made, and it can be made
differently.

## Materials

- Two contrasting object case studies (a clay pot vs. a plastic bottle; an earthen building vs. a
  concrete one)
- A life-cycle organizer with four stages: materials / making / use / disposal

## Preparation

- Gather the two object case studies; if possible, bring the actual objects so learners can hold them.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall that a design can be critiqued on **function, materials, fairness,
  and environmental footprint**. Today we trace the full footprint.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is analysis, not a morality play — the point is that every design is a **trade-off**, and
the trade-offs look different in different places. The **environment** and **technology** lenses
carry it: trace each object from **materials → making → use → disposal**, and ask about **embodied
energy** (the energy spent to make it), **durability**, and **waste**. The **egalitarian** lens: who
can afford the cheap plastic bottle versus the handmade pot, who lives near the factory and the
landfill, and who has no choice at all — environmental harm falls unevenly (this lesson connects to
Grade 9 environmental justice). The **ethics** lens: a designer who leaves out wheelchair users,
children, or the poor has made a decision, not an oversight; **accessibility** is a design choice.
Keep the **worked example** on one object, then let learners trace the other (S-011). Use the
**circular economy** idea as a horizon: designing so a thing can be repaired, reused, or returned to
the loop, not thrown away (S-452). Hold questions as questions — there is rarely one "right" object,
only clearer trade-offs.

## Procedure

1. **Meet the life cycle (5 min).** Look at the life-cycle diagram. Every made thing travels
   **materials → making → use → disposal**, and ideally back to materials through reuse or recycling.
2. **Worked example: one object, together (15 min).** Trace the plastic bottle (or the mass-produced
   object) through the four stages: what it is made of, where it is made and at what energy cost, how
   it is used, and where it goes when it is done. Note one way it *helps* life (convenient, light,
   cheap) and one way it *costs* the environment (waste, energy, persistence).
3. **Analyze: the other context (20 min).** In pairs, trace the clay pot or earthen wall (or the
   handmade local object) through the same four stages. Compare: what does each do better, and for
   whom?
4. **Weigh the trade-offs (10 min).** For each object, ask: who can afford it, who is left out, and
   where does the harm fall? Say which answers are evidence and which are your values.
5. **Close (5 min).** Name one design choice in your own life you would now make differently, or one
   question about a made object you want answered.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a partly filled life-cycle chart with one stage blank to complete.
- **Extension:** Add a third context — a different climate or economy — and explain how the "best"
  design answer changes there.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the learner trace both objects through all four stages
  and name at least one trade-off across two contexts, keeping evidence separate from values?
- **Peer-check:** Can your partner find the trade-off you described in the actual objects?

## Home connection

Pick one object at home and ask: what is it made of, where will it go when it breaks, and could it
have been made differently?
