# Lesson 4 — Music as Identity, Community & Resistance

## Summary

Learners listen to music from **more than one culture** — reggae from Jamaica and nueva canción from
Chile, with an optional third tradition from the learners' own world — and analyze how each builds
**identity** (a shared sense of who we are), **community** (belonging, gathering, participation), or
**resistance** (protest, solidarity, survival under pressure). They practice separating what they
*hear* from what they *value*, and they treat every tradition as **living heritage** to honor with
respectful questions — never as a specimen.

## Objectives

- Analyze how music can build identity, community, or resistance, using examples from more than one
  culture. _(D07.S2.09.02)_

## Connection

Think of the song your family sings at a wedding, a funeral, a festival, or a march. It is not just
decoration: it gathers people into one rhythm, reminds them who they are, and, when times are hard,
keeps their courage alive. A song can hold a memory that no textbook does — a name, a wrong, a hope.
Across the world, people who had little else have still had a song, and the song carried what the
people needed to survive and to resist. Today you listen to how that works.

## Materials

- Two or three short listening excerpts with brief context notes
- A listening organizer with three columns: identity / community / resistance

## Preparation

- Choose and queue the excerpts; have a line or two of context for each (who made it, when, and why).
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the critique habit — **observation is different from judgment**.
  Today we apply it to music that *does* something in the world.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is about respectful listening, not ranking. The **worked example** models the analysis on
one song before learners analyze the others (S-011). Two well-documented examples carry the
**global** lens: **reggae** "originated within a cultural space that was home to marginalized groups"
in western Kingston and is recognized as "a voice for all" on questions of "injustice, resistance,
love and humanity" (UNESCO, S-450; also Britannica, S-445); **nueva canción** is the Latin American
movement that fused folk forms with protest and social change during the 1960s–80s, when musicians
faced censorship and violence for singing (Britannica, S-449). Both are living traditions — people
still make them today — so speak of them as *present*, not past. The **ethics** lens: resistance
music often emerges from real harm (apartheid, dictatorship, slavery, colonial rule); name the harm
honestly but keep hope and agency attached. The **egalitarian** lens: ask *whose* song gets recorded,
celebrated, and remembered, and whose is forgotten. The **community** lens: call-and-response and
group singing are *participatory* — the community is not the audience; it is the instrument. If a
learner brings a tradition of their own, honor it first and let them teach it.

## Procedure

1. **Meet the organizer (5 min).** Look at the three circles. One song can build **identity** (we
   know who we are), **community** (we gather and belong), and **resistance** (we refuse and endure)
   — often at the same time.
2. **Worked example: reggae (15 min).** Listen to a reggae excerpt with its context. Together, fill
   the organizer: what you *hear* (offbeat guitar, bass line, Rastafari references) and what it
   *does* (gives voice to the marginalized, praises, comments on injustice). Say each as an
   observation before any judgment.
3. **Analyze: nueva canción (15 min).** Listen to a nueva canción excerpt. In pairs, fill the
   organizer: how does it build identity (folk roots, local instruments, Spanish words of home), and
   how is it resistance (singing what is forbidden, keeping memory alive under dictatorship)?
4. **Compare (10 min).** What do the two traditions share? What is different because their places,
   histories, and instruments differ?
5. **Close (10 min).** If you brought a tradition, share it. Otherwise, name one song you already
   know that builds identity, community, or resistance — and how.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters ("This song gathers people because ___.").
- **Extension:** Add a third tradition and analyze its function with the same evidence discipline,
   then name what makes it distinct.
- **Access (Deaf / hard of hearing):** Meet each song through its **words and written rhythm** — read
  the lyrics (with translation), follow a rhythm chart or notation, or feel the pulse on a speaker's
  vibration — and cite lyric and rhythm as evidence exactly as a listener would cite what they hear.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the learner cite *heard* evidence (rhythm, instrument,
  call-and-response, lyric) for each function, across at least two cultures, and keep observation
  separate from judgment?
- **Peer-check:** Can your partner hear the evidence you pointed to?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home for one song that mattered in a hard time or a celebration. What did it help them
feel or do?
