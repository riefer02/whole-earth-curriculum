# Lesson 1 — Digital & Mixed-Media Art: Combining Techniques

## Summary

Learners **make a hand-made element** — a drawing, painting, or found-object print — then **bring it
into a free digital tool** and combine it with at least one **digital technique** (a color layer, a
photograph, or text) to produce one **mixed-media artwork**. They end by **reflecting in writing on
the choices the technology made possible**. The unit opens here because making something of your own
gives the rest of the unit — reading media, composing music, performing, and designing — a craft and
a critical eye to build on.

## Objectives

- Create a digital or mixed-media artwork that combines at least two techniques — one hand-made and
  one digital — and reflect on the choices the technology made possible. _(D07.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last picture you liked online. Almost none of it began and ended in one place: a photo
was taken, then cropped, then a filter was added, then text was pasted on top, then it was shared.
Every image is built in **layers** — and once you see the layers, you can build your own. A quilt,
a mosaic, a page from a scrapbook, and a wall of street posters are all the same idea: parts placed
over and beside each other to make one whole. Today you make one of those wholes yourself, with your
hand and a screen working together.

## Materials

- Paper, pencils, and one hand-made element
- A device with a free image editor (Krita, GIMP, Inkscape, or a browser-based tool)
- A camera or scanner (a phone camera is enough)

## Preparation

- Confirm the free image editor is installed or loads in a browser; test it once yourself.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall that a *series* of artworks explores one theme and that a critique
  separates **observation** (what I see) from **judgment** (what I value). Today we turn that into
  *making* with layers.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Its goal is one finished mixed-media piece plus a
short written reflection — **not** technical mastery of a specific program. Keep the tools minimal
and free so no learner is left out (the egalitarian lens): Krita and GIMP are raster editors and
Inkscape is a vector editor, all open source (S-440, S-441). The **worked example** in the Procedure
models the full pipeline *before* independent work, because combining techniques is a new skill and
novices need explicit instruction first (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006; S-011). The **ethics**
lens: when you borrow an image, a texture, or a tradition's pattern, credit it and respect its
license — the difference between learning from a tradition and taking it. The **global** lens: invite
learners to draw inspiration from any living tradition they know (a kente cloth's woven geometry, a
rangoli's symmetry, an ukiyo-e print's flat color, a mural's scale) as *questions*, not as patterns
to copy without understanding. Keep the reflection honest: what did the tool let you do that your
hand alone could not?

## Procedure

1. **Plan the layers (5 min).** Look at the layer diagram. An artwork can be one, two, or three
   layers stacked. Decide: what will be your **bottom** (a color wash, a painted background), your
   **middle** (a hand drawing or print), and your **top** (digital text, a shape, or a second image)?
2. **Make the hand-made element (15 min).** Draw, paint, or print one element on paper — a face, an
   animal, a place you love, a symbol that matters to you. It does not need to be perfect; it needs
   to be *yours*.
3. **Bring it in (10 min).** Photograph or scan your element and open it in the image editor. This is
   the worked example, so follow these steps as they are modeled: (a) create a new canvas, (b) add
   your photo as a layer, (c) add a second layer on top, (d) on that new layer, add **one** digital
   technique — a flat color, a shape, or a line of text.
4. **Combine and revise (15 min).** Try what the tool makes easy: **undo** a mistake, **move** a
   layer, **change the color**, or **duplicate** an element. Make at least one revision you could not
   have made on paper.
5. **Reflect (10 min).** Write three sentences: what layers you used, one thing the tool let you do
   that your hand could not, and one thing your hand gave the piece that the screen did not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a pre-made two-layer template (background + one empty layer) so the learner
  focuses on adding one element.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth layer or try a blend mode (multiply, overlay) and describe in one
  sentence what it changed about the mood.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Build the layers as a **texture collage** — a different material
  for each layer (paper, cloth, sand, foil, string) so each layer is read by touch, and name each
  layer's role out loud. The idea of "stacked layers" is tactile, not only visual.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation + artifact):** Does the piece combine at least one hand-made and one
  digital element? Does the reflection name a specific technological choice (undo, layers, color,
  duplication) rather than just "I used a computer"?
- **Self-check:** Can you point to each layer in your own piece and say what it contributes?

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to show you one object made by combining materials — a quilt, a repaired pot, a
collaged photo frame, a decorated doorway. What layers make it up?
