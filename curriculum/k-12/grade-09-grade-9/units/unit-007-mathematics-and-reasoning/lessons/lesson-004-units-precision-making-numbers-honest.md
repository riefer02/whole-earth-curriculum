# Lesson 4 — Units & Precision: Making Numbers Honest

## Summary

Learners **choose units and a level of precision** for a real measurement, distinguishing
**precision** (how fine the markings are) from **accuracy** (how close to the true value), and
interpret what each level of precision is honest for. They meet the global reach of the metric
system and the many unit systems people have used, and learn that over-precision is a kind of
dishonesty.

## Objectives

- Choose appropriate units and levels of precision when measuring and calculating, and interpret the
  results in context. _(D05.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

Say a friend asks how far you walked to school. "Two kilometers" is honest. "Two point zero zero
zero kilometers" is not — unless you actually measured to the nearest meter. Every number you report
carries a hidden promise about how carefully you measured. A number with too many digits promises
more than you know, and a number with too few throws away information you had. Today you learn to
choose units and precision so your numbers tell the truth about what you actually know.

## Materials

- Measuring sheet
- Worked-example card

## Preparation

- Copy the measuring sheet and worked-example card.
- Retrieval: from Grades 5–8, recall converting among units (metric and others) and rounding.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **precision is how fine your
measuring tool's markings are; accuracy is how close your result is to the true value; the last
digit you report is the one you are sure about, and you choose units and precision to match what the
situation needs.** Use a **worked example first** (S-011): the same distance reported as "2 m"
(one significant figure, fine for ordering a rope), "1.8 m" (two, fine for fitting a desk), and
"1.83 m" (three, fine for a precise cut). The critical-thinking lens: a number's digits are a claim
about measurement, and we should read them that way. The ethics lens: over-precision is dishonesty
in disguise — a calculator that prints eight digits is not "more accurate," it is just echoing noise;
choosing honest precision is an act of truthfulness (philosophy §5). The global lens: the metric
system, built on shared base units, is used nearly everywhere because its conversions are powers of
ten, while people have long used many other honest systems — the cubit, the pace, the *li*, the
mile — each matched to its tasks (S-429). The egalitarian lens: honest measurement belongs to
everyone and needs no special equipment; a hand span or a pace is a real unit. For a learner who
cannot see the ruler markings, use a tactile measure instead — a rope with knots, a notched or
Braille ruler, or a partner who reads the markings aloud — and let pace-counting or hand spans carry
the same lesson; the idea (match your digits to what you actually know) is fully non-visual. The technology lens:
digital tools over-report digits, so the human's job is to decide what is meaningful, not to copy
the screen.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grades 5–8: what is the difference between meters and kilometers? What
   does it mean to round 1.827 to two decimal places?
2. **Meet precision vs. accuracy (8 min).** **Precision** = how fine the markings are. **Accuracy** =
   how close to the true value. A coarse ruler can be accurate; a fine ruler can be precise. They
   are different things.
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** The same length reported three ways: **2 m** (nearest
   meter — fine for buying rope), **1.8 m** (nearest 10 cm — fine for fitting a desk), **1.83 m**
   (nearest cm — fine for cutting a shelf). Each extra digit promises a finer measurement. Report
   only as many digits as your tool honestly gives you.
4. **Measure three ways (15 min).** Choose one real distance (a desk, a doorway, a wall). Measure it
   with three tools — a meter stick, a 30 cm ruler, and a hand span or pace. On the measuring sheet,
   record each result *with the right number of digits for that tool*, and note what each precision
   is good for.
5. **Interpret in context (10 min).** For each measurement, ask: would this precision matter if I
   were (a) estimating paint for a wall, (b) cutting a shelf to fit, (c) telling a friend roughly
   how big the room is? Write which precision you would choose and why.
6. **Close (5 min).** A number is a promise. Choose your digits so the promise matches what you
   actually know.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fill-in table (tool → smallest marking → example result with correct digits).
- **Extension:** Do a small calculation with measured numbers (e.g., area of a rectangle) and round
  the answer to the number of significant figures the measurements justify.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner state a result with a number of digits that matches
  the tool, and explain which precision a real task needs?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed measuring sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Measure the same thing at home two ways (a ruler and a step or hand span) and write both results
with honest precision. Which precision would you use to order something that must fit?
