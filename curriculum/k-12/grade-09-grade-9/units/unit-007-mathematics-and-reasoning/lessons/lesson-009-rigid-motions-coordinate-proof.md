# Lesson 9 — Rigid Motions & Coordinate Proof

## Summary

Learners **prove congruence with rigid motions and coordinates**: they show two figures are congruent
either by naming a sequence of translations, rotations, and reflections that maps one onto the
other, or by using the **distance formula** to show corresponding sides are equal. They connect this
to the repeating symmetries of tiling and design across cultures.

## Objectives

- Use rigid motions and coordinates to prove and reason about geometric relationships such as
  congruence. _(D05.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

Pick up a shape, slide it, turn it, or flip it over — it does not stretch or shrink. That is a
**rigid motion**, and it is the exact meaning of "same shape and size." Two figures are
**congruent** if a sequence of rigid motions carries one onto the other. People have used this idea
for thousands of years to tile floors and walls — Islamic geometric patterns, South Asian rangoli,
African woven designs — repeating a few shapes by sliding, turning, and flipping them. Today you
learn to *prove* congruence with motions and with coordinates.

## Materials

- Coordinate-grid sheet
- Worked-example card

## Preparation

- Copy the coordinate-grid sheet and worked-example card.
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall translations, rotations, reflections, and the distance formula.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **congruence means "carried onto
by a sequence of rigid motions (translations, rotations, reflections)," and on a coordinate grid you
can prove two figures congruent either by naming that sequence or by using the distance formula to
show corresponding side lengths are equal.** Use a **worked example first, then guided practice**
(S-011): two triangles with vertices such that a single translation maps one onto the other, and a
distance-formula check that each corresponding side has the same length. The critical-thinking lens: "it looks the same" is not a proof — the sequence of motions or
the distance calculation is. The global lens: rigid motion is the mathematics behind tiling and
symmetry art worldwide — Islamic girih, South Asian rangoli and kolam, and woven/beaded African
patterns are built from a few shapes moved by rigid motions (S-024, S-429). The egalitarian lens:
proof here needs no expensive tool — tracing paper, a cut-out, or a coordinate grid all carry the
same argument, so no learner is locked out by equipment. The ethics lens: claiming "congruent" only
when a sequence of motions actually maps one figure onto the other is intellectual honesty — "it
looks the same" without a check is over-claiming (philosophy §5). The technology & environment
lens: dynamic geometry software can animate the motions, but the reasoning is the learner's; and
the same rigid symmetries appear in crystals, leaf arrangements, and the structures around us — a
proof is also a way of seeing the order in the natural world. Emphasize that a proof is a clear,
checkable argument, not a performance for the "mathy."

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what do a translation, rotation, and reflection each do? What
   does the distance formula find?
2. **Meet rigid motion and congruence (8 min).** A **rigid motion** preserves distance and angle — it
   does not stretch or shrink. Two figures are **congruent** if rigid motions map one exactly onto the
   other.
3. **Study the worked example (12 min).** On the grid, triangle A has vertices (0,0), (4,0), (0,3);
   triangle B has vertices (6,2), (10,2), (6,5). **Motion proof:** translate A right by 6 and up by 2 —
   every vertex moves the same way: (0,0) → (6,2), (4,0) → (10,2), (0,3) → (6,5) — so it lands
   exactly on B. **Coordinate proof:** by the distance formula, each side of A (4, 3, 5) equals the
   corresponding side of B (4, 3, 5), so the triangles are congruent.
4. **Guided practice (15 min).** With a partner, plot two new congruent triangles on the
   coordinate-grid sheet and prove they are congruent *both* ways — by naming a sequence of motions
   and by the distance formula.
5. **See it in design (10 min).** Look at a tiling or rangoli image (or one you draw). Find one shape
   and name the motions (slide, turn, flip) that repeat it across the pattern.
6. **Close (5 min).** "Same shape and size" now has a precise meaning: a sequence of rigid motions.
   That precision is the beginning of proof.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a cut-out of one triangle to physically slide, turn, and flip onto the other
  before writing the proof.
- **Extension:** Prove a quadrilateral is a parallelogram by showing (with the midpoint or distance
  formula) that its diagonals bisect each other.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a sequence of rigid motions and a distance check
  that prove two triangles congruent?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed coordinate proof (motion sequence and distance check), kept
  in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Find a tiled or patterned surface at home or nearby (a floor, a fence, a fabric). Trace one repeating
shape and name the slide, turn, or flip that repeats it.
