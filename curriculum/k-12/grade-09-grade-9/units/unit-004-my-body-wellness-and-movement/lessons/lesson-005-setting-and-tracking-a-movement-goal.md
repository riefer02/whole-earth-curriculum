# Lesson 5 — Setting and Tracking a Movement Goal

## Summary

Learners **set a personal movement goal and plan to track it over time**. The goal is
specific, measurable, and realistic; it is built on *function, feeling, or skill* — never
weight or appearance. They build a simple tracking log, test the first step, and commit to
a check-in, framing the whole thing as ownership, not punishment.

## Objectives

- Set a personal movement or fitness goal and track progress toward it over time.
  _(D03.S2.09.01)_

## Connection

Think of a goal as something you *own*, not something you *owe*. A good movement goal
sounds like "I want to be able to carry the groceries up the hill without stopping" or "I
want to dance without getting winded" or "I want to sit at my desk without my back aching"
— not "I should fix my body." The difference matters: a goal built on what your body can
*do and feel* invites you in, while a goal built on *shame* pushes you away. Today you set
a goal you would actually keep, and you build the tiny tracking habit that keeps it real.

## Materials

- Movement-goal planner
- Tracking log template

## Preparation

- Copy the planner and tracking log.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the body–mind loop — movement is one of the ways you
  can deliberately shift your own body state.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **a good movement goal
is (1) specific and measurable, (2) realistic — a small step you can actually do this
week, (3) built on function, feeling, or skill rather than weight or appearance, and (4)
tracked simply, with a planned check-in and a kind response when a week is missed.** This
is a *skill*, so use a **worked example first, then guided practice** (S-011). The
critical lens here is ethical and egalitarian: hold the line firmly that goals must never
target weight, body size, or appearance — the curriculum is weight-neutral by design, and
a "goal" to shrink a body is not wellness, it is harm (S-407). The egalitarian lens: a
movement goal must fit *any* body and ability — walking, rolling, swimming, gardening,
dancing, stretching, carrying, cycling — and the UNESCO charter holds that physical
activity is a right for all, practiced inclusively without discrimination (S-342). The
global lens: movement traditions worldwide (dance, games, martial arts such as tai chi, capoeira, and kalaripayattu, manual work) all
count as movement; the goal is health and joy, not any single sport. The technology lens:
trackers and apps can help record, but they can also distort a healthy relationship with
movement by making it about numbers and comparison — use them only if they help, and never
to rank bodies (see Grade 10's fuller treatment). Emphasize **mastery, not ranking**: a
missed week is information, not a verdict — the learner simply restarts, smaller. Model a
goal that is genuinely small, so no learner is set up to fail.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: how does movement shift body state — and therefore
   mood and thinking? Today we turn that into a plan we own.
2. **Meet a good goal (10 min).** A good movement goal is **specific** ("walk or roll
   outside three times a week" — not "move more"), **measurable** (you can say yes or no),
   **realistic** (a small step you can do this week), and built on **function, feeling, or
   skill** — never on weight or appearance. It also names a **reason** that is yours.
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** _Worked example:_ **Goal:** walk or roll outside
   three times a week, starting at ten minutes. **Reason:** I want more energy in the
   afternoon and a clearer head before studying. **Start point:** one ten-minute walk this
   week. **Small steps:** week 1 — two walks; week 2 — three; week 3 — three, a little
   faster or longer if it feels good. **Tracking:** mark each day done and write one word
   for how it felt. **If I miss a week:** I restart smaller — no blame, no quitting.
4. **Draft yours (15 min).** Fill the planner: goal, reason, start point, small steps, and
   a tracking table. Make it yours — your body, your life, your joy.
5. **Test the first step (8 min).** Do the first small step right now if you can — ten
   minutes of walking, stretching, or whatever you chose. Notice how it feels; adjust the
   goal if it was too big or too small.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap planners with a partner. Check: is it specific and
   measurable? Realistic? Built on function/feeling/skill, with no weight or appearance
   language? Fix what needs fixing.
7. **Close (2 min).** One sentence: "I am the owner of this goal, not its employee." Set a
   check-in day.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a pre-written goal with blanks ("___ times a week, starting at ___
   minutes, because ___") and ask the learner to fill it in.
- **Extension:** Add a **recovery** line to the plan — rest days, and what to do if you
   feel sore or tired — the mark of a sustainable goal.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Is the goal specific, measurable, realistic, and
  function/feeling/skill-based, with a tracking plan and a check-in?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed planner, kept in the portfolio; the tracking log is
  filled in over the following weeks and revisited in the unit capstone.

## Home connection

Start the tracking log today. At your check-in, write one honest sentence: what worked,
what did not, and one small change. Bring the log back to the capstone lesson.
