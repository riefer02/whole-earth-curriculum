# Lesson 3 — Media, Peers, and the Messages My Body Hears

## Summary

Learners **evaluate body-image messages** from media and peers using four decode
questions — who made it, what does it want, what is left out, how does it make me feel
about my body. They separate a message's *claim* from its *value*, name retouching and
comparison culture, and practice a steady response to a peer comment.

## Objectives

- Evaluate body-image messages from media and peers and articulate a body-positive or
  body-neutral view of their own body. _(D03.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

You have seen the image a hundred times: a single, narrow kind of body, smooth and edited,
posing with a product as if the product made the body. And you have heard the comment —
"you look different," "that's for people who are fit," or a silent glance at a phone held
up for comparison. These messages feel personal, but they are not really about *you*: they
are made by someone for a purpose, and they leave most bodies out on purpose. Learning to
*decode* a message — to ask who made it, what it wants, and what it left out — turns you
from a person the message happens *to* into a person who reads it and chooses.

## Materials

- Media-message analysis sheet
- Ad or social-post examples

## Preparation

- Copy the analysis sheet and gather the sample messages.
- Retrieval: from earlier media-literacy work (D07 and D10), recall the habit of asking
  *where information comes from* and *what the source wants* (S-063, S-217).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **decode a body-image
message with four questions — (1) Who made it? (2) What does it want me to think, feel, or
buy? (3) What is left out — which bodies, which realities? (4) How does it make me feel
about my own body? — then separate the message's factual claim from its value message.**
This is a *skill*, so model the decode on one example before learners try it themselves
(S-011). Keep the emotional temperature safe: choose sample messages that are analyzable
without being cruel, and never show a learner an image chosen to make them feel bad. The
critical-thinking lens: decoding is the same source-checking habit as fact-checking a
claim (S-063, S-217). The ethics lens: a message that profits from making people feel
their bodies are wrong is a question of values, not evidence. The egalitarian lens: the
"ideal" body is historically and culturally specific — it changes by decade and by place —
so no single body type is the truth (S-401, S-407). The global lens: different cultures
have prized very different bodies, and beauty standards are local, shifting, and often
imported with power attached. The technology lens: retouching and filters now make the
"ideal" literally impossible to match — even the person in the image does not look like
the image. Be explicit that the point is **never** to shame anyone's appearance, choices,
or culture, and never to rank bodies; the point is to loosen a message's grip. Remind
learners that if a message ever hurts, they can step away, and that media influence on how
adolescents see themselves is real and worth taking seriously (S-311). Body image is
sensitive; hold it with care and route distress toward a trusted adult.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What do we already ask about any piece of media — where it comes
   from and what it wants? Today we aim that same skill at messages about *our bodies*.
2. **Meet the four decode questions (8 min).** Write them where all can see: **(1) Who
   made it? (2) What does it want me to think, feel, or buy? (3) What is left out? (4) How
   does it make me feel about my body?** A message is never neutral; it has a maker and a
   purpose.
3. **Model the decode (12 min).** Take one sample ad or post. Run all four questions out
   loud. Notice the techniques: **retouching** (skin smoothed, proportions altered),
   **lighting and composition** that make one body look like the norm, and **comparison
   culture** (the feeling that you should measure yourself against the image). Then
   separate the **claim** ("this product exists") from the **value** ("you are not good
   enough as you are; buy this to be acceptable").
4. **Decode in pairs (15 min).** Choose a second message. Fill in the analysis sheet with
   all four questions and write one sentence naming the claim and the value separately.
5. **Practice the peer response (10 min).** A partner says a common comment — "you look
   different lately," "that's a workout for thin people," "you'd be prettier if…". Practice
   a steady, non-aggressive answer that returns the power to you, such as: "My body is not
   here to be evaluated" or "I decide what my body means." Swap roles.
6. **Close (5 min).** One sentence each: "A message I used to absorb is ___; now I read it
   as ___." Reading a message is not the same as believing it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide two of the four questions answered and ask learners to complete the
  rest; give sentence starters for the claim/value split.
- **Extension:** Find a second message that pushes the *opposite* value (body acceptance)
  and analyze it with the same four questions — showing the tool cuts both ways.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner run all four decode questions and separate
  claim from value?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed analysis sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Find one body-image message you see this week (an ad, a post, a package). Run the four
questions on it in one or two sentences and bring your notes to the next lesson.
