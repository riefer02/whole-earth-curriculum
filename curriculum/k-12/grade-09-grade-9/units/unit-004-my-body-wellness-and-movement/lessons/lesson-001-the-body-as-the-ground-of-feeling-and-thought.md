# Lesson 1 — The Body as the Ground of Feeling and Thought

## Summary

Learners name the **somatic basis of emotion and thought** — the idea that feeling and
thinking are not floating "in the mind" but built *in and with* the body. They trace a
**two-way loop** between body state and mind, study a worked example, run a short
**body-notice**, and record one example of their own.

## Objectives

- Explain the somatic basis of emotion and thought, using an example of how body state
  shapes mood and thinking. _(D03.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Think of a moment your heart pounded before you spoke in front of people, or your stomach
churned before a test, or you snapped at someone when you were tired or hungry and only
realized why later. Those are not failures of self-control — they are your body and your
mind doing what they always do: working as **one loop**. The "feeling" and the "thought"
were shaped *by* the body's state, just as your thoughts can set your heart racing. Today
you learn to name and trace that loop, so you can read yourself more clearly instead of
being pushed around by signals you do not understand.

## Materials

- Body–mind loop handout
- Notebook or journal

## Preparation

- Copy the body–mind loop handout (or draw the loop on the board).
- Retrieval: from Grade 8, recall the nervous system and the fight, flight, or rest
  response — the sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest")
  branches, and how stress shows up in the body (S-338, S-339).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **emotion and thought
have a somatic basis — a body basis. The brain and body form a two-way loop: body state
(heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, gut signals, hormones) shapes mood and thinking,
and thoughts shape body state in return.** This builds directly on Grade 8's nervous
system and stress work, deepening it from "stress happens in the body" to "all feeling and
thinking happen *with* the body" (S-338). Use a **worked example first, then guided
practice** — naming a body signal and tracing the loop is a skill, and explicit modeling
carries skills for novices (S-011). Keep the science honest and hedged: the **somatic
marker** idea (Damasio) holds that bodily responses help guide feeling and decision-making
(S-406), and the **interoception** account (Barrett) holds that the brain integrates
internal body signals to construct emotional experience (S-401) — these are two respected
scientific stories, offered as stories, not gospel. The ethics lens: reading our own
signals honestly is a form of self-respect, and it helps us avoid blaming ourselves for a
body that is just tired or hungry. The egalitarian lens: every body reads signals
differently — some bodies feel signals strongly, some faintly, and some people use
assistive tools — and no way is more legitimate than another. The global lens: cultures
worldwide have long mapped feeling to body parts (a "heavy heart," a "knotted stomach"),
so this is not one region's idea but a widely shared human understanding (S-401). The
technology lens: the devices that now measure our heart rate and breathing are just tools
that make an always-on loop *visible*; the loop itself predates every device. Be careful
and non-shaming: never tell a learner their body is "wrong"; the point is noticing, not
correcting. If a learner notices something distressing (a racing heart that will not
settle, chronic pain), normalize it and quietly route them toward a trusted adult or the
help-seeking paths named in later units.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 8: what are the two branches of the autonomic nervous
   system, and what does each do? (Fight-or-flight / rest-and-digest.) Today we go one
   step deeper: *all* feeling and thinking ride on the body — not just stress.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Look at the diagram. The brain and body are a **two-way
   loop**. Body state — heart rate, breath, muscle tension, gut signals, hormones — reaches
   the brain as **interoception** (your sense of what is happening *inside* you), and it
   colors how you feel and think. Then your thoughts and feelings send signals back out,
   changing your body state. It never runs one way.
3. **Study the worked example (10 min).** _Worked example:_ You slept badly and skipped
   breakfast. **Body state:** heavy eyelids, low energy, empty stomach. **Mood:** flat and
   irritable. **Thinking:** "Everything is pointless today; that comment from a friend must
   have been an insult." Now trace the reverse: you notice the tight shoulders, take one
   slow, long exhale, eat something. **Body state shifts** → the same comment now reads as
   ordinary, and the day looks manageable. The *facts* did not change; the *state you
   thought from* did.
4. **Body-notice (8 min).** Sit or stand comfortably. Close your eyes or soften your gaze.
   Scan slowly: your breath, your shoulders, your jaw, your hands, your stomach. Do not fix
   anything — just notice. What is your body doing right now? Name one signal and a word
   for the mood it leans toward.
5. **Make it yours (15 min).** In your journal, draw the two-way loop with your own
   example: a body state → a mood → a thought, and then a small change in body state (a
   breath, a stretch, water, food, rest) → a different thought. Use the handout's blank
   spaces.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Share your example with a partner. Check together: is the loop
   *two-way*? Did you name a real body signal and a real thought? Fix anything missing.
7. **Close (2 min).** One sentence each: "My body shapes my thinking when…" The body is
   not a container the mind rides in — it is the ground the mind stands on.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the loop pre-labeled and ask learners to fill in only the body
  signal and the mood; give a sentence starter ("When my body feels ___, I think ___").
- **Extension:** Add the third term — *prediction*. The brain guesses what the body will
  need next and that guess becomes part of the feeling (S-401). Write one sentence
  explaining how a prediction could make a neutral event feel dangerous.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a body state, a mood, and a thought,
  and trace the loop in both directions?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The learner's own two-way loop, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Before you sleep tonight, do a one-minute body-notice and write one line: what your body
was doing and how your mood leaned. Bring that line to the next lesson.
