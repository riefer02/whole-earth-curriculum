# Lesson 4 — A Body-Positive or Body-Neutral View of Myself

## Summary

Learners **articulate a body-positive or body-neutral view of their own body**. They
distinguish three resting places — body positivity, body neutrality, and body acceptance —
and the shared idea underneath them all: *appearance is not your worth, and your body's
value is not up for a vote.* They draft a short personal statement that is genuinely
theirs, not borrowed from media or peers.

## Objectives

- Evaluate body-image messages from media and peers and articulate a body-positive or
  body-neutral view of their own body. _(D03.S1.09.02)_

## Connection

You do not have to love every part of your body every day to live at peace with it. Some
people find power in loving their body loudly — that is **body positivity**. Others find
peace by simply *not making their appearance the center of their worth* — that is **body
neutrality**: your body is not the most important or most interesting thing about you.
Both are real resting places, and both rest on the same floor: *appearance is not your
worth.* Today you find your own words for the view you already half-hold, so the message
you carry is yours — not the one the ads sold you.

## Materials

- Body-view statement template
- Notebook or journal

## Preparation

- Copy the statement template.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, recall the four decode questions and the claim/value split.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **body positivity and
body neutrality are two different, valid ways to hold a steady view of one's own body —
positivity says "I love and celebrate my body," neutrality says "my body's appearance is
not what makes me valuable" — and both rest on body acceptance: appearance is not your
worth, and your body's value is not up for a vote.** This is a *values and identity* lesson
more than a skill, so use inquiry and reflection, but still model the statement before
learners draft (S-011). Treat the research carefully and honestly: positive body image is
a real, studied construct — it is *not* the same as "thinking you are attractive," and it
is measured by things like appreciating your body's function, filtering body-image
information, and kindness toward your body (S-407). The ethics lens: how we speak to and
about our own bodies is a practice of respect that we model for others. The egalitarian
lens: the "ideal" body is historically and culturally specific, so no one body type is
closer to some true standard — and *every* body, including bodies with disabilities,
illness, or assistive devices, is a full and legitimate body (S-407, S-411). The global
lens: cultures worldwide have carried many standards of beauty; the modern, media-driven,
single standard is one recent and powerful story, not a law of nature. Hold the room
safely: **sharing is always optional**, never required; a learner who is not ready to say
"positive" things about their body can rest at *neutral* — that is complete, not a lesser
version. Never ask anyone to rank or evaluate a peer's body. If body image is a point of
real pain for a learner, connect them to a trusted adult or the help-seeking paths named
later in the year.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what is the difference between a message's claim and
   its value? Today we write a message of our own — to ourselves — and make sure it is
   really ours.
2. **Meet the three views (10 min).** Read them on the spectrum: **Body negativity** —
   appearance is constantly measured and found lacking (the message the ads want you to
   carry). **Body neutrality** — my appearance is not the measure of my worth; my body is
   the home I do things with, not a project to fix. **Body positivity** — I can appreciate,
   even celebrate, my body for what it is and what it does. Both of the last two are
   healthy resting places; neither is "more correct." You may rest differently on different
   days.
3. **Model the statement (8 min).** _Worked example (neutral):_ "My body is not here to be
   evaluated. It carries me through my day — it lets me think, move, laugh, and care for
   the people I love. That is what makes it mine, and that is enough." _Worked example
   (positive):_ "I appreciate my body for what it does for me — my legs that carry me, my
   hands that make things, my voice that speaks up. I do not need to match an image to be
   whole." Notice: neither sentence mentions weight, shape, or comparison.
4. **Draft yours (15 min).** Use the frames to write your own two-to-four sentence
   statement. Aim for: **(a)** what your body *does* or *makes possible*, **(b)** a line
   that is *yours* (a true memory or fact of your life), and **(c)** no appearance-ranking.
   Rest at neutral if positivity does not feel true today.
5. **Self-check and peer-check (8 min).** Read it to yourself. Ask: Is it mine, or is it a
   borrowed slogan? Is it kind? Would it hold on a hard day? If you wish, share with a
   partner and help each other replace any appearance-ranking with a function line.
6. **Close (9 min).** Write one line you can say to yourself on a hard day, and underline
   it. You do not have to love every part of your body to live at peace with it.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the single-sentence frame "My body is good at ___ and that is enough"
  and fill the blank with a real thing the learner's body does.
- **Extension:** Write a second statement as if you were saying it to a friend who is
  struggling — then notice what you would offer them that you might withhold from
  yourself.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation/self):** Does the statement name function over form, avoid
  appearance-ranking, and sound genuinely the learner's own?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The personal body-view statement, kept in the portfolio (shared
  only if the learner chooses).

## Home connection

Put your underlined line somewhere you will see it this week — a note, a phone lock screen,
a page in your journal. Notice one moment it helped and be ready to say it from memory.
