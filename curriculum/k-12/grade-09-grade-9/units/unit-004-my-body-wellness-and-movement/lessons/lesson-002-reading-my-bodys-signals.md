# Lesson 2 — Reading My Body's Signals

## Summary

Learners practice **reading body signals accurately** — hunger, fatigue, stress — and
tracing how each silently steers mood and thinking. They build a shared signal list, study
a worked example, run a guided **body scan**, and log their own signals with a simple,
non-judgmental response for each.

## Objectives

- Explain the somatic basis of emotion and thought, using an example of how body state
  shapes mood and thinking. _(D03.S1.09.01)_

## Connection

Have you ever argued with someone and only an hour later realized you were just hungry?
Or reread a text message you thought was rude, then noticed you had slept four hours and
suddenly it read fine? Your body was talking the whole time — you just had not been
listening. Reading your own signals is like checking the weather before you trust a mood:
"Am I angry at them, or am I running on an empty tank?" Naming the signal does not make it
go away, but it stops it from steering you in secret. Today you practice that listening.

## Materials

- Signal cards
- Notebook or journal

## Preparation

- Copy or make the signal cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall the two-way body–mind loop and the word
  *interoception* (your sense of what is happening inside you).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The skill to land: **noticing a body signal
(hunger, fatigue, stress), naming it, and tracing how it steers mood and thinking — then
choosing a small, non-judgmental response (water, food, a slow breath, rest).** This is a
*skill*, so use a **worked example first, then guided practice** (S-011). The
critical-thinking lens: this is the same move as checking a source before trusting a
claim — check the body state before trusting a thought. The ethics lens: reading your own
signals is self-respect, not self-indulgence; it is the opposite of the old message that
we should "push through" and ignore the body. The egalitarian lens: bodies signal
differently — some people feel hunger as a sudden crash, others as a slow fade; some
people use assistive devices and cannot feel certain signals, and that is not a deficit.
The global lens: every culture has words for reading the body's needs, and no one way of
naming or responding is more legitimate. Hold the tone as **noticing, never judging**: a
learner who notices they are stressed or tired is doing it right; the lesson never grades
how "calm" anyone gets. Remind learners that a body scan can sometimes surface distress;
if a learner becomes uncomfortable, they may pause or step out, and the facilitator or a
trusted adult can follow up. Keep all examples free of weight, calories, or "should"
language about food or rest — a small snack is fuel, not a moral act.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what is the body–mind loop, and what is
   *interoception*? Today we get better at the "listening" side of the loop.
2. **Build the signal list (10 min).** With a partner, name body signals you have felt:
   tight jaw, shallow breath, empty stomach, heavy eyes, warm face, clenched fists, a
   flutter in the chest. Add one mood or thought each signal tends to steer toward. Share
   the best ones with the group and build one class list.
3. **Study the worked example (8 min).** _Worked example:_ **Signal:** my shoulders are up
   by my ears and my breath is shallow. **Mood:** anxious, on edge. **Thought:** "I'm going
   to mess this up." **Response (not a fix, just a shift):** one slow, long exhale, drop
   the shoulders, a sip of water. **Then:** the same task looks a little more doable. The
   thought was not the truth — it was the state talking.
4. **Guided body scan (8 min).** Sit or stand comfortably. Soften your gaze or close your
   eyes. Move your attention slowly from your jaw to your shoulders, your breath, your
   stomach, your hands, your legs. At each stop, ask only: *what is happening here?* Name
   one signal clearly in your mind.
5. **Log it (15 min).** In your journal, make a three-column table for three signals:
   **Signal → Mood/thought it steers → Small response.** Use real ones from your body scan
   or your day. Keep responses kind and practical: water, food, a breath, rest, moving,
   talking to someone.
6. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap logs with a partner. Check: is the signal concrete? Does
   the response treat the body with care rather than blame? Fix anything harsh or vague.
7. **Close (4 min).** One sentence each: "Next time I feel ___, I'll first check if I'm
   ___, ___, or ___." Listening to the body is a skill, and like any skill, it grows with
   practice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give three pre-written signals and ask learners to match each to a mood and
  a response; use a fill-in ("When my body feels ___, my mood leans ___, so I can ___").
- **Extension:** Add a fourth column — *was the thought accurate?* — and write how checking
  the body state changed (or did not change) your answer.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name a concrete signal, trace it to a mood
  or thought, and propose a kind response?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The three-row signal log, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

This week, catch one moment where a mood might actually be a body state (hunger, thirst,
short sleep). Write one line about what you noticed and what you did. Bring it to the next
lesson.
