# Lesson 8 — Hunger, Food Security, and Health Around the World

## Summary

Learners **explain how hunger, food security, and unequal access to healthy food shape
health outcomes around the world**. They learn the four pillars of food security —
availability, access, utilization, stability — and the key distinction that *hunger is not
usually a lack of food on Earth but a failure of access and fairness.* They write a short
food-security reflection and gather the unit's threads.

## Objectives

- Explain how hunger, food security, and unequal access to healthy food affect health
  outcomes around the world. _(D03.S3.09.02)_

## Connection

There is more than enough food grown on Earth to feed everyone — and yet hundreds of
millions of people still go hungry, not because food does not exist, but because it does
not *reach* them, or they cannot *afford* it, or a shock (a drought, a war, a lost job)
took away what they had. That gap — between the food that exists and the food people can
actually get — is the heart of **food insecurity**, and it lands hardest on health:
hunger stunts growth, weakens immunity, and makes every illness harder to fight. Learning
this does not make you powerless; it makes you someone who can see a *system* where others
see only a shortage. Today you name the system and its pillars.

## Materials

- Food-security diagram
- Notebook or journal

## Preparation

- Copy the food-security diagram.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall the food-system stages and that the system's benefits
  and harms fall unevenly.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **food security rests
on four pillars — availability (enough food exists), access (people can get and afford
it), utilization (the food is safe and nourishing, and bodies can use it), and stability
(all three hold over time) — and hunger is usually a failure of access and fairness, not a
shortage of food on Earth.** The evidence: the FAO estimates that roughly 1 in 11 people
worldwide faced hunger, and that billions more faced moderate or severe food insecurity —
figures to state accurately and with the caveat that they shift year to year (S-409, S-119).
Hold the distinction firmly: *evidence* (the numbers, the four pillars) vs. *values* (that
everyone deserves enough to eat — a widely shared commitment, stated as a commitment, not
a fact). The egalitarian lens: unequal access is not natural or inevitable — it is made by
prices, policies, distance, and power, and it can be unmade. The global lens: food
insecurity looks different everywhere — a drought in one place, an unaffordable market in
another, a disrupted supply chain in a third — but the pillars are the same worldwide. The
environment lens: climate shocks (droughts, floods) are now a major driver of food
insecurity, connecting this lesson to the year's climate work (S-409). The community lens:
mutual aid, school meals, food banks, and community gardens are real, human-scale answers
people build together. The ethics lens: ending with *agency, not despair* is essential —
learners should leave knowing something they could *do* (support a food drive, grow food,
learn and speak about access), never with nihilism. Avoid any framing that shames poverty,
blames individuals for their hunger, or moralizes about food.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: what are the six stages of a food system, and who
   does the system treat unevenly? Today we look at the biggest unevenness of all: who
   gets fed, and who does not.
2. **Meet the four pillars (10 min).** Study the diagram. **Availability** — enough food
   is produced. **Access** — people can get it and afford it. **Utilization** — it is safe
   and nourishing, and bodies can use it (which needs clean water and health too).
   **Stability** — all of this holds through the year and through shocks. Remove one
   pillar and the whole table wobbles.
3. **Study the key distinction (10 min).** The world grows enough food for everyone — yet
   roughly 1 in 11 people still face hunger (S-409). So hunger is usually **not** a
   shortage of food on Earth; it is a failure of **access and fairness** — price, distance,
   conflict, climate, and inequality decide who gets fed. This is the difference between
   "there is not enough" and "it does not reach everyone."
4. **Apply it (15 min).** With a partner, take two contrasting situations — a drought-hit
   region, and a city where healthy food is sold but too expensive for many. For each,
   name which pillar fails and how that shapes health (hunger, weakened immunity, illness
   that is harder to fight). Write your reasoning.
5. **Write your reflection (10 min).** In your journal, finish these three sentences: "Food
   security means…", "Hunger is usually a failure of…, not a lack of…", and "One thing I
   could do, even small, is…". This reflection is your portfolio artifact.
6. **Close (5 min).** Gather the unit: one sentence each connecting the threads — my body
   shapes my mind (Lessons 1–2), I hold my own view of my body (Lessons 3–4), movement is
   a lever I own (Lessons 5–6), and food is a system of fairness (Lessons 7–8). We do not
   meet the world's hardest truths with despair — we meet them with understanding and a
   thing we can do.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give the four pillars pre-defined and ask learners to match each to a
  plain-language example; provide the reflection sentences as fill-ins.
- **Extension:** Research one country's hunger trend and write a short paragraph explaining
  which pillar is most at risk there, with a source.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the four pillars and explain that
  hunger is usually a failure of access, not a global shortage?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The food-security reflection, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask a family member or neighbor where their food comes from and whether getting enough
healthy food has ever been hard. Write one sentence about what you heard, and one thing
your community already does (or could do) to widen access.
