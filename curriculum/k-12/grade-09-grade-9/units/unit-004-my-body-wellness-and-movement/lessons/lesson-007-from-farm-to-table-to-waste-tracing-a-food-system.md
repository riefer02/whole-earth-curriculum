# Lesson 7 — From Farm to Table to Waste: Tracing a Food System

## Summary

Learners **analyze a food system from farm to table to waste**, tracing one food through
six stages — produce, process, transport, sell, eat, waste — and naming the effects of
each stage on **health, workers, and the environment**. They distinguish food that is
*lost or wasted* from food that is *eaten*, and connect the journey to their own plate.

## Objectives

- Analyze a local food system from farm to table to waste and its effects on health,
  workers, and the environment. _(D03.S3.09.01)_

## Connection

Before it reached your hand, the rice or bread or fruit you ate had a long life you never
saw: someone grew it, someone harvested it, someone packed and moved it, someone sold it,
and somewhere, some of it was thrown away. That whole journey — farm to table to waste —
is a **food system**, and it touches *your health* (what ends up on your plate), *workers*
(who is paid what to grow and carry it), and *the environment* (water, soil, fuel, and
waste along the way). Tracing one food is how you stop seeing a meal as a single object
and start seeing it as a chain of choices and consequences. Today you follow that chain.

## Materials

- Food-system flow sheet
- One food item per learner

## Preparation

- Copy the flow sheet.
- Retrieval: from earlier grades, recall that food comes from plants and animals raised by
  people (S-080), and that it moves through plant, grow, harvest, transport, sell, and
  cook before it reaches a table (S-081).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The concept to land: **a food system is a
chain of stages — produce, process, transport, sell, eat, waste — and each stage has
effects on health (what people eat and its quality), workers (who does the labor, under
what conditions, for what pay), and the environment (water, soil, energy, emissions, and
waste).** This is a *systems analysis* skill, so model the trace on one food, then guide
learners through their own (S-011). The global lens: food moves farther and through more
hands than it once did; the same plate now links a field on one continent to a kitchen on
another (S-081). The environment lens: a large share of food is lost or wasted — roughly a
fifth of the food available to consumers worldwide — which wastes the water, land, and
energy used to produce it (S-410). The ethics and egalitarian lenses: the system does not
treat everyone equally — farm and food workers often do hard, essential work for low pay
and little protection, and the benefits and harms of the chain fall unevenly. The
technology lens: processing, refrigeration, and long-distance transport changed what food
is possible — and created the packaging and fuel costs the chain now carries. Hold the
tone carefully: this is **not** a lesson in shaming anyone's food or family. Every family
eats what it can, within its means and culture; the point is to *understand the system*,
not to grade a plate. Distinguish evidence (how food moves, how much is wasted) from
values (that workers deserve fair pay, that waste matters) throughout.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Where does food come from, and what steps does it pass through
   before it reaches a table? Today we trace those steps and ask what each one does to
   people and the planet.
2. **Meet the six stages (10 min).** Read them on the flow sheet: **produce** (grow and
   harvest), **process** (clean, mill, cook, package), **transport** (move it, often far),
   **sell** (market, shop, stall), **eat** (your table), and **waste** (what is thrown
   away or spoiled). Each stage has effects on **health**, **workers**, and the
   **environment**.
3. **Model the trace (12 min).** _Worked example — rice:_ **Produce:** grown in flooded
   fields, using water and labor. **Process:** milled and bagged. **Transport:** shipped
   long distances, burning fuel. **Sell:** sold at a market or shop. **Eat:** a staple that
   feeds billions. **Waste:** spoiled or thrown-away rice wastes the water and work that
   made it. At each stage, name one effect on health, workers, or the environment.
4. **Trace your own food (15 min).** Choose your food item. Fill the flow sheet through
   all six stages, and write at least one effect on **health**, one on **workers**, and one
   on the **environment** somewhere in the chain.
5. **Peer-check (5 min).** Swap sheets with a partner. Check: are all six stages present
   and in order? Is there at least one effect for each of the three categories? Fix what is
   missing.
6. **Close (8 min).** One sentence each: "Tracing my food changed how I see it because…"
   Then add one small, honest thing a person *could* do — not a rule, just a possibility
   (buy local when possible, waste less, learn where a staple is grown).

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the six stages pre-labeled and ask learners to fill in only the
  effects; give a fill-in ("At the ___ stage, ___ affects ___").
- **Extension:** Compare two foods — a local staple and a heavily processed snack — and
  write one paragraph contrasting their effects on health, workers, and the environment.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner order the six stages and name at least one
  effect on health, one on workers, and one on the environment?
- **Portfolio artifact:** The completed flow sheet, kept in the portfolio.

## Home connection

At your next meal, ask where one ingredient came from and how far it likely traveled.
Write one sentence about what you found. Bring it to the capstone lesson.
