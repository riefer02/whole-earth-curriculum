# Lesson 5 — Naming My Values

## Summary

Learners name the values they *actually* hold, using their own lived choices as
evidence rather than adopting a ready-made list. They learn the difference between a
**value** (what matters; what we ought to do) and a **fact** (how things are), and
they draft the first part of a values-and-purpose statement.

## Objectives

- Articulate their developing values, purpose, and sense of what a meaningful life
  looks like for them. _(D01.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

You can tell what a person values not by what they *say* they care about, but by what
they *spend* — their time, their money, their attention, their courage. The friend who
stays up to help someone is spending sleep on care; the neighbor who fixes the shared
well is spending labor on fairness. Values are the things you are willing to pay for
with your life, in small and large coins. Naming them — your own, not borrowed — is
where a meaningful life begins to take shape.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A values compass (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the values compass; leave its points blank or partially blank.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 4, you named a strength and a growth area with
  evidence. Today you use the same evidence-habit on your *values*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a value is a
commitment about what matters and what we ought to do, discovered from what a person
actually spends — and it is categorically different from a fact, which is how things
are.** Teach the fact/value distinction explicitly with a worked example (S-011),
then let learners name their own values.

The **ethics** lens is the center: values are the stuff of ethics, and the lesson's
key move is philosophical — distinguishing "is" from "ought" (the fact/value
distinction), a distinction learners will need all year and beyond. The
**egalitarian** lens: every learner's values are honored; no value here is ranked
above another, and no learner is told what to value — the curriculum teaches *how* to
think about values, never *what* to value (philosophy §11). The **global** lens:
different cultures and traditions name and weigh values differently — honor, filial
duty, freedom, care, hospitality — and none is a single correct list; the compass
examples are only examples. The **technology & environment** lens: what we actually
spend our attention and money on is now shaped by technology (feeds, ads, convenience)
and by our relationship to the living world (what we consume, what we protect) — those
spending patterns are honest evidence of our values, worth reading with clear eyes.

**Emotional safety.** Values are personal but not as tender as identity; still, never
require a learner to defend a value against a hostile peer. The task is to *name and
understand*, not to debate or rank each other's values. "Pass" and "I'm still figuring
this out" are complete answers.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What did you learn yesterday about naming your growth with
   evidence? Today you turn evidence toward a new question: *what do I value?*
2. **Split the world in two (15 min).** A **fact** is a claim about how things *are*:
   "the reservoir is low." A **value** is a claim about what *matters* or what we
   *ought* to do: "we should share the water fairly." Facts can be checked with
   evidence; values are chosen, defended, and lived. Worked example: sort these five —
   *the river is polluted; polluters should clean it up; many people lack clean water;
   everyone deserves clean water; the well is empty in summer.* Which are facts, which
   are values, and how can you tell? Write the two piles in your journal.
3. **Find your values from your life (20 min).** Do not start from a list. Start from
   your *spending*. Recall three moments when you paid for something with your time,
   attention, comfort, or courage: a time you helped, stood up, waited, practiced,
   protected, or refused. For each, ask: *what mattered enough to pay that price?* Name
   the value inside each moment (care, fairness, truth, courage, loyalty, freedom…).
   Write the value and the moment that shows it.
4. **Draft the compass (10 min).** On your values compass, write your two or three
   strongest values at the points. Then, under each, one short line: what living by it
   looks like for you.
5. **Close (5 min).** Read your compass once more. This is the first page of your
   values-and-purpose statement — not finished, just begun. Put it in your portfolio.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Offer the three "spending" prompts as sentence starters and a short bank
  of value words to choose from *after* naming a moment.
- **Extension:** Test one value against a hard case — a situation where it conflicts
  with another value you hold — and write one sentence about how you would weigh them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name at least two values, each grounded in a
  lived moment, and separate a fact from a value in the sort?
- **Portfolio:** The values compass, gathered toward D01.S2.11.01.

## Home connection

Watch for one small thing a person at home *spends* today — time, effort, attention —
and ask yourself (or them) what value it shows. Share your compass if you wish.
