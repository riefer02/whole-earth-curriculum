# Lesson 7 — My Place in a Global World

## Summary

Learners trace the threads that tie their own daily life to people far away — the food
they eat, the water they drink, the climate they share, the work their things
represent, the news on their screens, the medicines they may one day need. They see
themselves as a node in a global web, and they write a first reflection on their place
in it.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their place in a global, interconnected world and what they owe to people
  unlike themselves. _(D01.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Pick up the shirt you are wearing, or the fruit you ate this morning, or the phone in
your pocket. Somewhere, a hand you will never shake helped make it, and somewhere the
earth gave something for it — water, cotton, metal, energy. You are not a separate
island; you are a knot in a net that wraps the whole planet. Seeing that net honestly —
where its benefits and its burdens fall — is the beginning of knowing what you owe to
people who do not look or live like you.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A global-web diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the global-web diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 5–6, recall the values and the direction you named.
  Today we ask what that direction owes to *distant others* — people unlike you.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **daily life is made
of concrete threads to people far away, and tracing those threads — with an honest eye
to where benefit and burden fall — grounds the abstract idea of a "global world" in
something real.** Teach with a worked example of one object's journey (S-011).

The **global & anthropological** lens is the core: this is interdependence as lived
fact, traced through real goods and systems, never romanticized. The **ethics** lens:
once the thread is visible, the question "what do I owe the people on the other end?"
becomes real rather than abstract. The **egalitarian** lens: the benefits and burdens
of these threads are *unevenly* distributed — where one is born, the work available,
and the risks carried are shaped largely by structure, not individual worth (World
Bank poverty/inequality data, S-234; UNDP human-development data, S-283). Keep that
distinction explicit: this is about *systems*, never about ranking people. The
**technology** lens: much of the web runs on technology — global supply chains,
shipping, and screens that carry news and feeling across borders in an hour. The
**environment** lens: every thread also touches the living world — water, soil,
climate — and the people most affected by environmental harm are often those who
contributed least to it; that is a fact to hold alongside the value that they are owed
care.

**Emotional safety.** This lesson can surface guilt. Keep the frame on *connection and
agency*, not blame: a learner is not personally guilty for the structure of the world,
but is capable of choosing how to live within it. "What do I owe?" is an open question,
not an accusation.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What values and direction did you name in Lessons 5–6? Today we
   widen the frame: who else is on the other end of the life you live?
2. **Trace one thread (15 min).** Worked example, together: a cup of rice. It was
   planted, tended, and harvested by farmers — often in a country far from the one
   where it is eaten — then milled, shipped across an ocean, and sold. Every step held
   someone's labor and used water and soil. Trace your own everyday object the same
   way, in three to five steps: *where did its parts come from, whose hands touched it,
   and what did the earth give?*
3. **Draw the web (15 min).** On your global-web diagram, write your object's journey
   at the matching nodes (food, water, climate, work, news, medicine). Then add the
   honesty check: *along this thread, who benefits most, and who carries the risk?*
   Remember — this is about systems, not about ranking people.
4. **First place-in-the-world reflection (15 min).** In your journal, write a short
   reflection: *Here is one concrete way my life is tied to people far away; here is
   where the benefit and the burden fall; and here is one thing I now wonder about
   what I owe them.* Keep it honest and open — a question is a fine way to end.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the web and the reflection in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *I am a knot in a net that wraps the planet.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Trace one object together step-by-step first, with a word bank of
  places and roles.
- **Extension:** Trace a second object and compare the two threads — which is more
  just, and by what measure?

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the reflection trace at least one concrete thread,
  name where benefit and burden fall, and raise a genuine "owe" question?
- **Portfolio:** The global-web and reflection, gathered toward D01.S2.11.02.

## Home connection

Choose one thing in your home and, with someone there, trace its journey together —
out loud, guessing where you are unsure. Notice how much of the world lives in one
room.
