# Lesson 11 — Authorship: Choosing My Project

## Summary

Learners take **authorship** — they choose a personal project or commitment that is
their own, scope it so it can actually be begun, and plan it as a map with milestones
and a check-in. This is the unit's self-directed capstone: the project begins here and
is seen through in the days ahead.

## Objectives

- Take authorship of a personal project or commitment and see it through with
  self-direction. _(D01.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

There is a difference between *having* a good idea and *authoring* it — writing your
own name on it and seeing it through. Most meaningful things in a life are not
assigned; they are chosen, scoped, begun, and carried. A garden, a skill, a small act
of repair in your neighborhood, a thing you will make or learn, a habit you will
build. Authorship is the muscle this unit has been building toward: you decide, you
plan, you begin, and you keep your own word to yourself.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A project map (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the project map.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lessons 9–10, recall the decision loop and the three-part
  move under uncertainty. A project is a *long decision* — you will use both.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **authorship means
choosing, scoping, planning, and beginning a project of one's own — and a project that
can actually be seen through is one that is small enough to start and real enough to
matter.** Teach scoping with a worked example (S-011), then let learners choose
freely.

The **social-emotional** lens: self-direction is a peak skill of adolescence, and
agency is a developmental protective factor — contribution and responsibility build
thriving (Search Institute developmental assets, S-156). The **ethics** lens: a chosen
project carries the chooser's values; invite learners to pick something that *does
something* for others or the world, not only for themselves — though the choice is
theirs. The **egalitarian** lens: projects must be scoped to each learner's real
constraints — time, resources, support — so no learner's plan is set up to fail; a
small, honest project is worth more than a grand, abandoned one. The **global** lens:
a personal project can be local and still connected — a shared garden, a language
learned, a skill passed on — and learners can see their small work as a knot in the
global web they mapped in Lesson 7. The **technology & environment** lens: projects
may use technology as a tool (a document, a recording, a shared space) and may serve
the living world (restore, plant, clean, teach) — but neither is required; what matters
is that it is *theirs*.

**Emotional safety.** Choice must be real and free. No project is too small; no learner
is judged for a modest scope. Some learners may not know what to choose — offer a menu
of examples, and allow "a commitment, not a project" (a practice, a promise, a habit).
The word is *authorship*, not *achievement*.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What values and direction have you named this unit? A good
   project points the way your compass already points.
2. **Meet the shape of a project (15 min).** A project worth authoring is **small
   enough to start and real enough to matter.** Worked example: not "save the river,"
   but "spend one afternoon a week for three weeks helping clear one stretch of bank."
   Not "learn to code," but "build one small thing with help this month." Write the
   two-part test in your journal: *small enough to start; real enough to matter.*
3. **Choose yours (15 min).** Choose one project or commitment — yours alone, chosen
   freely. It might build on your values, your direction, or your place in the world.
   Write: *what I will do, why it matters to me, and who or what it serves.*
4. **Draw the map (15 min).** On the project map, write your project at "start," then
   break it into **three milestones** — the next small steps you can actually take —
   each with a note or date. Put a **check-in** flag at the midpoint: a moment you will
   stop, look, and adjust. Write one sentence about what you will do at the check-in if
   a milestone has slipped (remember: *information, not a verdict*).
5. **Close (5 min).** Sign your name on the map — this is authorship. Put it in your
   portfolio. Your project begins now, and you will check on it in the next lesson and
   in the days after.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Offer a menu of example projects and a pre-drawn map with three blank
  milestone boxes; help scope each to "one week, small, real."
- **Extension:** Add a "risk and repair" column to the map: for each milestone, one
  thing that could go wrong and the fix.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the project pass the two-part test (small enough to
  start, real enough to matter), with three concrete milestones and a check-in?
- **Portfolio:** The signed project map, launching the capstone toward D01.S3.11.02.

## Home connection

Tell someone at home your project in one sentence and ask them to be a witness — not a
judge, just someone who knows you are doing it. A witness helps authorship hold.
