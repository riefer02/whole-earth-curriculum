# Lesson 6 — Purpose and a Meaningful Life

## Summary

Learners meet **purpose** as a *direction* the self moves toward — not a single
destination to arrive at by a deadline. They learn that purpose grows by trying and
reflecting rather than by sudden certainty, that open exploration is a normal and
healthy stage, and that only a minority of young people feel a clear sense of purpose
yet. They extend their values-and-purpose statement.

## Objectives

- Articulate their developing values, purpose, and sense of what a meaningful life
  looks like for them. _(D01.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

A compass does not tell you the exact house you will live in; it tells you which way is
north so you can walk. Purpose is like that: it is a *direction*, not a destination.
You do not need to know your one career or your one calling today — very few people do
at your age, and many people's purpose changes across a life. You only need to notice
which way you keep turning: toward making things, toward helping people, toward
understanding, toward protecting the living world. That turning is the beginning of a
meaningful life.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A path-to-purpose diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the path-to-purpose diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 5, what is the difference between a fact and a
  value, and which values did you name? Today we ask what *direction* those values
  point you toward.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **purpose is a
direction, not a destination; it grows by trying and reflecting; open exploration is
normal and healthy; and "I don't know yet" is a complete answer.** Teach the
distinction between *direction* and *destination* with a worked example (S-011).

The **social-emotional** lens: identity consolidation is the developmental work of
adolescence, and Erikson framed this stage as one of *exploration toward commitment*
(S-155); Marcia's research showed that open exploration ("moratorium") is a normal,
healthy part of identity formation, not a failure (S-403). The **ethics** lens: a
purpose worth having is one that considers its effect on others — meaning and
harm are connected questions. The **egalitarian** lens: research finds only about one
in five young people has a clear sense of purpose yet, and purpose grows with support
and reflection (Damon, S-400) — so *uncertainty is the norm, not a deficit*, and no
learner is behind for not having their life figured out. The **global** lens: what a
"meaningful life" means differs across cultures — some center contribution to family,
others to a craft, others to a faith, others to a cause — and none is above another.
The **technology & environment** lens: our imagined future selves are shaped by what we
see around us, including screens (which futures look visible and possible?), and a
meaningful life now often includes the question of what we owe the living Earth.

**Emotional safety.** Purpose is loaded with pressure in many cultures. Explicitly
*lower* the pressure: this is not the day to choose a career. "I don't know yet" is
celebrated, not repaired. No learner should leave feeling behind.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Which values did you name yesterday? Keep them where you can see
   them — they are the needle of your compass.
2. **Direction, not destination (15 min).** Worked example: a person who keeps turning
   toward *helping others learn* might become a teacher, a coach, a trainer, a parent
   who reads to children — many destinations, one direction. The **direction** is the
   "toward"; the **destination** is the specific place. Also, psychologists who study
   identity describe an open, exploring stage — "moratorium" — as a normal and healthy
   part of forming who you are (Marcia, S-403): not having chosen yet is *part of
   choosing*. Write the two words and the difference in your own words.
3. **Notice your turning (15 min).** What have you repeatedly turned toward — even
   before anyone asked? Making or fixing things? Understanding how things work?
   Defending someone? Creating beauty? Growing food? Asking big questions? Write the
   "toward" you keep feeling, and one small piece of evidence (a project, a habit, a
   choice you keep making).
4. **Imagine possible selves (15 min).** Picture two or three *possible selves* — the
   people you hope to become (Markus & Nurius, S-399). For each, write one line: what
   that self cares about, and what it *does*. Then circle the direction they share in
   common. That shared direction is the seed of your purpose.
5. **Close (5 min).** Add today's reflection to your values-and-purpose statement.
   Remember: about one in five young people feels a clear purpose now, and purpose
   grows by trying and reflecting (Damon, S-400) — so wherever you are on that path is
   exactly a place to be.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Offer sentence frames ("I keep turning toward ___; I know because ___")
  and a list of possible directions to consider.
- **Extension:** Write one paragraph connecting a value (Lesson 5) to a direction
  (today) to a possible self, naming what each link adds.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the reflection name a *direction* (not only a job), with
  one scrap of evidence, and hold "not yet" as acceptable?
- **Portfolio:** The direction/possible-selves reflection, extending the
  values-and-purpose statement toward D01.S2.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: "What did you think you'd become, and what did you actually turn toward?"
Listen for how their purpose changed — that is normal, and it is the point.
