# Lesson 1 — The Weather of a Group

## Summary

Learners meet the idea of an **emotional climate**: the shared feeling that runs
through a group, a crowd, or a whole society, larger than any one person and yet made
of many people's feelings at once. They practice *reading* a climate before they try
to change one, and they set up the journal that will become this unit's portfolio.

## Objectives

- Articulate the emotional climate of a group or society and how collective emotions
  such as hope, fear, and grief arise. _(D01.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Walk into a room right after someone has shared wonderful news, and you feel it before
anyone says a word. Walk into a market on a day when trade is bad, or onto a bus the
morning after a storm, and a different air is there. Every group has a *weather* — a
shared feeling made of everyone's feelings at once. Learning to read that weather is
the first step to understanding it, and later, to shaping it.

## Materials

- A journal or notebook (this becomes your unit portfolio)
- An emotional-climate chart, printed or drawn (the three collective emotions, with
  blank rows for your own examples)

## Preparation

- Arrange seats in a circle or a way everyone can see the whole group.
- Draw or print the emotional-climate chart; have a blank page ready in the journal.
- Retrieval (spaced): in earlier grades you learned to name your own feelings, to hold
  two feelings at once, and to notice how culture shapes which feelings are shown. This
  unit turns the same skills *outward*, toward whole groups and societies.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an emotional
climate is a shared feeling bigger than any one person, and it can be read — named
with evidence — before it is judged or changed.** Teach *reading* as a skill with a
worked example (S-011), then let learners observe a real group.

The **social-emotional** lens is the ground here: reading a climate draws on the
emotional literacy learners built in earlier grades (CASEL self- and social awareness,
S-001; RULER's label-emotions-with-precision skill, S-015). The **ethics** lens:
how we *name* a group's feeling can honor or flatten it — "they're all panicking"
is very different from "there is fear here, and it comes from a real threat." The
**egalitarian** lens: a climate is felt unevenly — the same news lifts some and
wounds others, and who feels hope or fear is not distributed equally. The **global**
lens: climates span nations (the mood of a country after a victory or a loss), so
"reading the room" scales up to reading the world. The **technology & environment**
lens: screens now carry a group's weather across borders — a storm of feeling on one
continent can be felt in a room on another within an hour; and the living environment
has its own shared states (a drought, a flood) that shape a community's emotional
weather.

**Emotional safety first.** This unit touches identity, values, and purpose, which are
vulnerable. From this first day, make the ground rules explicit and non-negotiable:
(1) no one is ever required to share a private feeling or story — "pass" is a complete
answer; (2) we observe and name *climates* and *groups*, never to single out or shame a
person; (3) there are no wrong feelings, only feelings; (4) "not yet" is information,
never a verdict. Hold collective emotion as a *question to explore*, not a fact to
accept (philosophy §11). If a learner steps away or stays silent, honor that.

## Procedure

1. **Gather and recall (5 min).** Think back, without speaking yet: in earlier grades,
   what did you learn about your *own* feelings — naming them, holding two at once,
   noticing where they live in your body? Keep one of those skills in mind; today we
   turn the same skill toward a whole group.
2. **Meet the idea (10 min).** An **emotional climate** is the shared feeling of a
   group, bigger than any one person, made of many feelings at once. Worked example:
   after a hard loss, a whole community can carry grief you can feel in the air — the
   lowered voices, the slower steps, the shared silence. That is a *climate of grief*.
   Two others named in this unit are **hope** (the shared lift when a real opening
   appears) and **fear** (the shared tightening when a threat looms). Look at the
   chart; keep it open.
3. **Read a real group (15 min).** Notice the room you are in right now — by sight,
   sound, or feel. Without naming any one person, write three words that capture the
   *climate* of the group — the shared feeling — and one clue you noticed that led you
   to each word (a tone, a silence, a speed, a posture — heard as easily as seen). If
   you are alone, observe any group you were in today.
4. **Name where it comes from (15 min).** For each climate word, ask: *where does this
   shared feeling come from?* It usually comes from something the group shares — a
   history, a condition, a piece of news, a threat, a promise. Write one sentence for
   each: "the group feels ___ because ___." This is the beginning of explaining *how*
   collective emotions arise.
5. **Open the portfolio (5 min).** Label the first page of your journal "Feelings,
   Identity & Purpose." This is where every piece of this unit's work will gather.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it aloud or write it: a climate is *shared*, and it is
   *readable*. Name one group whose weather you would like to understand better this
   unit.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Offer a starter bank of climate words (heavy, light, tense, warm,
  hopeful, worried) and one worked example to copy before writing their own.
- **Extension:** Compare two climates — the same room before and after news, or a
  stadium crowd versus a vigil — and name what changed the weather.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name the climate of a group and give at
  least one evidence clue, rather than only describing their own feeling?
- **Portfolio (unit):** The journal is begun; the climate reading becomes the first
  artifact toward D01.S1.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to describe the "mood" of a place they were in today — a shop, a
queue, a workplace, a bus — and what made it that way. Notice: they are reading a
climate, the same skill you practiced today.
