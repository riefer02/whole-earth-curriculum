# Lesson 8 — What I Owe to People Unlike Me

## Summary

Learners widen their circle of care. They learn an honest truth — empathy is real but
*partial*: it runs strongest toward the near, the familiar, and the single named face —
and that reasoned **compassion**, **solidarity**, and **mutual aid** can carry care
farther than feeling alone can. They write a statement of what they owe to people
unlike themselves, and why.

## Objectives

- Reflect on their place in a global, interconnected world and what they owe to people
  unlike themselves. _(D01.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

It is easy to care about your own family — their faces are in front of you. It is
harder to care about someone you will never meet, in a place you will never see, whose
life is nothing like yours. Yet your food, your climate, and your future are tied to
exactly those people. The question this lesson asks is one of the oldest and hardest
questions humans have: *how wide is my care, and can I choose to widen it?* Feeling is
one way to care — but it is not the only way, and it is not always the widest.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A widening-circles diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the widening-circles diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 7, recall one thread that ties you to distant others.
  Today we ask what that thread *owes*.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **empathy is real but
partial — strongest toward the near and the named — so widening care toward distant,
unlike others requires reasoned compassion and solidarity, not only feeling.** Teach
the empathy/compassion distinction with a worked example (S-011); hold the value
question open, never as a single right answer.

The **ethics** lens: "what do I owe distant others?" is a core question of ethics, and
the lesson invites learners to reason about it rather than be told the answer. The
**egalitarian** lens: empathy's documented limits are themselves a form of inequality —
feeling is not distributed fairly, so relying on feeling alone would leave distant
others uncared-for; reasoned commitment is a more reliable equalizer. The **global**
lens: mutual aid and solidarity are practices found across cultures and times (the
harambee, gotong royong, minga, and barn-raising traditions documented in earlier
units), and they show that care can be *structured* and *chosen*, not only felt. The
**technology** lens: screens both help and hinder — they can bring a distant face
close enough to feel for, and they can flood us until we numb (the psychic-numbing
research, Slovic, S-414). The **environment** lens: the circle can widen past humans —
to future generations and the living world — a widening many traditions already treat
as owed, not optional.

**Emotional safety.** "What do I owe?" can feel like a demand for guilt or sacrifice.
Frame it as *agency and choice*: the question is how wide a learner *chooses* to care,
and there is no correct size of circle to report. No forced disclosure of personal
beliefs; "I am still working this out" is a complete answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What thread ties you to people far away? Hold it in mind.
2. **Meet the circle (10 min).** A **moral circle** is the set of beings whose needs
   count in your choices. Everyone's circle has a near center — family, friends — and a
   far edge — strangers, distant others, future generations, other species. The
   question is where, and how far, you choose to draw it.
3. **Feel, then reason (15 min).** Two truths, side by side.
   - *Empathy* — feeling with another — is real but **partial**. Research shows it runs
     strongest toward those who look like us and toward one named face, and it weakens
     as numbers grow (Bloom, S-413; Slovic's identifiable-victim effect and psychic
     numbing, S-414). Feeling alone will not carry care to millions.
   - *Compassion* — the reasoned wish to reduce suffering — and *solidarity* — acting
     with others across distance — do not depend on feeling in the same way. You can
     commit to fair treatment of a person you will never meet. Worked example: a
     campaign that keeps a fair wage in a supply chain cares about people no one will
     ever see; it runs on commitment and structure, not on tears.
   Write the distinction in your own words.
4. **Choose your care (20 min).** On the widening-circles diagram, mark where your care
   already reaches, and where you want it to reach next. Then write a short statement:
   *Here is what I think I owe to people unlike myself, here is why, and here is one
   concrete thing I could do.* It may be small — that is fine; small, chosen, sustained
   care outruns large, fleeting feeling.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the circles and the statement in your portfolio. Remember:
   the circle widens by *choice and practice*, not by guilt.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Offer a starter set of "one concrete thing" examples (fair-trade
  choice, listening, sharing a skill, speaking up) and sentence frames.
- **Extension:** Analyze a real solidarity or mutual-aid effort and name where it runs
  on feeling and where on structure.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the statement name what the learner owes distant others,
  give a *reason*, and include one concrete, chosen action?
- **Portfolio:** The widening-circles statement, gathered toward D01.S2.11.02.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Is there a person or place you help even though you'll never
meet them?" Listen for whether their care runs on feeling or on commitment.
