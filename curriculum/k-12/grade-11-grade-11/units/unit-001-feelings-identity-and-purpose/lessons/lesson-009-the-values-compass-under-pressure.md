# Lesson 9 — The Values Compass Under Pressure

## Summary

Learners meet a **decision loop** — a five-step discipline for acting in line with
their values even when pressure is high: *pause, name your values, consider your
options, choose, reflect.* They practice it on real decisions, notice how pressure
(including peer pressure) tries to skip the loop, and begin a values-aligned decision
log.

## Objectives

- Make decisions that align their actions with their values, even under pressure or
  uncertainty. _(D01.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

The hardest decisions are the ones that arrive *fast* and *loud* — a friend dares you
to join in, a crowd is angry and wants you to agree, a chance to win asks you to cut a
corner. In those moments the pressure is doing everything it can to make you *skip*
the thinking. A compass is useless if you never look at it. This lesson gives you a
small, repeatable loop for looking — even when everything is moving fast.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A decision-loop diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the decision-loop diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 5, recall the values you named — they are the
  needle of the compass you will use today.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **values alignment
under pressure is a skill, and skills are taught with a worked example and practiced —
the five-step loop is that scaffold (S-011).** Pressure, including peer pressure, is
developmentally real at this age (adolescence is a period of heightened sensitivity to
peers, Steinberg, S-229), so treat resisting it as a *skill to practice*, not a
character flaw to shame.

The **ethics** lens: this is responsible decision-making made explicit (CASEL, S-001)
— the loop forces a pause in which "what is right?" and "what do I value?" are actually
asked. The **egalitarian** lens: a decision is not only about the chooser — the loop's
"consider options" step includes *who is helped and who is hurt*, so fairness is built
into the discipline rather than bolted on. The **global** lens: the same loop scales —
communities and nations under pressure make decisions the same way, and learners can
read a public decision (a policy, a movement's choice) with the same five steps. The
**technology & environment** lens: pressure now arrives through screens (a fast-moving
post, a group chat, an algorithm that rewards reacting), and decisions now often carry
environmental weight (what to buy, what to throw, what to stand for) — the loop works
for all of it.

**Emotional safety.** Use decisions that are real but not traumatic. Do not require any
learner to disclose a private dilemma; they may work with a hypothetical or a public
example. Celebrate the *process*, not the "right" answer — there is often no single
right answer, only a more or less honest one.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Which values did you name in Lesson 5? Write your top two where
   you can see them.
2. **Meet the loop (15 min).** Worked example, together. The situation: your friends
   are laughing at someone and want you to join in. Run the loop:
   1. **Pause** — stop the autopilot; notice the pressure.
   2. **Name your values** — fairness, kindness.
   3. **Consider options** — join in, stay silent, say something, leave. For each: *who
      is helped, who is hurt?*
   4. **Choose** — the option that best fits your values (maybe: "that's not funny;
      leave them be").
   5. **Reflect** — afterwards: did it feel right? what would you keep or change?
   Copy the loop into your journal.
3. **Run your own (20 min).** Choose one real decision you face or recently faced — one
   where the pressure was on. Run it through all five steps, writing each. If you do
   not want to use a private one, use this public one instead: *a rumor about someone
   is spreading in a group chat, and everyone expects you to pass it on.*
4. **Peer-check (10 min).** Trade loops with a partner. Check: are all five steps
   present, and does the "choose" actually follow from the values named? Give one thing
   that is strong and one question that could sharpen it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Start your decision log: one entry, dated, with the five steps.
   This log will grow across the unit and into your portfolio. Remember: pressure wants
   you to skip the loop; the loop is how you keep your compass in your hand.

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking, pointing to a word card, arranging found objects, drawing, or gesturing; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking is still a full response.
- **Support:** Provide the worked example as a fill-in template and a short menu of
  options for step 3.
- **Extension:** Run the loop on a decision where *two of your own values conflict*,
  and write how you weighed them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the learner's decision log show all five steps, with
  the choice traceable to the named values?
- **Portfolio:** The first decision-log entry, gathered toward D01.S3.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask an adult to describe a time they had to decide something hard under pressure. With
them, see if their story maps onto the five steps — and what they learned.
