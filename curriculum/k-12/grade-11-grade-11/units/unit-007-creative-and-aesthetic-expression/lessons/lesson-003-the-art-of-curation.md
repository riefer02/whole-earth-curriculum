# Lesson 3 — The Art of Curation: Selection & Order

## Summary

Learners **curate a small exhibition** — a set of artworks (their own or others') chosen
around a **theme** and placed in a deliberate **order**. They write the reasoning behind
the **selection** (why these works) and the **order** (why this sequence), and discover
that **curation** is itself a form of meaning-making: a composition built out of other
people's compositions.

## Objectives

- Curate a small exhibition of artworks, their own or others', and write the reasoning
  behind the selection and order. _(D07.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of a playlist you made once — you did not just throw songs in a heap; you put them
in an order so the whole evening would *go* somewhere. A gallery wall is the same act in
space: a curator picks which works share a room and which one you meet first, second,
last, so that walking through the room *tells* something. Curation is not organizing; it
is composing — and the medium is other people's art.

## Materials

- Curation planning sheet
- A small set of artworks (6–10 items)
- Art journal

## Preparation

- Gather the set of works: learners' Lesson-2 artworks, plus optional reproductions of
  others' works (respecting that other cultures' arts are met as living traditions, with
  attribution, never as specimens).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 2, how elements and principles steer feeling — curation steers
  feeling *across* several works at once.
- Model one worked example: a three-work mini-exhibition and its written reasoning.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **curation has two
deliberate moves — *selection* (choosing a set that serves a theme) and *order* (arranging
it so the whole says something more than any part); the written reasoning makes both moves
explicit.** Teach the two moves with a worked example and guided practice (S-011); the
theme and the works stay the learner's own.

The **intellectual** lens: the reasoning "why this, why now, why here" is argument — the
same muscle as essay-writing, wielded in space. The **critical-thinking** lens: learners
justify each placement with evidence ("this work comes first because its calm mood sets
up the urgency of the next"), not just taste. The **global** lens: museums and galleries
are one way to keep art before the public eye; markets, homes, shrines, and festivals are
others — curation is a human practice, not a Western invention. The **community** lens:
curation is a gift to an audience; a good exhibition is *for* someone, and learners name
who they are curating for.

Honor living traditions: if learners include another culture's work, attribution and
permission matter — the appreciation-versus-appropriation rule from Grade 10 governs
here (S-056, as a frame for heritage carried hand to hand).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What was the last playlist, shelf, or wall you arranged on
   purpose? What did the *order* do? That is curation.
2. **Meet selection and order (12 min).** **Selection** = choosing a set that serves a
   **theme**. **Order** = arranging it so the sequence builds. Worked example: a
   three-work show on "water." The curator picks a calm image of a full river first, a
   dry cracked riverbed second, and a child carrying a water jug last — so the viewer
   moves from *plenty* to *loss* to *who it costs*. The order, not any single image,
   carries the argument.
3. **Select (12 min).** From the available works, choose 3–5 that serve one theme. On
   your sheet, list what you chose and write one line on why each work earns its place
   (and what you left out, and why).
4. **Order (12 min).** Arrange the chosen works in a sequence that says something more
   than any single piece. Try at least two different orders before you decide. The first
   and last positions carry the most weight — what should a viewer meet first? Leave
   with what?
5. **Write the reasoning (10 min).** In your journal, write the **wall text**: a title
   for the show, a two-sentence theme statement, and one sentence on why this order.
6. **Walk it (4 min).** With a partner, walk your exhibition in the order you chose.
   Ask: *what did the whole say that one piece could not?*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Curate just three works, and use frames ("this one first because…," "this
  one last because…") to build the reasoning.
- **Extension:** Add a *juxtaposition* — two works placed side by side so their contrast
  creates a third meaning — and write what that pairing does.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state the theme, justify each selection,
  and explain why the chosen order (rather than another) serves the theme?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The curation planning sheet plus the written wall text,
  saved for the Lesson 10 gallery.

## Home connection

Arrange four things you already own — photos, books, bowls — into a small "exhibition"
with a title and an order. Write one sentence on why that order. Invite one person to
walk it and say what the whole made them think.
