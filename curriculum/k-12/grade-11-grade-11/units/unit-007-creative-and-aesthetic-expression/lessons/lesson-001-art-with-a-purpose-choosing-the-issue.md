# Lesson 1 — Art With a Purpose: Choosing the Issue

## Summary

Learners open the unit by choosing a **social or environmental issue** that matters to
them and turning it into an **intention**: a clear statement of the **intended effect**
they want their work to have on an **audience**. They meet the unit's throughline — that
art, music, performance, and design can be a **deliberate, ethical voice** — and they
look at real artists who worked in a **series** on a single issue as living history to
honor with questions, never as specimens. Their chosen issue will thread through every
lesson that follows.

## Objectives

- Choose a social or environmental issue, state an artistic intention for it, and
  explain the intended effect on an audience. _(D07.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

A poster taped to a wall, a song that makes you stop, a mural painted over a blank
concrete wall, a mask and a drum at a festival — all of them are *made* by someone for a
reason, and all of them aim to do something to the people who see or hear them. The
question behind every one is the same: *what do I want this to make people feel, think,
or do?* That question is the difference between making a thing and making a thing that
*speaks*.

## Materials

- Issue-choosing worksheet
- Sketchbook or blank paper
- Art journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the issue-choosing worksheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D07.S1.10.02, D07.S2.10.02, D07.S3.10.02), the difference
  between appreciation and appropriation — the rule that will govern how we meet other
  cultures' arts this unit.
- Prepare two or three images of issue-driven artworks or public art for a short,
  shared "what is it trying to make us do?" warm-up.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an artwork addresses
a social or environmental issue and is judged by its intended effect on an audience —
and naming that effect first (before making) is what turns craft into a voice.** This is
the unit's North Star; every later lesson returns to it. Hold "intended effect" as a
*value* the learner chooses, not a fact to be right about (philosophy §5, §11).

The **intellectual** lens: an intention is a testable idea — after making, the learner
asks "did it do what I meant?" The **ethics** lens: choosing an issue is a moral act;
learners weigh consequences for others. The **global** lens: Jacob Lawrence painted a
60-panel series, *The Migration Series* (1940–41), on the Great Migration of African
Americans from the rural South to the urban North — one artist's sustained answer to a
vast social question (S-363); Hokusai's woodblock series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*
(c. 1830–32) turned one mountain seen many ways into a meditation on a place (S-364).
Meet both as documented history, not decoration. The **egalitarianism** lens: every
learner's issue matters equally; no issue is too small to be worth an artist's voice.

Teach the move "issue → intention → intended effect" explicitly with a worked example
(S-011), then let learners choose freely.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10, what is the difference between honoring another
   culture's art and taking it? That rule stays with us all unit.
2. **What does it make you do? (10 min).** Look together at two or three images — a
   mural, a protest poster, a photograph. For each, ask only: *what is it trying to make
   the viewer feel, think, or do?* You are naming **intended effect**.
3. **Choose your issue (15 min).** On your worksheet, list social and environmental
   issues that matter to you — things that make you angry, sad, hopeful, or determined.
   For each, write *who it affects* and *why it matters to me*. Then circle one to carry
   through this whole unit.
4. **Turn it into an intention (15 min).** Write one sentence that completes this frame:
   *"I want my audience to feel ______, think ______, and do ______."* That sentence is
   your **intention**. Worked example: an artist worried about plastic waste writes, "I
   want my audience to *feel* uneasy about how much we throw away, *think* it could be
   different, and *do* one thing — carry a reusable bag."
5. **First sketch (8 min).** Make a fast, rough first sketch for your issue in any form —
   image, sound-shape, movement, or made object. It can be terrible; it is a starting
   point, not a finished thing.
6. **Close (2 min).** Say your intention aloud to a partner, and hear theirs. You are
   now co-authors of your own education, each with an issue worth a voice.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use sentence stems ("I care about ______ because ______") and a short list
  of example issues if choosing feels overwhelming.
- **Extension:** Draft a full three-sentence artist statement, and note one *specific*
  audience you want to reach (a neighborhood, a class, a decision-maker).

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name one issue, state *who it affects* and
  *why it matters to them*, and write a clear intended-effect sentence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed worksheet and the intention sentence,
  saved as the spine of the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask one person at home: *what is one thing in our neighborhood or in the world you wish
someone would fix, and why?* Write down their answer, and note whether it overlaps with
your own issue.
