# Lesson 4 — Composing a Response in Sound

## Summary

Learners **arrange or compose** a short piece of music or organized sound that responds
to their chosen issue. They choose **elements of music** — melody, rhythm, harmony,
timbre, dynamics, tempo, texture, form — deliberately, **notate** the piece simply (as a
**graphic score** if useful), and explain in writing the **intended effect** on a
listener.

## Objectives

- Arrange or compose a piece that responds to a social or environmental issue and explain
  its intended effect. _(D07.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

You already know how sound *does* things to you: a slow, heavy drum can make a room feel
serious; a fast high whistle can make you alert; a single voice after a long silence can
stop a crowd. Composers choose those effects on purpose. A protest song, a film's
soundtrack, a piece of music written for a river or a city — all of them are answers to
one question: *what should this make a listener feel, and how do I build that with sound?*

## Materials

- Sound-composition planning sheet
- Voices, body percussion, and found sound-makers
- Art journal

## Preparation

- Copy the planning sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D07.S2.10.01), composing and recording a piece with a clear
  idea; from Lesson 1, each learner's issue and intention.
- Prepare one demonstration: the same short pattern played three ways (slow/soft, fast/
  loud, with a silence) so the class can *hear* how elements change the effect.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **the elements of music
are the levers a composer pulls — melody, rhythm, harmony, timbre, dynamics, tempo,
texture, form (S-368); pulling a lever changes the listener's feeling, and the written
explanation names which lever and why.** Teach the move "choose two elements → build a
short piece → say the intended effect" with a worked example and guided practice (S-011);
the issue and the feeling stay the learner's own.

The **intellectual** lens: composing is reasoning in sound — a sequence of deliberate
choices, not an accident. The **critical-thinking** lens: learners separate *observation*
("the tempo is slow, the volume soft") from *judgment* ("it feels mournful"). The
**global** lens: music that answers injustice exists across cultures — **reggae** grew
among marginalized groups in western Kingston and became "a voice for all" on injustice,
resistance, love, and humanity (S-450); **nueva canción** in Latin America fused folk
forms with protest and social change, and its musicians faced censorship and violence
under dictatorship (S-449). Meet these as living traditions to honor with respectful
questions, never to imitate carelessly. The **environment** lens: sound can also *be* the
issue — a piece built from recorded water, wind, or traffic lets the environment itself
speak.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, your issue and intention. Today the same intention
   becomes a *sound*.
2. **Meet the levers (12 min).** The elements of music are **melody, rhythm, harmony,
   timbre, dynamics, tempo, texture, and form** (S-368). Worked example: a composer
   responding to noise and haste builds a piece that begins **fast and loud** (tempo,
   dynamics), then drops to a single soft note held long, with **silence** around it — the
   silence is the point. Name the lever, hear the effect.
3. **Choose and plan (8 min).** On your sheet, choose **two elements** to carry your
   message, and sketch a simple **graphic score**: a line or symbols that show what
   happens over time (loud/soft, fast/slow, high/low).
4. **Build it (15 min).** Using voices, body percussion, and found sound-makers, compose
   a short piece (30–60 seconds) that answers your issue. Perform it, listen, and adjust
   until the chosen elements do what you meant.
5. **Notate and explain (10 min).** Write your score so another person could follow it,
   then add two sentences: *which two elements you used, and the intended effect on the
   listener.*
6. **Share and check (5 min).** Play your piece for a partner. Ask only: *what did it
   make you feel, and which moment made that happen?* Compare their answer to your
   intention.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work with one element (loud/soft or fast/slow) and a single repeated
  pattern, then say in one sentence what it should make a listener feel.
- **Extension:** Use three elements, write a fuller score, and note where a listener
  might read the piece differently than intended — and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the two elements they chose, point to
  where each appears in the piece, and explain how each produces the intended effect?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The notated (or recorded) piece plus its written
  intended-effect explanation, saved for the Lesson 10 gallery.

## Home connection

Listen to one piece of music at home or in your neighborhood and name *two* elements the
maker is using. Write one sentence on what the piece is trying to make you feel, and
whether it works on you.
