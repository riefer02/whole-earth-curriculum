# Lesson 7 — Directing a Program: Selection & Staging

## Summary

Learners **direct or curate a short performance program**: they select and order acts,
then make **staging** choices — who stands where, facing what, in what light or sound —
and write the reasoning behind the **selection and staging**. They learn that directing
is curation in time *and* space at once.

## Objectives

- Direct or curate a short performance program and write the reasoning behind the
  selection and staging. _(D07.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

A wedding order of events, a talent show, a festival stage, a play with an intermission —
someone *directed* each one: decided that this act opens and that one closes, that this
performer stands center and that one waits in the wings, that the lights go down here and
the music swells there. Those choices are invisible until you look for them, but they are
the difference between a list of acts and a *show*. Today you make them on purpose.

## Materials

- Program-and-staging planning sheet
- A set of short performance pieces (3–6)
- Art journal

## Preparation

- Gather the pieces: learners' Lesson-6 performances, plus optional remembered or
  described pieces from other traditions (with attribution and respect).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3 and Lesson 5, the two moves of curation; from Lesson 6, how
  spacing and level carry meaning.
- Model one worked example: a two-act program with a stated reason for the order and for
  one staging choice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **directing = curation
applied to performance; it has two deliberate layers — *selection/order* (which acts, in
what sequence) and *staging* (blocking, sightlines, levels, light, sound); the written
reasoning names both.** Teach the layers with a worked example and guided practice
(S-011), using devised-performance and movement pedagogy that presents traditions with
respect (S-260, S-189).

The **intellectual** lens: every staging choice is a reason — "she faces the audience so
we see her reaction" is an argument about where attention should go. The
**critical-thinking** lens: learners test choices against the audience's actual view
(**sightlines**), not just the director's intention. The **global** lens: the
performance program is a worldwide form — festivals, rituals, and ceremonies all select
and stage acts; the Philippine **tinikling** (dancers stepping between bamboo poles in
imitation of the tikling bird) is one living tradition to honor with respectful
questions, not to borrow carelessly (S-116). The **community** lens: a program is
*for* an audience — learners name who they are directing for and make the staging serve
that audience's eyes and ears.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 3 and 5, what were the two moves of curation? Today
   you add a third: **staging**.
2. **Meet selection, order, and staging (12 min).** **Selection** = which acts earn a
   place. **Order** = what sequence they run in. **Staging** = where each performer
   stands, faces, moves, and what light or sound surrounds them. Worked example: a
   two-act program on "welcome." The director opens with the loudest, most inviting act
   (selection + order) and places its performers downstage center, facing the audience,
   while the quieter closing act begins upstage and walks forward — so the audience's
   eyes are met first and led last.
3. **Select and order (12 min).** Choose 2–4 acts that serve one theme, and put them in
   a sequence. On your sheet, write one line on why each act earns its place and why this
   order.
4. **Stage it (12 min).** Sketch a floor plan of the space and the audience. Mark where
   each performer stands and faces, and note one staging choice (a level, a position, a
   silence, a light or sound cue) that serves the theme. Check the **sightlines**: can
   the audience actually see what you want them to see?
5. **Write the reasoning (9 min).** In your journal, write the program's title, a
   two-sentence theme, and one sentence on the reason for the selection, the order, and
   one staging choice.
6. **Show the plan (5 min).** Tell a partner your program, then point to your floor plan
   and explain the one staging choice you are proudest of.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Direct a two-act program with sentence frames ("this act opens because…"),
  and make one staging choice (front vs. back) with a stated reason.
- **Extension:** Add a *transition* between acts (a shared silence, a walk, a sound) and
  write how it links the two, plus a note on any sightline you adjusted for the audience.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner justify the selection and order, and
  explain one staging choice with a reason tied to the theme and the audience's view?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The floor plan plus the written reasoning, saved for the
  Lesson 10 gallery.

## Home connection

Watch any arranged sequence of performance — a ceremony, a match's opening, a festival
program. Name one *staging* choice you noticed (who stood where, who faced whom, what
sound or light framed it) and write one sentence on what it was meant to do.
