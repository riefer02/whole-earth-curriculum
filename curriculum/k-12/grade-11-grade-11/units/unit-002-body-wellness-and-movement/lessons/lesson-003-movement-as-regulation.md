# Lesson 3 — Movement as Regulation: How Moving Shifts Your State

## Summary

Learners move in a way that fits their own body, notice how their state changes, and
learn what the evidence says about *why* movement lifts mood and clears thinking.
They leave able to use movement deliberately — not as punishment or weight control,
but as a way to shift how they feel.

## Objectives

- Use movement deliberately to regulate your own state and explain the evidence for
  why movement affects mood and thinking. _(D03.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of a day you felt heavy, stuck, or irritable — and then you walked somewhere,
played, danced, or carried something, and afterward the world looked different.
Movement is not only about fitness or appearance; it changes your chemistry and your
mind. A farmer after a morning of labor, a student between lessons, a dancer before a
show — all use the same lever: a body in motion talks back to the brain.

## Materials

- Movement-shifts-state chart
- Practice log
- Space to move (with a seated option always available)

## Preparation

- Clear space for safe movement.
- Prepare a short movement sequence that works standing, seated, and wheeling.

## Facilitator note

This lesson combines a **practice** (movement) with **evidence** (mechanisms), so hold
both. Model the movement first, then guide it, then let learners adapt it
(Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006; S-011). **The evidence is hedged, not a sales
pitch:** research shows physical activity improves mood and reduces symptoms of
depression and anxiety, and describes several mechanisms — endorphins (natural
opioid-like chemicals), monoamines (serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline), and BDNF
(brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — but no single chemical is the whole story
(Mikkelsen et al., 2017; S-408). WHO guidance adds that regular activity supports
significant physical and mental health benefits (S-341).

**Anti-ableism and dignity.** Movement is *never* a test of a body. Every movement can
be done seated, wheeling, lying, or at low intensity. No one is required to reach a
target heart rate or intensity. The point is the *shift in state*, not performance.
For learners who cannot move, or can move very little, offer a **non-motor
alternative**: *motor imagery* — vividly imagining a movement you enjoy, which
activates some of the same brain pathways as doing it — or simply noticing the *urge*
to move and the small real shifts of breath and posture without changing them. The
evidence and the before/after noticing are open to every body, moving or still. Never
frame movement as a way to shrink or fix a body — bodies are not problems to solve.

The **ethics** lens: moving to feel well is a kind act toward yourself. The
**egalitarian** lens: movement is for every body, adapted freely, never ranked. The
**global** lens: people move in countless culturally shaped ways — work, dance, sport,
play — and all of them can shift state. The **technology** lens: trackers and apps
count movement, but the benefit does not require a device; it is worth asking whether
a tracker helps or distorts your relationship with your body. The
**critical-thinking** lens: read the evidence carefully — "movement helps" is not the
same as "more is always better."

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From memory: why does slow breathing calm you? Write the
   mechanism in one sentence, then check your log.
2. **Before (5 min).** Mark your state on the spectrum. Write two words for how you
   feel *before* moving.
3. **Move (10 min).** Follow a simple sequence, adapting every part to your body:
   gentle marching or tapping in place, big arm circles, a slow reach up and fold
   forward, a side-to-side sway, a shake-out of hands and feet. Do it seated or
   standing — whatever fits you. Two minutes is enough; more is welcome. If moving is
   not possible or comfortable for you, use motor imagery instead: close your eyes and
   vividly imagine a movement you enjoy, or simply track the small, real shifts of
   breath and posture without changing them.
4. **After (5 min).** Mark your state again. What changed? Did anything feel lighter,
   warmer, clearer, or the same?
5. **The evidence (10 min).** Read or listen to the mechanism: movement can improve
   mood and reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety; possible mechanisms include
   endorphins, monoamines, and BDNF (Mikkelsen et al., 2017; S-408). Discuss: what
   does "one mechanism among several" tell you about how to read health claims?
6. **Explain it back (10 min).** In your log, complete: *"Movement can shift my state
   because…"* and *"A way I can move that fits my body and my place is…"* Share with
   a partner.
7. **Close (5 min).** One slow breath. Notice your state. Movement is a tool you
   carry with you.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer a seated-only sequence and sentence starters for the log entry;
  offer motor imagery or "notice the urge to move" as a non-motor alternative.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to find one additional peer-reviewed detail about the
  movement–mood link and note it with a source.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner describe their before/after state and
  name at least one mechanism (endorphins, monoamines, BDNF) with appropriate
  hedging?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The before/after state note and "why it works" entry in
  the practice log.

## Home connection

Once this week, when you feel stuck or heavy, move for two minutes in a way that fits
your body and your space — stretch, walk, dance, carry something. Log what happened.
Movement is a tool, not a chore.
