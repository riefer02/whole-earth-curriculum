# Lesson 10 — Designing Inclusive Movement

## Summary

Learners design an inclusive movement activity — a game, dance, or routine — that
adapts to many bodies (standing, seated, wheeling, low-energy) and environments
(indoor, outdoor, small, loud, low-resource). They apply the seven principles of
universal design and test their design with a partner, so the strand ends in a real,
usable artifact.

## Objectives

- Design inclusive movement activities that adapt to a wide range of bodies,
  abilities, and environments. _(D03.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of the last game you played where someone stood at the edge, watching. Maybe the
rules assumed two working legs, quick hands, or full hearing. Now imagine the same
game rebuilt so that the person at the edge plays fully — not as a "special" version,
but as the way the game simply *is*. That is inclusive design: not an add-on for a
few, but a design that never had a default to begin with.

## Materials

- Universal-design-game chart
- Practice log
- Paper for a design sketch

## Preparation

- Prepare the chart and the universal-design principles.
- Gather a few simple objects that could become game equipment (soft ball, cloth,
  container).

## Facilitator note

This is the strand's **summative design task**, and it should feel like real design
work, not a worksheet. Walk learners through a **worked example** first: a simple
circle pass game, then show how three adaptations widen it — play seated or standing;
use a larger, softer, slower ball; allow an underarm roll instead of a throw; drop the
"you're out" rule in favor of "everyone keeps playing." Then let learners design their
own.

**Anchor to the seven principles** (Equitable Use, Flexibility in Use, Simple and
Intuitive Use, Perceptible Information, Tolerance for Error, Low Physical Effort,
Size and Space for Approach and Use; S-369). Ask designers to name *which* principle
each adaptation serves. This turns "be nice to everyone" into a checkable design
standard.

**Test for who is left out.** Require each design to name at least three distinct
bodies/needs it welcomes (for example: a person who uses a wheelchair, a person with
low energy or chronic pain, a Deaf or hard-of-hearing person, a person in a small or
loud space) and at least one environment it works in. Avoid able-bodied default and
avoid ranking bodies.

The **ethics** lens: inclusion is a design decision, not a favor. The **egalitarian**
lens: who is in the circle, and who has been quietly designed out? The **global**
lens: inclusive games and adapted sports exist worldwide (wheelchair basketball,
goalball, boccia, and many traditional games with seated variants). The **technology**
lens: consider whether equipment or an app widens access — or quietly depends on
resources not everyone has.

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve (5 min).** Name the seven universal-design principles from memory (try
   for at least four), then check. What is the difference between a barrier and an
   adaptation?
2. **Study the worked example (10 min).** Look at the chart: one circle game, three
   ways to play. List the adaptations and guess which universal-design principle each
   serves. Add one more adaptation of your own.
3. **Design (15 min).** Choose a movement activity — a game, a simple dance, or a
   short routine. Write: *the goal, the basic rules, and at least three adaptations*
   that welcome different bodies or environments. Name the principle each adaptation
   serves. Sketch it if that helps.
4. **Test for gaps (10 min).** Trade designs with a partner. Your job as tester: find
   *one body or environment still left out*, and suggest one way to widen the design.
   Revise your own design if the feedback fits.
5. **Write the final rules (10 min).** Produce a clean, short set of rules a
   facilitator could run tomorrow — clear enough that anyone could lead it, in any
   setting.
6. **Close (5 min).** One sentence to yourself: *"A game is fair when…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a design template with fields (goal, rules, three adaptations,
  principle for each); design two adaptations instead of three.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to write a one-paragraph rationale for how their design
  reflects the social model of disability.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** A complete inclusive-movement design — goal, rules,
  three or more adaptations mapped to universal-design principles, and a tested
  revision that widens access.
- **Peer:** A tester's check that no body or environment was left out without notice.

## Home connection

Try your design with two or three people this week — family, friends, younger
learners — in whatever space you have. Note what needed to change in the real world
that you did not predict on paper. That is how good design is made.
