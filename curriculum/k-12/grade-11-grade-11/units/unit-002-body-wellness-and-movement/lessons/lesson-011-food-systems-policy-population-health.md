# Lesson 11 — Food Systems, Policy, and Population Health

## Summary

Learners analyze how food systems and public policy shape population nutrition and
health. They trace food from farm to plate to waste, learn who decides what is easy
and affordable to eat, engage with real global data on hunger and food waste, and
propose one change that would widen access.

## Objectives

- Analyze how public policy and food systems shape population nutrition and health,
  and propose a change that widens access. _(D03.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

Why is it easier and cheaper, in many places, to buy a sugary drink than clean
drinking water, or a packaged snack than fresh vegetables? It is rarely a personal
failure of willpower — it is a *food environment* built by decisions: what farms get
subsidized, what stores are allowed where, what is advertised to children, what school
meals contain. Your plate is personal, but the choices around it are political.

## Materials

- Food-system map
- Practice log

## Preparation

- Prepare the food-system map and the data sheets (hunger, food waste, dietary
  guidance).

## Facilitator note

This lesson moves from **personal nutrition to structural analysis**, and it is where
the egalitarian lens is sharpest. Teach the food system concretely: food is grown,
transported, sold, eaten, and wasted, and at each step a *policy lever* shapes what is
available, affordable, and advertised. Name real levers without endorsing any single
policy: **subsidies** (which crops are cheap to grow), **food labeling and marketing
rules** (WHO recommends limiting marketing of foods high in fat, salt, and sugar to
children; S-312), **school and public meal programs**, and **zoning/retail access**
(where stores and markets can open, and where "food deserts" form).

**Data, hedged and sourced.** About **733 million people faced hunger in 2023**
(roughly 1 in 11) and about **2.33 billion faced moderate or severe food insecurity**
(FAO SOFI; S-409). Roughly **1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022** — about
a fifth of the food available to consumers (UNEP; S-410). Figures shift year to year;
treat them as honest snapshots, not eternal truths. WHO's healthy-diet guidance
(diversity across food groups; limit free sugars, salt, and saturated fat) frames what
"healthy food" means at the population level (S-038).

**Values vs. facts.** That *everyone deserves enough to eat* is a value, not a fact —
state it as such, and let learners form their own proposals. Do not shame any body,
any family's food, or any culture's way of eating.

The **ethics** lens: access to healthy food is a question of right and wrong, not just
economics. The **egalitarian** lens: who benefits from the current system, and who is
left out? The **global** lens: food systems differ everywhere — compare a smallholder
farm, a megacity market, a desert food-desert, a coastal fishing village. The
**technology** lens: refrigeration, transport, and apps reshape food systems — for
better and worse. The **environment** lens: wasted food wastes the water, land, and
energy used to grow it (S-410).

## Procedure

1. **Retrieve (5 min).** What is one way movement traditions build community? Then
   pivot: what did you eat today, and where did it come from?
2. **Trace the food system (10 min).** Follow the map — farm, transport, market,
   plate, waste. At each step, ask: *who decides?* Note the policy levers above each
   step. Add one food from your own life and trace its path.
3. **Meet the data (10 min).** Read the figures: ~733 million people hungry in 2023;
   ~1.05 billion tonnes of food wasted in 2022. Discuss: what does it mean that
   hunger and waste exist *at the same time*? What does that say about systems rather
   than individuals?
4. **Analyze a lever (10 min).** In pairs, choose one policy lever (subsidies,
   labeling/marketing rules, school meals, zoning). Find: *who benefits, who is left
   out, and one real example anywhere in the world.*
5. **Propose a change (10 min).** Write a short proposal: *the change, who it helps,
   how it widens access, and one trade-off or cost.* Use hedged, sourced language.
6. **Close (5 min).** One sentence: *"Food is personal and political because…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a proposal template (change / who it helps / trade-off) and a
  single lever to analyze.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to research one real policy's measured effect and add a
  source to their proposal.

## Assessment

- **Summative (portfolio):** A policy/food-system analysis naming a lever, its
  winners and the left-out, plus a concrete proposal that widens access, with a
  trade-off acknowledged.
- **Peer:** A partner's check that the proposal is concrete and names who it helps.

## Home connection

Look at the food available within a short distance of your home. What is easy to get,
and what is missing? Who decides that? Write or discuss one observation — noticing is
the first step of change.
