# Lesson 11 — Arguing for Care and Solidarity Toward Distant Others

## Summary

Learners learn to argue — not to shout or win, but to *reason*. They build a
**claim-reason-evidence** argument for a position on care, solidarity, and collective
responsibility toward people far away, learn to mark the difference between evidence and
values, and answer the strongest objection to their own position.

## Objectives

- Argue for a position on care, solidarity, and collective responsibility toward distant
  others. _(D02.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Caring about distant others is easy to feel and hard to argue for — someone will ask
"why should we spend on strangers when our own have needs?" If your only answer is "it
feels right," you will lose the argument, and maybe the cause. A *reasoned* argument —
a claim backed by reasons backed by evidence, with your values named honestly — is how
care becomes persuasive, not just sincere. This lesson builds that argument with you.

## Materials

- Your journal
- An arguing-with-reasons diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the arguing-with-reasons diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 2, recall the steelman. An argument that ignores the
  strongest objection is a strawman of *yourself*; today we build the full structure and
  answer the objection.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a persuasive position
on care/solidarity/collective responsibility is a claim backed by reasons backed by
evidence, with values named as values — and it answers the strongest objection.** Teach
the structure with a worked example; assess the *reasoning*, never the position reached
(inquiry over creed).

The **ethics** lens: what we owe distant others is a genuine ethical question, and the
skill is reasoning about it, not parroting a conclusion. The **egalitarian** lens:
reasoned argument is an equalizer — it lets a person with a minority or unpopular
position be heard on the strength of their reasons, not their status. The **global**
lens: the "distant others" of the argument are real people in real places; keep them
concrete and dignified, never abstract props. The **critical-thinking** lens: the
evidence-versus-values distinction is the core discipline — a claim about what *is*
(cited) differs from a claim about what *ought* to be (a value), and strong arguments
know which is which.

**Emotional safety.** Learners hold different positions, and that is the point. No
position is graded as correct; "I am still forming my view" is a valid stance. Keep the
debate structured so no one is attacked personally. Honor opt-outs from speaking aloud;
a written argument is a full submission.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What made a strong argument, from Lesson 2? (Stating the other
   side at its strongest.) Today we build our own side — fully and honestly.
2. **Meet the structure (10 min).** Worked example, together. A position: *our community
   should keep paying into a fund that supports people rebuilding after a flood far away.*
   Build it:
   - **Claim** — what I argue (above).
   - **Reason** — why it matters (their recovery is our shared security and dignity; the
     flood was not their fault).
   - **Evidence** — what I can show (data on how the fund helped; the fact that the
     flood's cause — a changing climate — is shared, IPCC S-005).
   - **Value, named** — "I hold that a stranger's life is worth the same as a
     neighbor's." That is a value, not a fact — say so.
   - **Objection answered** — "Why not our own first?" → "We can do both; 'our own' and
     'distant' are not enemies."
3. **Build your argument (20 min).** Choose your position on care, solidarity, or
   collective responsibility toward distant others. Write it in the five parts: claim,
   reason, evidence, value (named), objection answered.
4. **Peer challenge (10 min).** In pairs, share (only what you choose). Your partner
   offers the *strongest* objection; you answer it. Then switch. Adjust your argument.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the argument in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *I can argue for care with reasons and evidence, not just feeling.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Argue by writing, speaking, or recording; expression is
  flexible.
- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Build and exchange arguments in
  writing, sign, or text; a written or signed objection and answer is a full peer
  challenge. Reading your partner's written or signed objection is the listening.
- **Support:** Offer a filled-in template with sentence frames for each of the five
  parts and a menu of positions.
- **Extension:** Write a short essay defending your position against two objections, and
  mark every claim as evidence or value.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Does the argument include all five parts — a clear claim,
  at least one reason, evidence (or an honest "here is where I lack evidence"), a value
  named as a value, and an objection answered?
- **Portfolio:** The argument, gathered toward D02.S4.11.01.

## Home connection

Explain your position to someone at home and invite their strongest objection. Notice
whether answering it strengthens or changes your argument.
