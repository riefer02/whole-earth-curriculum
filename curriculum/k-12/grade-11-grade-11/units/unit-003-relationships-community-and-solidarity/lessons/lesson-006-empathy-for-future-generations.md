# Lesson 6 — Empathy for Future Generations

## Summary

Learners stretch empathy one step further — beyond distance to *time*. The people of the
next century are people they will never meet, yet their choices today shape those lives.
Learners see their decisions as **seeds handed forward**, and write a letter to a person
in the future naming what they hope that person will still have.

## Objectives

- Apply empathy to people they have never met, such as those far away or in future
  generations. _(D02.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Some of the things you touch today will outlive you: a forest, a building, a river, a
language, a debt, a habit of care. The people who will live with them are not born yet —
but they are coming, as surely as you arrived. You cannot see their faces or hear their
voices, which is exactly why it is so easy to forget them. Yet your choices are already
a letter you are writing to them, whether you sign it or not. This lesson helps you
write it on purpose.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A future-generations timeline (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the future-generations timeline.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 5, recall the three bridges — name, story, shared
  need. Future generations have no name or story yet; the shared need is all we can
  know — and it is enough to start from.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **future generations
are real others we will never meet, and intergenerational choices — land, water, climate,
knowledge, debt, care — are how we already shape their lives.** Teach with a worked
example of one intergenerational decision; hold the "how much do we owe them" question
open (it is a live ethical debate, not a settled answer).

The **ethics** lens: whether and how much we owe people who do not exist yet is a central
question of intergenerational ethics, and learners reason about it rather than being told.
The **egalitarian** lens: future people cannot vote, lobby, or speak — they are the
ultimate voiceless party, so fairness asks us to speak for them; and the *burdens* of
today's choices (climate, pollution) fall unevenly on the young and the yet-unborn, who
contributed least (IPCC, S-005). The **global** lens: many cultures hold the unborn and
the ancestors in mind when deciding — stewardship and legacy are widespread ideas, not
one region's. The **environment** lens: the climate and living world are the clearest
"letters to the future" — what we emit and protect now is read by people in 2100. The
**technology** lens: some technologies (a dam, a data archive, a long-lived material)
commit the future for decades or centuries; their makers rarely meet their inheritors.

**Emotional safety.** The future can feel heavy or abstract. Keep it grounded and
hopeful: focus on what a learner *can* hand forward, not on despair. No one is told what
to believe about how much is owed; the letter is personal and may be kept private.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What three bridges carried empathy to a distant other? (Name,
   story, shared need.) Future generations have no name and no story yet. What *is*
   left? (The shared need — clean water, breathable air, a livable future.)
2. **Meet the intergenerational choice (10 min).** Worked example, together. A village
   decides whether to cut a slow-growing forest now for quick income, or protect it for
   wood, water, and shade decades later. The people who planted nothing but will inherit
   the choice are not born. The question "sustainable development" tries to hold:
   meeting today's needs without taking away the future's ability to meet theirs
   (Brundtland, S-395). Notice: this is a decision *about people you will never meet*.
3. **Map the seeds (15 min).** On your timeline, list three or four things your life is
   already handing forward — land, water, climate, a language, a skill, a habit, a debt,
   a technology. For each, ask: *who inherits this, and what will it mean for them?*
4. **Write the letter (20 min).** In your journal, write a short letter to a person in
   the year 2100 — someone you will never meet. Tell them: *Here is what I hope you
   still have; here is what I worry I am taking from you; and here is one thing I want to
   do, even now, to hand something good forward.* It may be small; small, steady care
   outruns grand gestures.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the timeline and the letter in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *my choices are a letter to people I will never meet — and I can write it well.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Write the letter, draw it, or record it as a spoken message;
  expression is flexible.
- **Support:** Offer sentence frames for the letter and a list of "seeds" to choose from.
- **Extension:** Research one real intergenerational decision (a treaty, a forest, a dam,
  a debt) and argue where the future's interests were honored or forgotten.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the letter name what the learner hopes a future person
  still has, what they worry about taking, and one concrete thing they will do to hand
  something forward?
- **Portfolio:** The timeline and the letter, gathered toward D02.S2.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone older at home: "What did the people before you hand forward to you — and
what do you hope to hand to me?" Listen for the seeds.
