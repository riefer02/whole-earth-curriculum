# Lesson 12 — Designing a Mutual-Aid or Cooperative Effort

## Summary

Learners design a real **mutual-aid or cooperative effort** that meets a need in a
community they care about. They learn the key distinction — **charity** is one-way
giving that keeps the power with the giver, while **mutual aid** and **cooperatives**
share both the giving *and* the deciding — and they draft the effort so the people
helped are co-authors, not recipients.

## Objectives

- Plan and begin a sustained mutual-aid or cooperative effort with accountability.
  _(D02.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

Imagine two ways to help a struggling neighbor. In one, you hand them food and decide
what they need. In the other, you sit down together, ask what is needed, and build a
way to meet it that they help run. The first is charity; the second is mutual aid. Both
feed someone — but only the second treats the neighbor as an equal with a say. A
cooperative — a shop, a farm, a credit circle owned and decided by its members — is
that same idea made durable. This lesson helps you design the second kind, the kind that
lasts and dignifies.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A charity-vs-mutual-aid diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the charity-vs-mutual-aid diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 10, recall your contribution plan. A mutual-aid effort
  is a contribution grown into something *shared and sustained* — with others holding it,
  not just you. Today we design it.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a mutual-aid or
cooperative effort differs from charity because it shares the deciding as well as the
giving — and a good design names who is helped, who helps, what is done, and how
decisions are made.** Teach the charity/mutual-aid distinction with a worked example
(Spade, S-345); hold "sharing power is better" as a value, distinct from the descriptive
distinction.

The **ethics** lens: the difference between helping *at* someone and helping *with*
someone is a moral difference about dignity and agency. The **egalitarian** lens: the
core — where does the power sit? A design that lets the giver decide is charity; one
that lets everyone decide is mutual aid. The **global** lens: mutual aid and cooperatives
are widespread, documented forms across the world — communal-work traditions (harambee,
gotong royong, mink'a/minga, S-164), worker cooperatives and credit unions (ICA, S-486;
Mondragón, S-487) — not the invention of any one region. The **technology** lens:
technology can carry a mutual-aid effort across distance (shared documents, group
messages, digital collection) or concentrate decisions in a few hands — the design
chooses which.

**Emotional safety.** Designing a real effort can feel like a big commitment. Reassure
learners: this is a *plan and a first beginning*, not a lifetime pledge, and it may be
small. No one is required to act beyond their capacity or safety. All forms of effort —
a study-help circle, a shared garden, a mutual-fund pool, a co-op of any size — are
equally valid; no scale is graded as better.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What was the contribution you planned in Lesson 10? Now imagine
   it is not *your* project but *our* project. What changes?
2. **Meet the distinction (10 min).** Worked example, together. A need: several families
   cannot afford school supplies.
   - **Charity:** a donor buys supplies and distributes them. The donor decides; the
     families receive.
   - **Mutual aid:** the families and neighbors pool small amounts, buy in bulk together,
     and together decide who needs what first. Everyone gives *something*; everyone
     decides.
   The goods may look similar; the power is different. A **cooperative** is this idea
   made permanent — members own and run it, one member one vote (ICA, S-486).
3. **Design the effort (25 min).** In your journal (or with a partner), design a
   mutual-aid or cooperative effort for a real need you can see. Answer:
   - **Need:** what real need does it meet?
   - **Members:** who is helped — and are they also *helpers and deciders*?
   - **Tasks:** what does it do, and who does what?
   - **Deciding:** how are decisions made, and how does every member have a voice?
   - **First move:** what is the smallest first step this week?
4. **Peer check (10 min).** Share designs in pairs (only what you choose). Your partner
   asks: "Where does the power sit?" and "Do the people helped have a voice?" Adjust.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the design in your portfolio. Say or write: *I
   can design help that shares the giving and the deciding.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Design by writing, drawing the circle of members, or speaking;
  expression is flexible.
- **Support:** Offer a template and a menu of small, feasible efforts; pair learners who
  want to design together.
- **Extension:** Draft the same effort twice — as charity and as mutual aid — and write
  which you would choose and why, naming the trade-off.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Does the design name the need, the members (as helpers and
  deciders, not only recipients), the tasks, a decision-making method that gives every
  member a voice, and a concrete first move?
- **Portfolio:** The effort design, gathered toward D02.S4.11.02.

## Home connection

Ask at home: "Is there something our household or street could do *together* that no one
could do alone?" Notice where the deciding would sit.
