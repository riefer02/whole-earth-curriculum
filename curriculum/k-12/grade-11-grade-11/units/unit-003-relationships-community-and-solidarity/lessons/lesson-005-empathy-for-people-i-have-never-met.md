# Lesson 5 — Empathy for People I Have Never Met

## Summary

Learners take empathy to its hardest reach: people they will never meet. They learn an
honest truth — empathy is real but *partial*: it weakens with distance and as numbers
grow (Slovic's **psychic numbing** and the **identifiable-victim effect**) — and that
**names, stories, and shared needs** are the bridges that carry care across. They write
an empathy bridge to one distant other.

## Objectives

- Apply empathy to people they have never met, such as those far away or in future
  generations. _(D02.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

You can feel a friend's sadness the moment you see their face. But what about a fisher
whose coast is vanishing thousands of kilometers away, or a child in a city you will
never visit, whose day looks nothing like yours? There is no face in front of you, no
voice in the room — and yet they are as real as your friend. Feeling with them does not
happen automatically; it has to be built. This lesson shows you the bridges — a name, a
story, a shared need — and why they matter.

## Materials

- Your journal
- An empathy-across-distance diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the empathy-across-distance diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 2, recall "staying open to being changed." Empathy for
  a distant other asks the same openness — toward a life unlike yours.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **empathy is real but
partial — it fades with distance and numbers — so caring for distant others requires
bridges: a name, a story, a shared need.** Teach the empathy/psychic-numbing distinction
with a worked example; keep the value question open (how wide *should* care go is a
choice, not a test).

The **ethics** lens: whether distant strangers count in my choices is a core ethical
question, and the lesson invites reasoning, not a single answer. The **egalitarian**
lens: empathy's documented limits are themselves an unfairness — if care runs only on
feeling, the far and the many get less of it; building bridges is a more reliable
equalizer. The **global** lens: the distant other is not an abstraction — they are
someone's neighbor with a name and a day; treat real people with dignity, never as
props. The **technology** lens: screens both help and hurt — they can carry a distant
face and story close enough to feel, and they can flood us until we numb (Slovic,
S-414).

**Emotional safety.** This lesson can surface overwhelm or guilt about the world's
suffering. Frame it as *widening care by choice and practice*, not as a demand to feel
everything or save everyone. No learner is asked to disclose personal reactions; "I am
still working this out" is a complete answer.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** When was a time a single person's story moved you more than a
   big number about many people? Why do you think that is? Hold the thought.
2. **Meet the partial empathy (10 min).** Worked example, together. Research shows a
   strange and important thing: we feel more for **one named face** than for a
   statistic. One child's name and story can move us to act; a report about thousands
   can leave us numb — this is the **identifiable-victim effect** and **psychic
   numbing** (Slovic, S-414). Empathy is real, but it is *partial*: it runs strongest
   toward the near, the familiar, and the single. That is not a failure — it is how
   feeling works. So caring for the many and the far cannot run on feeling alone.
3. **Build the bridge (20 min).** Choose one distant person or place — a coast, a
   city, a valley far away. Find and write three bridges:
   - **A name** — who is one real person there? (or imagine one with care)
   - **A story** — what is one ordinary thing in their day?
   - **A shared need** — what do they need that you also need? (safety, food, water,
     belonging, a future)
   Notice: the shared need is where the bridge lands.
4. **Write the bridge (15 min).** In your journal: *Here is one distant other, here is
   the bridge (name, story, shared need), and here is what I now feel or wonder.* Keep it
   honest — a genuine question is a fine ending.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the bridge in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *feeling fades with distance, but names, stories, and shared needs carry it across.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Build the bridge by writing, speaking, drawing, or arranging
  found objects; expression is flexible.
- **Support:** Offer a pre-chosen distant person's short story and sentence frames for
  each bridge.
- **Extension:** Compare two bridges — one that moves you and one that does not — and
  analyze *why* (nearness? similarity? a single name?).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the bridge name a distant other and include all three
  elements — a name, a story, and a shared need — and state what the learner feels or
  wonders?
- **Portfolio:** The empathy bridge, gathered toward D02.S2.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Is there someone far away whose story stays with you?" Listen for
the name, the story, and the shared need — the three bridges.
