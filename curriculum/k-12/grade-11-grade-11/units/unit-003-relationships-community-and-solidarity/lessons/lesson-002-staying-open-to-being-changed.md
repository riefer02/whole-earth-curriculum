# Lesson 2 — Staying Open to Being Changed

## Summary

Learners take deep listening one step further: not just hearing the other, but staying
open to being *changed* by them. They learn the **steelman** — stating the strongest
version of the view they oppose, instead of the weakest — and run a structured exchange
where each person must name one true thing in the other side. They reflect on why
"listening is not losing."

## Objectives

- Practice deep listening across disagreement, staying open to being changed by what
  others say. _(D02.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

It is easy to listen politely while quietly staying exactly where you were. Being
*changed* is harder — it can feel like losing. But consider: every belief you hold that
is worth anything, you once did not hold. Someone, a book, an experience, a friend —
something changed your mind to get you here. Being open to being changed is not
weakness; it is how you got to be thoughtful at all. This lesson asks what it would
take for you to change your mind again, on purpose, with dignity.

## Materials

- Your journal
- An open-to-change diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the open-to-change diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 1, recall the four steps of the deep-listening loop.
  Today we ask the harder question that loop makes possible: *am I willing to be
  changed?*

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **deep listening
culminates in real openness to being changed, and the "steelman" — restating the
strongest version of the opposing view — is the concrete skill that makes it
possible.** Teach the steelman with a worked example; structure the exchange so no one
is pushed to abandon a conviction, only to see it clearly.

The **ethics** lens: treating a disagreement as a shared search rather than a contest
is an ethical stance — it treats the other person as a source of truth, not an obstacle.
The **egalitarian** lens: a steelman gives the weaker or less popular side its best
possible hearing, which is what fairness to *ideas* requires, not just fairness to
people. The **global** lens: many traditions of decision-making — from deliberative
councils to community assemblies — are built on the premise that the group is wiser
when everyone can change the others and be changed in turn. The **critical-thinking**
lens: steelmanning is the heart of reasoning well — you cannot refute a view you have
not first understood at its strongest.

**Emotional safety.** Changing one's mind can feel like a threat to identity. Frame it
as *curiosity and growth*, never as defeat. No learner is graded on whether they change
their mind — only on whether they can state the other side fairly. Offer opt-outs; a
learner may choose a low-stakes topic or reflect privately. "Listening is not losing"
is the constant refrain (Hess, S-349).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, what are the four steps of the deep-listening
   loop? Now add the question the loop opens: *would I let this change me?*
2. **Meet the steelman (10 min).** Worked example, together. A **strawman** is the weak,
   distorted version of an opposing view — easy to knock down. A **steelman** is the
   strongest, most honest version — hard to dismiss. Example: two people disagree about
   whether their village should build a road through a forest. The strawman of "no road"
   is "they don't care about anyone's job." The steelman is "they fear losing a water
   source and a place the whole community depends on, and they want a slower, more
   careful plan." That version is harder to dismiss — and truer.
3. **The exchange (20 min).** In pairs, choose a topic you genuinely disagree on
   (small or large — a rule, a habit, a policy). Person A states their view in one or
   two sentences. Person B must **steelman** it back — "So your strongest point is…" —
   and then name **one true thing** in it. Switch. The rule: you may not answer until
   you have named one true thing in the other side.
4. **Reflect (15 min).** In your journal: *Did stating the other side's best version
   change anything in how I see it? Was there a moment I felt myself soften?* Be
   honest — no softening is also a real answer.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and reflection in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *I can stay open to being changed — that is strength, not surrender.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Respond by speaking, writing, or drawing the two views meeting
  at an open door; expression is flexible.
- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Run the exchange in writing, sign,
  or text instead of speech: each person writes or signs their view, then the other
  writes or signs the strongest version and one true thing. Reading or watching with full
  attention is the listening; writing, signing, typing (AAC), or drawing is the speaking.
- **Support:** Provide a steelman sentence frame ("Their strongest point is that …,
  and the true part is …") and a list of low-stakes topics.
- **Extension:** Write a one-page steelman of a view you currently reject, then write
  the strongest reply you can — and name what, if anything, shifted.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Did each person state the *strongest* version of the
  other's view (not a strawman) and name one true thing in it before replying?
- **Portfolio:** The steelman exchange and the reflection, gathered toward D02.S1.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a view you two differ on, and try stating *their* strongest
point back to them before you answer. Ask: "Did I get you right?"
