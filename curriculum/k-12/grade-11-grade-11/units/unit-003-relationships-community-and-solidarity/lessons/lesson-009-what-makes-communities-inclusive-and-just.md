# Lesson 9 — What Makes Communities Inclusive and Just

## Summary

Learners move from resilience to the harder questions of **inclusion** and **justice**.
A community can be strong and still leave people out. Learners learn to ask three
questions — *who has a seat, who has no chair, and who holds the gate* — and use
historical and contemporary examples to see how belonging is given and withheld. They
audit one community they know.

## Objectives

- Analyze what makes communities resilient, inclusive, and just, using historical and
  contemporary examples. _(D02.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

A group can feel wonderfully close to the people already inside it — and still have a
locked door. The warmest table in the world is not just if some people were never
invited, or were invited only to stand behind the chairs. Inclusion is not the same as
being nice; it is about who can actually *reach a seat*, and justice is about who
decides. This lesson gives you the three questions to ask any group, including your own.

## Materials

- Your journal
- An inclusive-just diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the inclusive-just diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 8, recall the three roots of resilience. A community
  can have all three and still exclude people — resilience and justice are different
  things. Today we ask the justice question.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **inclusion and justice
are not feelings of warmth but structures — who has a seat, who has no chair, who holds
the gate — and can be analyzed with historical and contemporary examples.** Teach the
three questions with a worked example; keep examples global and non-hierarchical.

The **ethics** lens: who a community leaves out, and whether that is just, is a moral
question about the worth of persons — all persons, not only the ones already inside. The
**egalitarian** lens: the lens is the core — look at how power and resources are shared,
and at whose needs are treated as optional; note that exclusion is often structural and
inherited, not chosen by the excluded (UNDP inequality data, S-283). The **global**
lens: every society draws membership lines — by birth, language, ability, belief,
gender, wealth, or migration status — and none has eliminated exclusion; the question is
how the lines are drawn and whether they can be crossed. The **critical-thinking** lens:
"who holds the gate" trains learners to look past a community's self-description to its
actual rules and power.

**Emotional safety.** Learners may recognize themselves as excluded, or as inside a group
that excludes. Keep analysis structural, not personal-shaming; no one is asked to confess
their own exclusion or exclusion of others. "I am still thinking about this" is a full
answer. Emphasize that noticing is the first step to changing, never a verdict.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What were the three roots of resilience? Now a harder question: a
   community can be resilient *and* unfair. What is the difference?
2. **Meet the three questions (10 min).** Worked example, together. Take a real pattern:
   a thriving neighborhood association that "welcomes everyone" — but its meetings are
   held at a time working people cannot attend, in a language not everyone speaks, and its
   decisions are made by a few long-time members. Ask:
   - **Who has a seat?** (the long-time members)
   - **Who has no chair?** (newcomers, workers, non-speakers of the meeting language)
   - **Who holds the gate?** (whoever sets the time, language, and agenda)
   Warmth is not the same as a chair.
3. **Study two examples (20 min).** From the provided case study (or your own
   knowledge), choose two communities — one historical, one contemporary, from different
   regions. For each, answer the three questions: *who has a seat, who has no chair, who
   holds the gate?* Then ask the justice question: *is the gate fair, and could it be
   opened?*
4. **Audit a community you know (15 min).** In your journal, choose one community you
   know. Run the three questions honestly: *Who has a seat here? Who has no chair — and
   why? Who holds the gate?* Then write one concrete change that would open it.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the audit in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *a just community notices who is missing — and opens the gate.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Audit by writing, drawing the table and chairs, or speaking;
  expression is flexible.
- **Support:** Provide a pre-filled example of the three questions and sentence frames
  for the audit.
- **Extension:** Compare two communities' membership rules in depth and argue which is
  more just, and by what standard (see SEP distributive justice, S-388).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the audit answer all three questions (seat, no chair, gate)
  for one real community and propose one concrete change that would open it?
- **Portfolio:** The case comparison and the audit, gathered toward D02.S3.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Who is missing from the groups we belong to — and what would it
take to invite them in?" Listen for the gate.
