# Lesson 7 — Empathy vs Fairness: When They Pull Apart

## Summary

Empathy and fairness usually point the same way — but not always. Sometimes caring for
the one person in front of us conflicts with being fair to the many. Learners learn to
**name the trade-off**, hear each side at its strongest, and make a weighing they can
defend. The point is not to pick a "right" answer, but to choose with open eyes instead
of letting feeling or rule silently win.

## Objectives

- Weigh empathy for individuals against fairness to a group when the two conflict.
  _(D02.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Imagine your closest friend did something that broke a rule the whole group depends on.
Your heart says: protect my friend. Fairness says: the rule applies to everyone, or it
means nothing. Both voices are real. Neither is a villain. This is not a rare dilemma —
it happens in families, schools, workplaces, and states, every day. The mature move is
not to pretend one voice is silent; it is to weigh them honestly. This lesson gives you
the scale.

## Materials

- Your journal
- An empathy-vs-fairness balance diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the empathy-vs-fairness balance diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 5, recall that empathy is partial — it runs strongest
  toward the one named face. That very bias is *why* fairness sometimes has to check it.
  Today we put the two on a scale.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **empathy and fairness
can genuinely conflict, and maturity is naming the trade-off and weighing it — hearing
each side at its strongest — rather than pretending one value cancels the other.** Teach
the weighing with a worked example; keep dilemmas hypothetical or lightly structured so
no learner is cornered.

The **ethics** lens: this is a live question in moral philosophy — impartiality versus
partiality, the one versus the many — and learners reason through it rather than receive
a verdict. The **egalitarian** lens: rules exist so that the powerful and the liked do
not quietly win; fairness is the equalizer, and it is *especially* needed exactly when
our empathy pulls toward the familiar face. The **global** lens: every society weighs
kinship and rules differently — some lean on the letter of the law, others on the
relationship — and none has "solved" the tension; it is a human constant. The
**critical-thinking** lens: the skill is articulating the strongest case for *both*
sides before deciding — the same steelman discipline from Lesson 2, now applied to a
hard choice.

**Emotional safety.** Learners may feel loyalty to real people in real dilemmas. Use
invented or lightly structured cases; no one is asked to reveal a personal conflict.
Emphasize that there is no "correct" weighing to report — the assessed skill is *naming
the trade-off and reasoning*, never the answer reached.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Why does empathy run strongest toward the one named face? (It is
   partial — near, familiar, single.) Why might fairness need to check it? Hold both.
2. **Meet the scale (10 min).** Worked example, together. A dilemma: your team's rule is
   that anyone who misses practice sits out the match. Your best friend missed practice —
   for a family emergency, not laziness. The match matters to everyone. Put it on the
   scale:
   - **Empathy for the one:** my friend was needed at home; punishing them doubles their
     hurt.
   - **Fairness to the many:** the rule protects the team; exceptions for friends mean
     the rule protects no one.
   Neither side is wrong. The task is to *name* what each side sacrifices.
3. **Practice a dilemma (20 min).** In pairs, choose a dilemma from the list (or invent
   one): a scholarship for one needy student or books for the whole class; a sick
   relative's medicine or food for many; one friend's secret or the group's safety. Argue
   each side *at its strongest* — use the steelman from Lesson 2. Then each of you, for
   yourself, state a weighing: *I would lean this way, because…, and here is what I
   accept losing.*
4. **Write the weighing (15 min).** In your journal: *The dilemma; the strongest case for
   empathy; the strongest case for fairness; my weighing and what I gave up.* A defended,
   honest weighing is the goal — not a "right" answer.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the weighing in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *when empathy and fairness pull apart, I can name the trade-off and choose with open
   eyes.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Weigh by speaking, writing, drawing the scale, or acting both
  sides; expression is flexible.
- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Weigh the dilemma in writing, sign,
  or a drawn scale; a partner can *read* the other's written or signed strongest case
  instead of hearing it. Stating both sides at their strongest — and your own weighing —
  is the skill, in any medium.
- **Support:** Offer a sentence frame ("The strongest case for the one is…; the
  strongest case for the many is…; I would lean…") and a gentle starter dilemma.
- **Extension:** Write a short essay on a real-world dilemma (a triage rule, an asylum
  policy, a workplace exception) and defend a weighing against the best objection.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Did the learner state the strongest case for *both*
  empathy and fairness, and then state a weighing with a reason and an acknowledged
  cost?
- **Portfolio:** The weighing, gathered toward D02.S2.11.02.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "Tell me about a time you had to choose between one person and
everyone. What did you do?" Listen for how they named the trade-off.
