# Lesson 1 — Deep Listening: To Understand, Not to Win

## Summary

Learners meet **deep listening** as a skill they can practice, not a talent some
people have. They learn a four-step loop — hear fully, pause, reflect back, check
"did I get you?" — and practice it in pairs on a small, safe disagreement. They write
a first reflection on what listening to understand feels like compared with listening
to win.

## Objectives

- Practice deep listening across disagreement, staying open to being changed by what
  others say. _(D02.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last argument where, while the other person was talking, you were
silently rehearsing your next sentence. Most of us do this — it is ordinary, not a
flaw. When you listen that way, you are not hearing the person; you are loading your
own weapon. Deep listening is the opposite: you set your reply down for a moment and
let the other person's words actually reach you. This lesson gives you the tool for
it — not a trick to win arguments, but a way to stay human with someone you disagree
with.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A deep-listening loop diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the deep-listening loop diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): recall from earlier grades (e.g., Grade 6 "acknowledge before
  you disagree," Grade 10 de-escalation) one thing you already know about listening in
  conflict. Today we deepen it into a full, teachable loop.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **deep listening is
a concrete, practiced skill — hear fully, pause, reflect back, check — and its point is
understanding, not winning.** Teach the loop with a worked example, then let learners
practice on a *low-stakes* disagreement (no forced disclosure of real conflicts).

The **ethics** lens: how we treat someone who disagrees with us is a moral choice, and
listening well is one way we honor another person's dignity. The **egalitarian** lens:
deep listening levels the field — it gives the quieter or less powerful voice the same
hearing as the loud one, and it refuses to let a disagreement turn one person into the
loser and the other into the victor. The **global** lens: careful, reflective listening
is practiced across cultures and traditions — from talking circles to council-style
decision-making — and is a skill, not the property of any one region. The
**social-emotional** lens: pausing before answering is self-regulation in action, and
"reflect back" is perspective-taking made audible.

**Emotional safety.** Learners may have painful real conflicts. Use only invented,
low-stakes practice disagreements (what to eat, which game, who should go first).
No one is asked to disclose a real rift; "I'd rather not share" is always honored. Do
not stage a debate with a "winner."

**Access.** "Deep listening" names *attention*, not the ear. Non-hearing learners run the
same loop by reading fully or watching a signed account; non-speaking learners reflect
back by writing, sign, or gesture. The loop is about presence, and it is open to every
mode of attending — this principle carries through the whole unit.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What is one thing you already know about listening well in a
   disagreement? Say or write it. Today we turn that into a loop you can run on
   purpose.
2. **Meet the loop (10 min).** Worked example, together. Here is a small disagreement:
   two housemates disagree about who should wash the dishes tonight. Watch the loop run:
   - **Hear fully** — listen without planning your reply.
   - **Pause** — let the words land before you answer.
   - **Reflect back** — "So you're saying you're exhausted from work and the pile feels
     unfair to you. Is that right?"
   - **Check** — "Did I get you?" and stay open to correction.
   Notice what is *missing*: the reply, the defense, the winning move. It comes later,
   and it is better for having waited.
3. **Practice in pairs (20 min).** Choose a small, safe disagreement from the list (or
   invent one): the last slice of fruit, which music to play, whose turn it is to sweep.
   Partner A states a view. Partner B runs the loop — hear, pause, reflect back, check —
   *before* answering. Then switch. If your partner corrects your reflection, accept it
   and try again. That correction is the skill working.
4. **Reflect (15 min).** In your journal, write: *What did listening to understand feel
   like, compared with listening to win? Where was it hardest — the pause, the reflect,
   or the check?* Be honest; difficulty here is information, not failure.
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the loop diagram and your reflection in your portfolio. Say or
   write: *I can listen to understand, not to win.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Writing is one way, not the only way — respond by speaking,
  drawing the loop, or acting it out; a response that uses neither writing nor speaking
  is still a full response.
- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Run the loop non-aurally — *read* a
  partner's written statement fully, or watch a signed account, before planning a reply;
  reflect back in writing, sign, drawing, or gesture; and check "did I get you?" on paper
  or a card. A non-speaking learner reflects back by writing, typing (AAC), signing, or
  pointing to a prepared card. Text, sign, and gesture are full listening and full
  speaking here.
- **Support:** Offer a sentence stem for the reflect-back step ("So what I hear you
  saying is …") and a word bank.
- **Extension:** Run the loop on a disagreement you *actually* hold, in writing, and
  note whether reflecting the other side changes anything in you.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** In the pair practice, did the listener complete all four
  steps — hear fully, pause, reflect back in their own words, and check "did I get you?"
  — before answering?
- **Portfolio:** The loop diagram and the reflection, gathered toward D02.S1.11.01.

## Home connection

With someone at home, try the loop once this week: let them finish, pause, then say back
what you heard and ask "did I get you?" Notice what changes.
