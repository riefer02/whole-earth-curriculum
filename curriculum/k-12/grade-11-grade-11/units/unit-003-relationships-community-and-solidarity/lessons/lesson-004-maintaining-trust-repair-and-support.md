# Lesson 4 — Maintaining Trust: Repair and Support

## Summary

Building trust is one thing; keeping it is another. Learners learn to maintain trust by
**repairing** it when it breaks: a three-step conversation — listen to what happened and
how it hurt, take responsibility for my part without excuses, and agree together on how
to make it right. They also name a concrete **support** they can offer one relationship,
and write a reflection that pairs the repair with the support.

## Objectives

- Build and maintain close, trusting relationships and offer the same support to
  others. _(D02.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Every relationship will be strained at some point — not because it is failing, but
because people are imperfect and lives are hard. What separates relationships that last
from those that quietly end is not the absence of hurt; it is the presence of **repair**.
When you break something with someone, the relationship does not automatically die. It
waits to see whether you will own it and make it right. And it is kept alive in the
quiet times by **support** — the small, steady acts that say "I am on your side." This
lesson gives you the tools for both.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A repair-conversation diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the repair-conversation diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 3, recall the four blocks of trust — reliability,
  honesty, care, repair. Today we practice the block that fixes the others: repair.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **repair is a teachable
skill — listen, take responsibility without excuses, agree on making it right — and it is
what keeps trust alive when it breaks; support is what keeps it strong between breaks.**
Model the repair conversation with a worked example, then let learners role-play a
*small, invented* mistake (never a real, unhealed harm).

The **ethics** lens: repair is the moral opposite of defensiveness — it puts rightness
and the other person ahead of my own pride. The **egalitarian** lens: a genuine apology
does not pull rank; the person who holds more power is *equally* called to own their
part, and repair restores the standing of the person who was hurt. The **global** lens:
repair is a cross-cultural practice — from restorative justice traditions (Diné hózhó,
Indigenous peacemaking circles) to formal apologies between states — and appears
wherever people decide a relationship matters more than being right. The
**social-emotional** lens: accepting responsibility without excuses is emotional
regulation under pressure — the hard, grown-up move.

**Emotional safety.** Repair touches real hurt. Use only small, invented mistakes for
role-play; never require a learner to re-enact a real harm or apologize on demand.
Opt-outs are honored. Frame "taking responsibility" as *owning my part*, not as
self-punishment or shame.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What are the four blocks of trust? Which one fixes the others
   when they break? (Repair.) Today we practice it.
2. **Meet the repair (10 min).** Worked example, together. A small, invented mistake:
   you borrowed a friend's tool and returned it late and damaged. Watch the three steps:
   - **Listen** — hear what happened and how it hurt, without interrupting or defending.
   - **Take responsibility** — "I was careless with your tool, and I'm sorry. That was
     my fault." No "but."
   - **Agree on making it right** — "How can I make this right? I can repair it or
     replace it." — decided *together*.
   Notice what is absent: excuses, blame, "you're overreacting."
3. **Practice in pairs (20 min).** Role-play a small invented mistake from the list (a
   broken promise, a thoughtless word, a forgotten favor). Person A owns the mistake and
   runs the three steps; Person B responds honestly. Then switch. Keep it light — this is
   practice, not a real confession.
4. **Write the support (15 min).** In your journal, complete both halves: *One repair I
   can practice: (the three steps in my own words).* And *One support I will offer this
   week to a relationship I care about: (specific and small).*
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the reflection in your portfolio. Say or
   write: *repair keeps trust alive; support keeps it strong.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Respond by role-play, writing, or drawing the three steps;
  expression is flexible.
- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Role-play the repair non-verbally if
  needed — write or sign each step, or act it with gesture and a written or signed "I'm
  sorry — how do I make it right?" Reading or watching the partner's account fully is the
  listening; a written, signed, drawn, or typed apology is a full repair.
- **Support:** Offer a starter script for each step and keep the invented mistakes
  trivial and safe.
- **Extension:** Write the repair conversation you would *want* to have in a real
  strained relationship, and identify the one line that feels hardest to say and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** In the role-play, did the person run all three steps —
  listen without defending, take responsibility without "but," and agree together on
  making it right?
- **Portfolio:** The repair script and the support reflection, gathered toward
  D02.S1.11.02.

## Home connection

Think of one small support you can offer someone at home this week, and do it — without
announcing it. Notice whether the relationship feels any different after.
