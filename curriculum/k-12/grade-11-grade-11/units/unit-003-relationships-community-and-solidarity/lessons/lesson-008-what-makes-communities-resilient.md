# Lesson 8 — What Makes Communities Resilient

## Summary

Learners analyze **resilience** — a community's ability to survive and recover from
shock. Using historical and contemporary examples, they identify three deep roots:
**shared care and mutual aid**, **many ways to decide and lead**, and **the ability to
change and adapt**. They map one community they know against these roots.

## Objectives

- Analyze what makes communities resilient, inclusive, and just, using historical and
  contemporary examples. _(D02.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of a flood, a fire, a factory closing, a war, a crop failure, or a sudden wave of
people needing shelter. Some communities come through changed but standing; others
shatter and never recover. The difference is rarely luck alone. It is whether the
community already had deep roots — people who share care before the crisis, many ways
to lead instead of one, and the habit of adapting. This lesson teaches you to see those
roots, so you can recognize them where they grow and help plant them where they do not.

## Materials

- Your journal
- A resilient-community diagram (see the asset)

## Preparation

- Print or draw the resilient-community diagram.
- Retrieval (spaced): from Lesson 3, recall the blocks of trust (reliability, honesty,
  care, repair). Resilience is trust *scaled up* to a whole community — the same blocks,
  grown into roots.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **resilient communities
rest on identifiable roots — shared care/mutual aid, distributed leadership, and
adaptability — visible in historical and contemporary examples across the world.** Teach
the roots with a worked example; keep examples global and non-hierarchical (no
"advanced vs. backward" community framing).

The **ethics** lens: resilience is partly a moral fact — communities that care for their
weakest members *before* a crisis are the ones who survive it, which makes care both kind
and practical. The **egalitarian** lens: resilience that depends on one leader or one
source fails everyone when that one breaks; widely shared care and leadership are more
equal *and* more durable. The **global** lens: draw examples from many regions — mutual
aid after floods in one place, cooperatives that steady a village economy in another,
Indigenous land-care that buffers drought in a third — never implying one region holds
the answer. The **environment** lens: many shocks are environmental (flood, drought,
fire, sea-level rise), and the communities that contributed least to them are often the
hardest hit (IPCC, S-005) — a fact to hold alongside the value that they are owed help.

**Emotional safety.** Disaster and loss are heavy. Use examples with hope attached and
name agency — what people *did* to recover, not only what they suffered. Avoid graphic
detail; offer opt-outs; remind learners that "not yet ready to discuss this" is honored.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What are the four blocks of trust? Now imagine a whole community
   that behaves this way, at scale. What would it be able to withstand?
2. **Meet the roots (10 min).** Worked example, together. Take a real pattern: a
   community hit by a flood. Compare two pictures:
   - One where neighbors barely know each other, one leader holds all decisions, and
     rebuilding copies exactly what was there before.
   - One where neighbors already share food and watch each other's children, many people
     can lead and decide, and rebuilding asks "what should we change so the next flood
     hurts less?"
   The second recovers — not because it is luckier, but because of three roots: **shared
   care and mutual aid**, **many ways to decide and lead**, and **the ability to change
   and adapt.**
3. **Study two examples (20 min).** From the provided case studies (or your own
   knowledge), choose two communities — one historical, one contemporary — from different
   world regions. For each, name: the *shock*, the *roots* it drew on, and the *outcome*.
   Look for the three roots at work.
4. **Map a community you know (15 min).** In your journal, choose one community you know
   (a neighborhood, a school, a faith or cultural group, a town). Write: *Here are its
   deep roots (which of the three does it have, and how do I know?); here is one root
   that is thin; and here is one thing that could strengthen it.*
5. **Close (5 min).** Put the diagram and the map in your portfolio. Say or write:
   *resilience is planted before the storm, in the roots.*

## Differentiation

- **Mode (expression):** Map by writing, drawing the tree and roots, or speaking;
  expression is flexible.
- **Support:** Provide one worked case study with the roots pre-labeled, and sentence
  frames for the map.
- **Extension:** Research one community's recovery in depth and write a short analysis
  locating all three roots (or explaining why one was missing and what it cost).

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Does the map identify at least two of the three roots in a real
  community, name one thin root, and suggest one strengthening action?
- **Portfolio:** The case comparison and the community map, gathered toward D02.S3.11.01.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home: "What has our family or neighborhood survived, and what got us
through it?" Listen for the roots — shared care, many hands, adapting.
