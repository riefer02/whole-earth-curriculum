# Lesson 14 — Strategies and Effects: Comparing Movements

## Summary

Movements choose their tools deliberately: a long walk, a mass refusal, a protective
embrace. Learners compare three strategies — the 1930 Salt March, a nonviolent act of
civil disobedience against the British salt tax in India (S-390); the 1952 Defiance
Campaign in South Africa, which defied unjust pass laws (S-146); and the Chipko
movement of 1970s India, where villagers, especially women, embraced trees to stop them
being felled (S-328). They weigh each strategy's aim, risk, and effect, and judge when a
strategy fits its struggle.

## Objectives

- Compare the strategies and effects of social movements across countries, and judge
  when a strategy fits its aim. _(D08.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

The same injustice can be met many ways: you can walk out, sit down, refuse to buy,
refuse to obey, or stand between the thing you love and the force that would take it.
Each has a different cost and a different reach — and choosing among them is the art of
resistance.

## Materials

- Strategy comparison chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy the chart; prepare the three strategy story cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 13, the shared pattern of aim, strategy, and effect.

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: **judge a strategy by its fit** — aim, risk, and effect together.
The **ethics** lens: nonviolent civil disobedience accepts a cost (arrest, punishment) to
expose an unjust law — a choice, not an accident. The **egalitarian** lens: the Chipko
movement was led in large part by village women defending the forests their communities
depended on (S-328) — a reminder that resistance has many faces, not only famous
leaders. The **environment** lens: Chipko links the struggle for dignity to the defense
of the land itself. The **global** lens: three continents, one shared repertoire.
The **critical-thinking** lens: no single strategy is "best" in the abstract; learners
argue fit for a specific struggle.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 13: name the three movements and what they shared.
   Today we zoom into *how* — the strategy.
2. **The Salt March (12 min).** In 1930, Mohandas Gandhi led a long march to the sea to
   make salt in defiance of the British salt tax — a nonviolent act of civil disobedience
   within India's independence movement (S-390). Fill the chart: strategy (march and
   civil disobedience), aim (defy an unjust tax, build independence), risk (arrest),
   effect (galvanized the movement).
3. **The Defiance Campaign (12 min).** In 1952, South Africans deliberately broke unjust
   pass laws and accepted arrest, in a mass campaign of noncooperation (S-146). Fill the
   chart: strategy (mass defiance), aim (repeal the pass laws), risk (arrest), effect
   (grew the movement against apartheid).
4. **Chipko (12 min).** In 1970s India, villagers — especially women — embraced the
   trees to stop them being cut, protecting the forests their communities lived by
   (S-328). Fill the chart: strategy (direct action, embrace), aim (save the forest),
   risk (confrontation), effect (forests protected; a model of environmental defense).
5. **Judge the fit (10 min).** With a partner: when does a march fit better than a mass
   arrest campaign or a protective embrace? Judge each strategy against its aim.
6. **Journal (4 min).** Write: *"I would choose… strategy for… struggle because…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a sorting activity matching strategy to aim before writing.
- **Extension:** Compare a fourth strategy — the peaceful resistance of Parihaka in 1881
  (S-144) — and place it in the chart.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name each strategy, its aim and risk, and
  argue when a strategy fits its struggle?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed strategy chart, completing the
  social-movements comparison.

## Home connection

Think of one change you want in your community. Choose a strategy — a walk, a refusal, a
protective act — and write one sentence on why it fits.
