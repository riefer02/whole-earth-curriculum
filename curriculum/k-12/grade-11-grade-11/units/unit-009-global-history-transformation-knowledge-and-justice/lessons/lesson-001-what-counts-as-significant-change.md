# Lesson 1 — What Counts as Significant Change?

## Summary

Every history book, film, and lesson makes a hidden choice: *which changes count as
important enough to include?* In this opening lesson, learners make that choice
visible. They build two timelines of the same years — one that marks rulers, battles,
and borders, and one that marks harvests, migrations, tools, and ideas — and notice
that the same span of time yields two different stories. Historians call this act of
choosing *significance*, and dividing time into named eras is *periodization* (S-314).

## Objectives

- Evaluate how the choice of what counts as significant change shapes historical
  accounts, using two contrasting examples built from the same years.
  _(D08.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of your own life in two tellings. One version lists the "big" things: a move, a
first day, a win or a loss. Another version lists the small daily things that quietly
shaped you: a habit, a friendship, a place you returned to. Both are true; neither is
complete. The same is true of whole peoples and centuries — what we decide to call
"significant" changes which story gets told.

## Materials

- Timeline strips (or string and pebbles)
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare the two sets of "event cards" for a shared recent span (see Procedure step 2).
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, the idea that historians divide time into periods and that
  periodization is a convention, not a fact of nature (S-314), and that historical
  thinking rests on sourcing and multiple causation (S-316).

## Facilitator note

The idea to land: **significance is a selection, and every selection favors some
people and some events over others.** The **critical-thinking** lens: learners name the
criterion behind each account ("why this and not that?"). The **egalitarianism** lens:
accounts centered only on rulers and wars leave out most people; centering ordinary
people, land, and ideas is a choice toward fairness. The **global** lens: different
peoples periodize their own pasts differently, and no single calendar or era is
"natural" (S-314). The **ethics** lens: who is remembered, and who is forgotten, is a
question with moral weight. Hold this lesson light — it is an opening frame, not a
verdict on any one nation's story.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: why do historians divide time into
   periods, and who decides where the dividing lines fall? (S-314) Say one thing you
   remember about "whose story gets told."
2. **Meet significance (10 min).** **Significance** means "important enough to be part
   of the account." Worked example: for the year a learner was born, list three
   different events — a national election, a family arrival, a new technology that
   changed daily life. Each is "significant" to a different story.
3. **Build the first timeline (12 min).** On a strip, place a set of events chosen by
   one criterion — rulers, battles, and borders. Label the criterion at the top:
   *"What rulers and states did."*
4. **Build the contrasting timeline (12 min).** On a second strip, place events from
   the *same years* chosen by a different criterion — harvests and climate, migrations,
   tools, and ideas. Label it: *"What ordinary people and the land lived through."*
5. **Compare (10 min).** Set the two timelines side by side. Discuss with a partner:
   which events appear on both? Which appear on only one? What does each account make
   invisible?
6. **Journal (4 min).** Write: *"The two timelines tell different stories because…"*
7. **Close (2 min).** Say in one sentence why "significant" is a choice, not a fact.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work from a single, familiar span (this school year); give a fixed menu
  of events to sort rather than generating them.
- **Extension:** Add a third criterion (e.g., changes to the environment) and compare
  all three; name what a criterion *cannot* capture.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state the criterion behind each account
  and identify at least one event each account leaves out?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two contrasting timelines with the one-sentence
  "because…" journal entry, kept as the opening frame of the unit portfolio.

## Home connection

Retell one family story twice — once "big events only," once "small daily things
only." Ask a family member which version feels truer and why.
