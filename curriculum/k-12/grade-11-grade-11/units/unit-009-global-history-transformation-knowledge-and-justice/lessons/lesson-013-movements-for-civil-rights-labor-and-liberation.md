# Lesson 13 — Movements for Civil Rights, Labor, and Liberation

## Summary

Ordinary people, acting together, have changed the terms of power. Learners compare
three movements across three countries and three aims: **civil rights** — the Montgomery
bus boycott in the United States (1955–56), when Black residents refused segregated
buses for over a year until the buses were desegregated (S-324); **labor** — the Wave
Hill walk-off in Australia (1966), when Gurindji stockworkers led by Vincent Lingiari
struck for fair pay and the return of their land (S-206); and **liberation** — Ghana's
independence movement under Kwame Nkrumah, which used boycotts, strikes, and nonviolent
"Positive Action" until independence on 6 March 1957 (S-276). They compare aim,
strategy, and effect.

## Objectives

- Compare social movements for civil rights, labor, and liberation across different
  countries and analyze their strategies and effects. _(D08.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

A group of people who refuse together — to ride, to work, to obey an unjust rule — can
do what no single person can. That is the engine of every social movement: not one
hero, but many ordinary people making a shared refusal visible and costly to the
unjust order.

## Materials

- Movement comparison chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy the comparison chart; prepare the three movement story cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, that freedom and dignity are ideas that spread and fuel
  struggles across continents.

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: **compare movements by aim, strategy, and effect — and by who
gained.** The **egalitarian** lens: these are struggles of people denied rights, fair
pay, and self-rule, and they center ordinary people, not only leaders. The **global**
lens: the three cases span three continents, and each belongs to its own people's
history — never reduced to a single narrative. The **ethics** lens: nonviolence and
strikes are choices with costs and consequences, and learners weigh them fairly. The
**community** lens: movements are collective, built on mutual support and solidarity.
Note that the same strategy (boycott, strike, noncooperation) recurs across very
different struggles — a shared human repertoire of resistance.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: what is a social movement, and name one you
   remember. Today we compare three.
2. **Civil rights (12 min).** In Montgomery, Alabama (USA), Black residents boycotted
   segregated buses for over a year, beginning in December 1955 after Rosa Parks was
   arrested; the boycott ended in December 1956 when the buses were desegregated
   (S-324). Fill the chart: aim (equal treatment on buses), strategy (boycott,
   carpooling, noncooperation), effect (buses desegregated).
3. **Labor (12 min).** At Wave Hill station in Australia (1966), about 200 Gurindji
   stockworkers and their families, led by Vincent Lingiari, walked off the job seeking
   fair pay and the return of their land (S-206). Fill the chart: aim (fair pay and
   land), strategy (walk-off/strike), effect (a landmark step toward land rights).
4. **Liberation (12 min).** In Ghana, Kwame Nkrumah's Convention People's Party used
   boycotts, strikes, and nonviolent "Positive Action" against British colonial rule;
   Ghana became independent on 6 March 1957 — the first sub-Saharan African colony to do
   so (S-276). Fill the chart: aim (self-rule), strategy (boycotts, strikes, Positive
   Action), effect (independence).
5. **Compare (10 min).** With a partner: what do the three share? Where do strategy and
   aim differ? Who gained, and what was won?
6. **Journal (4 min).** Write one sentence: *"Movements differ in aim but share…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work from the pre-filled story cards and a mostly-filled chart.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth movement (e.g., the 1952 Defiance Campaign in South
  Africa, S-146) and place it in the comparison.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state each movement's aim, strategy, and
  effect, and name what the three share?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed comparison chart, as the first half of
  the social-movements comparison.

## Home connection

Ask an adult about a time people acted together to change something — in your family,
workplace, or town. Note the aim, the strategy, and what changed.
