# Lesson 9 — Settlement, Resources, and Environmental Change

## Summary

People settle where land, water, and materials are; they use those resources; and their
use changes the environment. Sometimes the loop turns back: a changed environment
reshapes the settlement itself. Learners trace this three-part loop through irrigation
— carrying water to crops from the Nile and Tigris-Euphrates to today (S-130) — and
through the Ancestral Pueblo center of Chaco Canyon, where a long drought in the late
1200s helped push people to migrate (S-382), and the Classic Maya cities, whose decline
is best explained by several pressures working together (S-378).

## Objectives

- Analyze the relationship between human settlement, resource use, and environmental
  change across regions. _(D08.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

Every settlement you have ever seen sits where it does for a reason: water to drink,
soil to grow food, wood or stone to build with, a road or river to travel. The place
gives, people take, and the place changes. That is the loop — and it runs beneath every
city, village, and farm on Earth.

## Materials

- Settlement-resource-environment diagram sheet
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy the three-part loop diagram sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, how societies changed and were changed by their environments
  over long spans of time, including adaptation and decline.

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: **trace a three-part loop** — settlement → resource use →
environmental change → back to settlement. Teach it with a worked example (irrigation),
then guide practice on the Chaco and Maya cases (S-011). The **environment** lens: this
is the unit's core environmental reasoning. The **critical-thinking** lens: the Maya
decline is not one cause but several working together — drought, deforestation, and
warfare — and good history resists single-cause stories (S-378). The **egalitarian**
lens: when resources run short, the burden rarely falls evenly. The **global** lens: the
same loop runs in the Nile Valley, the Southwest, and the Maya lowlands — one human
pattern, many places. Note that "decline" is not "disappearance": Maya and Pueblo
peoples are living peoples, their descendants present today.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: why do people settle near rivers and
   floodplains? Today we trace the full loop, not just the first step.
2. **The worked example: irrigation (12 min).** People settled where rivers and fertile
   land met. To grow more, they carried water to crops — **irrigation**, from the Nile
   and Tigris-Euphrates to today (S-130). Trace the loop: settlement → using river water
   → changed land (more crops, but sometimes salty or waterlogged soil) → settlement
   adjusts.
3. **Case one: Chaco Canyon (12 min).** The Ancestral Pueblo center of Chaco Canyon was
   a thriving regional center from about 850 to 1250 CE. In the late 1200s, sustained
   drought contributed to people migrating to new pueblos elsewhere — where their
   descendants live today (S-382). Fill the loop for Chaco.
4. **Case two: Classic Maya cities (12 min).** Classic Maya cities flourished from
   about 250 to 900 CE. Their decline is best explained by several pressures together —
   warfare, exhausted soil, and drought — not one cause (S-378). Fill the loop and note
   where more than one arrow points at once.
5. **Compare the loops (10 min).** With a partner: what do the three cases share? Where
   does the loop turn back on the people?
6. **Journal (4 min).** Write: *"Settlement, resources, and environment are one loop
   because…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use pre-drawn loop arrows and fill only the labels, not the whole diagram.
- **Extension:** Add a modern case (a growing city drawing down an aquifer) and trace
  the same loop.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace the loop for a case and explain how
  environmental change can feed back into settlement?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed loop diagrams for irrigation, Chaco, and
  the Maya, as the settlement-resource-environment analysis.

## Home connection

Find where your home's water comes from and where its waste and smoke go. Trace your
household's one small loop of settlement → resource use → environmental change.
