# Lesson 5 — Dominant and Marginalized Knowledge Traditions

## Summary

Peoples know the world in many ways — through experiment and written records, and
through long, place-based practice passed across generations. Some traditions became
**dominant**: their knowledge was treated as authoritative, written down, and taught in
institutions. Others were **marginalized**: dismissed or suppressed, often under
colonial rule. Learners compare two traditions that know the same land or forest, and
meet two reminders that marginalized knowledge is real and deep — Aboriginal cultural
burning (S-320) and the scholarly manuscripts of Timbuktu (S-201).

## Objectives

- Evaluate the relationship between dominant and marginalized knowledge traditions,
  comparing what each knows and offers. _(D08.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Two people can know the same river: one measures its flow and charts its water quality;
another has fished it for decades and reads the season by where the fish gather. The
two knowings are different, and both are real. But the world often counts only one as
"knowledge" — and that ranking has a history behind it.

## Materials

- Two-traditions comparison chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare the two accounts of the same land (step 2) or use cultural burning (S-320)
  against a formal fire-management description.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, the idea that indigenous and scientific ways of knowing
  generate and test knowledge in different ways.

## Facilitator note

Hold both traditions *side by side*, never ranking one as "primitive." The
**egalitarianism** lens: asking who was credited and who was dismissed is a fairness
question. The **global** lens: Timbuktu's manuscripts (astronomy, mathematics, medicine,
law) prove that written scholarship flourished outside Europe and was honored on its
own terms (S-201); cultural burning shows knowledge held in practice, not just in text
(S-320). The **ethics** lens: calling one people's knowledge "not knowledge" was part of
colonial power, and it caused real loss. The **environment** lens: place-based knowledge
often encodes long observation of a living system. Frame this as "both are knowledge,
differently," and let learners weigh what each offers.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: name two ways people can know the same
   natural thing (e.g., a forest, a season). Today we ask why one way became "official."
2. **Meet the terms (10 min).** A **knowledge tradition** is a people's shared way of
   knowing — how they observe, test, record, and pass on understanding. A **dominant**
   tradition is treated as authoritative; a **marginalized** one is pushed aside or
   denied legitimacy. These positions come from power and history, not from how true the
   knowledge is.
3. **Compare two traditions (15 min).** Read the two accounts of the same land: one
   formal and written, one place-based and held in practice. On the chart, fill "what it
   knows," "how it knows," "who is credited," and "what it offers."
4. **Two reminders (10 min).** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have used
   **cultural burning** to care for Country for thousands of years — knowledge held in
   practice, not always in writing (S-320). And in Timbuktu, centuries of written
   scholarship in astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and law were gathered and honored
   (S-201). Both are real, deep knowledge.
5. **Discuss legitimacy (10 min).** With a partner: why did one tradition become
   "official" while the other was marginalized? What was lost when one was denied?
6. **Journal (5 min).** Write one sentence beginning: *"Both traditions are knowledge
   because…"* and one beginning: *"One was marginalized because…"*

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort pre-written "knows / how it knows / offers" cards under the two
  tradition headings.
- **Extension:** Compare cultural burning (S-320) with a formal fire-science account and
  name one place they agree and one place they differ.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner compare what the two traditions know and
  offer, and explain — in historical terms, not by ranking — why one became dominant?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed comparison chart and the two journal
  sentences, as the first part of the knowledge-traditions argument.

## Home connection

Ask an elder or neighbor how they know something in your place — weather, soil, a
recipe, a repair. Write down what their knowledge knows and offers, set beside the
"official" version.
