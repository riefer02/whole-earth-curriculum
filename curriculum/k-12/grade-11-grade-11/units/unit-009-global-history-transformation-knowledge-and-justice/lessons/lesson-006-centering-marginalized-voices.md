# Lesson 6 — Centering Marginalized Voices

## Summary

Centering a marginalized voice is not the same as adding a footnote. It means treating
that voice as a source of authority, crediting its knowledge, and letting it set some of
the questions. Learners practice this directly: first in a "centered circle" where the
previously quieted voices speak first, then in a written argument for centering, using
claim, reason, and evidence and answering a counterargument fairly.

## Objectives

- Argue for how marginalized voices can be centered, and explain how centering changes
  what is known, credited, and possible. _(D08.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of a group project where one person's idea was always talked over. When someone
finally said "let them finish," the whole plan changed — the group heard what it had
been missing. Centering is that move, practiced on the scale of whole knowledge
traditions that were long talked over.

## Materials

- Argument planner
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare the argument planner and a short example of a "centered" versus "added-on"
  treatment of a voice.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, the terms dominant and marginalized knowledge tradition.

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: a **centering argument** — claim, reason, evidence, and a fair
answer to a counterargument (S-011 models this explicitly). The **egalitarianism**
lens: centering is a repair of unfair credit and authority, not charity. The **ethics**
lens: who gets to speak and be believed is a justice question. The **global** lens:
across the world, peoples whose knowledge was suppressed under colonialism are now
having it recognized, credited, and in some cases returned (repatriated). The
**critical-thinking** lens: learners answer the real counterargument — "does centering
one voice silence another?" — by distinguishing *centering* (giving the silenced a full
seat) from *silencing* others. Keep the discussion cooperative, not competitive.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: what made one knowledge tradition dominant and
   another marginalized? Today we argue for the repair.
2. **Centering vs. adding on (10 min).** Worked example: adding a voice means quoting it
   once at the end; centering means asking it the first question, crediting it, and
   letting it shape the questions. Which changes the outcome more, and why?
3. **The centered circle (12 min).** In a circle, pose a real question from the unit
   (e.g., "how should this forest be cared for?"). The rule: those who have been quieted
   — or who hold a tradition usually left out — speak first. Listen without interrupting.
4. **Plan the argument (15 min).** On the planner, write: **Claim** — marginalized
   voices should be centered because… **Reason(s)** — centering recovers lost knowledge
   and repairs unfair credit. **Evidence** — name one tradition (e.g., cultural burning,
   S-320) and what centering it has changed. **Counterargument** — answer the worry
   that centering silences others.
5. **Write (10 min).** Turn the planner into one tight paragraph in your journal.
6. **Close (3 min).** One volunteer reads their claim aloud. Preview: next we ask how
   peoples have kept their *languages* and knowledge alive under pressure to assimilate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for claim, reason, and counterargument.
- **Extension:** Write the argument twice — once centering a knowledge tradition, once
  centering a language — and note what shifts.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the argument state a claim, give a reason backed by
  a named example, and answer a counterargument fairly?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed argument planner and paragraph, as the
  knowledge-traditions argument for the unit.

## Home connection

In a family or friend conversation, try the centered-circle rule once: let the person
usually talked over speak first. Note what you heard that you might have missed.

## Resources

- Explicit modeling and guided practice for argument skills: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark
  (2006) (S-011).
- The Timbuktu manuscripts as evidence of marginalized written scholarship: UNESCO
  Memory of the World (S-201).
