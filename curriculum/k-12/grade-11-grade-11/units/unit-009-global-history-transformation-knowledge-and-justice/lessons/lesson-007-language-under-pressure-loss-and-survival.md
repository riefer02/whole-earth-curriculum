# Lesson 7 — Language Under Pressure: Loss and Survival

## Summary

A language is more than words: it names a people's world, carries its knowledge, and
holds its identity. Across the globe, peoples have been pressured to give up their
mother tongues and speak a dominant language instead. Learners analyze what threatens a
language, what keeps it alive, and what is lost when it is lost — drawing on the roughly
7,000 languages spoken today, many endangered (S-426), and on writers who lived between
languages under colonial rule (S-424) and at the border between them (S-425).

## Objectives

- Analyze how peoples have maintained their languages under pressure to assimilate, and
  what is carried — and lost — in a language. _(D08.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of a word in your home or family that has no perfect translation — a name for a
kind of weather, a dish, a way of being together. That word *knows* something. When a
language stops being spoken, the things it alone could name begin to fade with it.

## Materials

- Language-loss-and-survival chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare the short excerpts from Ngũgĩ (S-424) and Anzaldúa (S-425), or paraphrase
  their core idea.
- Retrieval: from earlier grades, that language carries culture and that many peoples
  keep more than one language at once.

## Facilitator note

Hold this with care: many learners carry a language that has been pressured or lost in
their own family, and the topic is close. The **egalitarianism** lens: the pressure to
assimilate has fallen unevenly, on colonized and minoritized peoples most of all. The
**global** lens: this is not one region's story — roughly 7,000 languages are spoken
today, many endangered (S-426). The **ethics** lens: forbidding a child's mother tongue
was an act of power with real, lasting harm. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguish
the *fact* of language loss from the *value* that every language matters. Affirm home
languages as wealth (philosophy §multilingualism), and let learners who speak more than
one language share freely — never as specimens.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: how is knowledge passed between
   generations? One way is language. Today we study what happens when a language is
   pressured out of use.
2. **The scale (8 min).** More than 7,000 languages are spoken today, and many are
   endangered (S-426). A language is endangered when fewer and fewer children learn it
   as their first tongue. What does it mean that so many ways of naming the world are at
   risk?
3. **What threatens a language (12 min).** Under colonial rule and nation-building,
   children were often forbidden to speak their mother tongue at school and were taught
   in the colonizer's or state's language instead. The Kenyan writer Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o
   wrote that being made to write in English rather than Gikuyu distanced him from his
   people's own culture and history (S-424). Fill the "what threatened it" column.
4. **What a language carries (12 min).** The writer Gloria Anzaldúa lived between
   Chicano Spanish and English and wrote that one's languages are part of one's identity
   (S-425). With a partner, list what a language carries: names for land and seasons,
   stories, kinship, knowledge, identity. Fill "what was lost with it."
5. **What kept it alive (10 min).** Even under pressure, languages survived because
   families kept speaking them at home, songs and stories were passed on, and speakers
   refused to let them go. Fill "what kept it alive." Share one word from a home language
   if you wish, and what it carries.
6. **Journal (8 min).** Write: *"When a language is lost, we lose…"* — three concrete
   things, from the chart or your own life.
7. **Close (2 min).** Preview: next lesson we meet the people who are *bringing their
   languages home*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fixed list of "what a language carries" to match to examples
  before writing.
- **Extension:** Compare Ngũgĩ's account (S-424) with Anzaldúa's (S-425): how does each
  writer's language situation differ, and what is shared?

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name what threatens a language, what
  keeps it alive, and at least two concrete things carried in a language?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed chart and the "when a language is lost"
  journal entry, as the first part of the revitalization analysis.

## Home connection

Ask a family member for one word or phrase from your family's languages that has no
perfect translation, and write down what it carries.

## Resources

- Roughly 7,000 living languages and their endangerment: Ethnologue (S-426).
- Language carrying culture and history, and the cost of writing in the colonizer's
  language: Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Decolonising the Mind* (S-424).
- Living between languages and language as identity: Gloria Anzaldúa,
  *Borderlands/La Frontera* (S-425).
