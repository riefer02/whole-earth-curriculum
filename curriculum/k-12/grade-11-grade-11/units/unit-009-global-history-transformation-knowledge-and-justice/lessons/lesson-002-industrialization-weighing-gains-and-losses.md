# Lesson 2 — Industrialization: Weighing Gains and Losses

## Summary

Industrialization — the shift to machine-made goods, factories, and cities, powered in
large part by coal and oil — transformed human life over the last two and a half
centuries. It brought gains (cheaper goods, new medicine, faster travel) and losses
(displacement, dangerous work, polluted air and water), and those gains and losses did
not fall evenly. Learners build a "gains-and-losses" chart that always asks *for
whom?*, and meet the peppered moth as a small, real sign of industrialization's
environmental reach (S-304).

## Objectives

- Analyze industrialization as a long-term transformation and weigh what was gained and
  lost for different peoples and the environment. _(D08.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

The phone or cloth or bicycle near you was almost certainly made in a factory far away,
then carried across the world by fossil-fueled transport. That single object is a
thread connecting you to industrial work, to shipping, and to the air that the making
of it touched. "Gain" and "loss" are not abstract — they live inside everyday things.

## Materials

- Gains-and-losses chart
- History journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-column chart ("for whom? | gained | lost").
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, the Columbian Exchange and the growth of global trade
  (S-273), and from earlier grades how a tool or technology changes how people live.
- Prepare the worked example (step 2) and the moth card (step 5).

## Facilitator note

Teach the *skill* explicitly — a balance-sheet of change with a "for whom?" column —
then guide practice (S-011). The **egalitarianism** lens is central: a transformation is
not one experience but many, and naming who gained and who was left out is the honest
way to tell it. The **environment** lens: industrialization drew on coal and oil and
changed air, land, and water; fossil-fuel emissions have risen sharply since the
industrial era began (S-006). The **technology** lens: machines and energy are tools
whose benefits and burdens travel unevenly. The **global** lens: industrialization did
not happen the same way or at the same time everywhere, and its first centers do not
tell the whole story. Do not teach a single nation's triumph; teach a many-voiced,
many-costed change.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From earlier grades: what did the Columbian Exchange move, and
   what happened when new plants and animals crossed oceans? (S-273) Today we study a
   later, faster exchange: machines and fossil energy.
2. **Meet the weigh-in (10 min).** **Industrialization** = the shift to machines,
   factories, and cities. Worked example on the chart: *for factory owners*, gained
   wealth and power; *for mill workers*, gained wages but lost health and time; *for
   the air and river*, lost — smoke and waste. Note the "for whom?" column doing the
   work.
3. **Weigh more peoples (15 min).** With a partner, add rows: children who worked
   mills; farmers pushed off common land into cities; families who could finally buy
   cheap cloth; people in distant colonies who supplied raw cotton and bought back
   finished cloth. For each, fill "gained" and "lost."
4. **The environment's row (8 min).** Add a row for the environment. Coal smoke
   darkened industrial cities. In England, the peppered moth's dark form became common
   in sooty air, then declined again as the air was cleaned — a small, real trace of
   industrialization written on living things (S-304).
5. **Write the judgment (12 min).** In your journal, answer: *Was industrialization
   more gain or more loss?* First say "for whom," then give your reasoned judgment —
   evidence for gains, evidence for losses, and who carried each.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one "for whom" row that surprised you. Preview: next
   lesson turns to the recent *acceleration* of human impact on the whole Earth.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide pre-filled "gained/lost" cards to sort under each "for whom?"
  heading before writing.
- **Extension:** Compare industrialization in two regions (e.g., a European textile
  center and a colony that supplied its cotton) and note how the same machine produced
  opposite experiences.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Does the chart name *for whom* each gain and loss fell,
  including the environment, and does the judgment weigh both sides with evidence?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed gains-and-losses chart plus the written
  judgment, as the first long-transformation analysis.

## Home connection

Choose one everyday object at home. Trace it back one step — where was it made, what
power likely ran the factory, how far did it travel? List one gain and one loss of its
journey.
