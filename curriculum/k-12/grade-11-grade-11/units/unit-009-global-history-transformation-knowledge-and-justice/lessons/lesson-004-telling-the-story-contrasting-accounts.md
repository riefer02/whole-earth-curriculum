# Lesson 4 — Telling the Story: Contrasting Accounts of Significance

## Summary

The same change can be told many ways, and each telling chooses what to count as
significant. Learners compare two accounts of one transformation — say, the growth of
factory production — one written from the vantage of owners, states, and machines, the
other from workers, families, and the land. They name each account's criterion, what it
omits, and why a *single story* about a people or a place is dangerous (S-417). This
deepens the unit's first essential question: significance is chosen, and contrasting
accounts reveal the choosing.

## Objectives

- Evaluate how the choice of what counts as significant change shapes historical
  accounts, using contrasting examples and naming what each omits. _(D08.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Two people can describe the same street after a storm: one notes the blocked road and
the fallen power line; the other notes the flooded basement and the neighbor who helped
carry the water out. Both are true. Neither is the whole street. History works the same
way — every account lights up some things and leaves others in shadow.

## Materials

- Two-account comparison sheet
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare two short contrasting accounts of one change (see step 2) or use the ones in
  the lesson.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, that significance is a choice (S-314).

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: **read an account for its criterion (what it counts as significant)
and its omissions (what it leaves out).** The **critical-thinking** lens: learners name
the vantage of each account and test it against another. The **egalitarianism** lens:
centering only the powerful omits most people; a fair account widens the lens. The
**ethics** lens: what we omit from a story can erase real lives and real harms. The
**global** lens: no people is "one story"; Adichie's warning against the single story is
a global discipline (S-417). Keep the examples respectful and specific; the aim is to
sharpen reading, not to mock any one account.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: what makes an event "significant"? Say it in one
   sentence. Today we read two tellings of the same change.
2. **Read account one (8 min).** Read the first account of the growth of factory
   production — one that centers owners, machines, and a nation's rising wealth. On your
   sheet, note its **criterion** (what it treats as important) and its **omissions**.
3. **Read account two (8 min).** Read the contrasting account — one that centers
   workers' hours and injuries, families displaced into cities, and smoke over the
   river. Note its criterion and omissions.
4. **Compare (12 min).** With a partner, fill the comparison sheet: what does each
   account light up? What does each leave in shadow? Where do they disagree about what
   *mattered*?
5. **The single story (10 min).** The writer Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie warns of "the
   danger of a single story" — knowing only one account of a people or place flattens
   them into that one tale (S-417). Discuss: which of the two accounts, read alone,
   would be a "single story," and what would it hide?
6. **Journal (10 min).** Write: *"A fair account of this change must include both…
   because…"* Name at least one thing each account recovers that the other omits.
7. **Close (2 min).** Preview: next we turn from change over time to *knowledge* — whose
   knowing has been counted as authoritative, and whose has been pushed aside.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a fixed set of "lit up / left out" phrases to sort under each
  account before writing.
- **Extension:** Write a short third account from a vantage neither text includes
  (e.g., a river, a child laborer), naming its new criterion.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state each account's criterion and name
  at least one omission in each, and explain why no single account suffices?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The comparison sheet and the "must include both…"
  journal entry, as the significance-choice evaluation.

## Home connection

Find one news report or family retelling of a shared event. Ask: whose story does it
center, and who or what does it leave out? Say what a second account would add.
