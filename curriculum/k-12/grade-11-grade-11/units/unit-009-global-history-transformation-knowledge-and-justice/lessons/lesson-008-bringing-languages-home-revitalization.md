# Lesson 8 — Bringing Languages Home: Revitalization

## Summary

Language loss is not fate. Learners study four documented cases of revitalization — the
Māori kōhanga reo "language nests" begun in 1982, the Hawaiian Pūnana Leo preschools
begun in 1984, Welsh in schools and media, and the twentieth-century revival of Hebrew
(S-428) — and extract the shared pattern: immersion from the youngest age, elders and
children together, and a community that decides the language matters. They see that
language and ecological knowledge travel together (S-427) and plan a small
revitalization act of their own.

## Objectives

- Analyze how peoples have revitalized their languages and knowledge under pressure to
  assimilate, and what makes revitalization work. _(D08.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

A language lives when a child learns it and, in turn, speaks it to another child. That
is the whole engine of revitalization: not a museum of words, but the warm loop of one
generation passing a living tongue to the next — like a seedling handed hand to hand.

## Materials

- Revitalization case-study sheet
- History journal

## Preparation

- Prepare one-paragraph summaries of the four cases (S-428), or the case-study sheet
  with the key facts pre-filled.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, what threatens a language and what it carries.

## Facilitator note

The skill to land: **compare cases to find a shared pattern** — immersion, the
intergenerational link, community will, and the connection of language to land and
knowledge. The **egalitarianism** lens: revitalization is a people's own act of repair,
not a favor granted. The **global** lens: the cases span Aotearoa New Zealand, Hawaiʻi,
Wales, and the Jewish diaspora — one human pattern in many places. The **ethics** lens:
the earlier pressure to assimilate was a harm; revitalization is a healing. The
**community** lens: this is collective work, elders and children together. Keep it
hopeful and concrete — language revitalization is one of the great recoveries of the
last century, and it is still happening.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: name one thing a language carries. Today: how
   peoples have brought those languages back.
2. **Meet four cases (15 min).** For each case, fill the sheet — *who*: (a) Māori
   kōhanga reo, language nests begun 1982; (b) Hawaiian Pūnana Leo preschools, begun
   1984; (c) Welsh in schools and media; (d) Hebrew, revived over the twentieth century
   (S-428). *What they did*: immersed the youngest children in the language, with elders
   and community behind them. *What worked*: new generations became fluent speakers.
3. **Find the pattern (10 min).** With a partner, list the three or four things every
   case shares. Where do the cases begin — with children, with elders, or with both?
4. **Language and land (8 min).** The botanist Robin Wall Kimmerer writes that learning
   the Potawatomi language changed how she saw plants — the language names them as
   persons, not things, so the language carries an ethic of care (S-427). Discuss: how
   is a language also ecological knowledge?
5. **Plan a small act (12 min).** In your journal, design one tiny revitalization step
   for a language you know or care about — a word a day, a story circle, recording an
   elder, teaching one song. Name who would be in the circle.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share one step. Preview: we now turn to the land itself —
   settlement, resources, and a changing climate.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work from the pre-filled case sheet; choose one case to retell as a
  three-step story.
- **Extension:** Connect the Kimmerer language-land link (S-427) to a named plant or
  place in your region, and say what its name teaches.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the shared pattern across cases and
  explain how language carries ecological knowledge and why immersion matters?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The case-study sheet and the mini revitalization plan,
  as the revitalization analysis for the unit.

## Home connection

Learn one new word or phrase in a language of your family or community, and teach it to
one other person — completing one loop of the revitalization engine.

## Resources

- Four documented revitalization cases (Māori kōhanga reo 1982; Hawaiian Pūnana Leo
  1984; Welsh; twentieth-century Hebrew revival): Leanne Hinton (ed.), *Bringing Our
  Languages Home* (S-428).
- Language linked to ecological knowledge: Robin Wall Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*
  (S-427).
- Roughly 7,000 living languages: Ethnologue (S-426).
