# Lesson 6 — Common Ancestry and the Tree of Life

## Summary

Learners build the case for **common ancestry** from three lines of evidence:
**fossils** laid down over deep time, **homologous structures** shared across species,
and **DNA sequences** that grow more similar the more recently two species branched.
They read all three as a **tree of life** — a branching pattern, not a ladder with
humans "on top" — and state what each line of evidence can and cannot show.

## Objectives

- Explain the evidence for common ancestry from fossils, anatomy, and DNA, and read it
  as a branching tree of life. _(D06.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Look at your own hand: the pattern of bones — one, then two, then a fan — appears,
rearranged, in a whale's flipper, a bat's wing, and a bird's wing. The most natural
reading is not that each was invented separately, but that all four were *modified
from one shared plan*. And just as your close relatives resemble you more than distant
strangers do, the DNA of two species is more alike the more recently they split from a
common ancestor. Read together, these are the marks of one family — the tree of life.

## Materials

- Common-ancestry evidence chart
- Science journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the common-ancestry evidence chart.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, variation, inheritance, and selection. Today we ask what
  those, run backward in time, imply: shared ancestors.
- Prepare drawings or images of homologous structures and a simple branching tree.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **common ancestry is
supported by three converging lines of evidence — the fossil record in ordered rock
layers, homologous structures (the same body plan modified in different species), and
DNA sequences whose similarity tracks how recently two species branched (S-303,
S-177).** The result is a **branching tree**, not a ladder — every living species is a
leaf, none "higher" than another. Teach each line with a worked example (S-011), and
hold the humility: fossils are partial, and DNA shows relationship but not "progress."
Earth itself is about 4.54 billion years old — standard geology (S-308) — and life's
fossil record stretches across deep time in ordered rock layers.

The **intellectual** lens: independent lines of evidence that point the same way are
stronger than any one line alone. The **critical-thinking** lens: learners ask of each
line "what exactly does this show, and what does it *not* show?" The **global** lens:
the tree of life includes every people and every species on equal footing — it is a
picture of relatedness, not rank. The **ethics** lens: reading ourselves as one branch
among many, not the crown of a ladder, quietly reshapes how we treat other species
(previewing Lesson 7). Preview: Lesson 7 turns to the distinction between this
*evidence* and *beliefs about life's meaning*.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5, what makes a population shift over generations?
   Today we run that process backward: what does it imply about shared beginnings?
2. **Fossils and deep time (12 min).** **Fossils** appear in ordered layers of rock:
   older forms lie beneath younger ones, and forms appear, persist, and vanish in
   sequence over **deep time** (S-308). This is a record of change, not a snapshot.
   Record on your chart: what fossils show (change and sequence) and their limit
   (the record is incomplete).
3. **Homologous structures (12 min).** A **homologous structure** is the same body
   plan modified for different uses — a human hand, a whale flipper, a bat wing. Their
   shared pattern is best explained by a **common ancestor**, not separate invention
   (S-303). Compare them, and record what anatomy shows.
4. **DNA evidence (12 min).** Species that branched recently share more similar **DNA
   sequences** than species that branched long ago — the same logic by which relatives
   resemble each other more than strangers do (S-177). Record what DNA shows, and its
   limit: it measures *how related*, not "how advanced."
5. **Read the tree (10 min).** Draw a branching tree. Every living species is a leaf;
   branches that meet low on the tree shared an ancestor long ago, branches that meet
   high shared one recently. There is no "top" and no "higher" species — only related
   leaves.
6. **Close (4 min).** In your journal, write one sentence each for what fossils,
   anatomy, and DNA tell us about our place among living things.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compare just two visible body plans (hand and flipper) and draw a
  two-branch tree, leaving DNA as a mention.
- **Extension:** Explain why DNA and fossils are *independent* lines of evidence, and
  what it means that they point the same way; note a case where one line alone would
  mislead.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name three lines of evidence for common
  ancestry, say what each shows and its limit, and read the relationship as a
  branching tree rather than a ladder?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed common-ancestry evidence chart.

## Home connection

Find two living things you can see from home — two trees, two birds, two plants — and
write which traits they share and which differ, and what that might say about how
closely related they are.
