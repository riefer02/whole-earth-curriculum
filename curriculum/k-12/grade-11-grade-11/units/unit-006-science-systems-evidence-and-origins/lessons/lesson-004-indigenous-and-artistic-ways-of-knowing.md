# Lesson 4 — Indigenous and Artistic Ways of Knowing

## Summary

Learners complete the three-way comparison begun in Lesson 3. They examine **indigenous
ways of knowing** — observation built up over generations, carried in **oral
tradition**, and bound to **reciprocity** with the land — and **artistic ways of
knowing** — close attention, craft, and embodied expression that test truth by
resonance and fidelity. They name what each offers that science alone cannot, and
hold the three ways side by side **without ranking** them.

## Objectives

- Compare scientific, indigenous, and artistic ways of knowing: how each generates and
  tests knowledge, and what each offers. _(D06.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of what you know that no test has ever measured: which field floods each year,
when the fish return, how your grandmother's recipe should *feel* between your
fingers, what a song in your home language makes you feel. These are knowledge — real,
hard-won, and passed on — yet they were not produced by a laboratory. Different ways
of knowing are not rivals; they are different instruments for different songs. Naming
them fairly, without ranking one above another, is part of honoring the many peoples
who hold them.

## Materials

- Ways-of-knowing comparison chart (from Lesson 3)
- Science journal

## Preparation

- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, the scientific loop and its strengths and limits. Today we
  complete the other two columns.
- Prepare a respectful example of each: an Indigenous knowledge practice (such as
  cultural burning or a seasonal calendar) and an artwork or song that carries
  knowledge. Present these as living traditions of specific peoples, not as specimens.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **indigenous ways of
knowing build knowledge through observation accumulated over generations, carried in
oral tradition and law, and tested against the long-term health of land and people;
artistic ways of knowing build knowledge through close attention, craft, and embodied
expression, tested by resonance, fidelity, and the honesty of the making.** These are
not "lesser science" — they are different ways, each with its own standards
(philosophy §11). Cite real, named practices: Aboriginal cultural burning (S-320,
S-321), the reciprocity ethic in Robin Wall Kimmerer's writing (S-427), and Aboriginal
and Torres Strait Islander art and oral tradition carrying knowledge of Country
(S-085). Present Indigenous knowledge as **living** and contemporary, held by specific
peoples, never as a single undifferentiated "ancient wisdom."

The **global** lens: knowledge lives in many traditions across the world; no single
people invented knowing. The **ethics** lens: it is a question of justice to honor
knowledge traditions that were suppressed and to name the peoples who hold them. The
**egalitarianism** lens: holding ways of knowing side by side without ranking is an
egalitarian act — knowledge is not a hierarchy. The **intellectual** lens: a fuller
picture of the world comes from more than one way of looking. Preview: Lessons 5–8
turn to a specific claim — evolution — that science grounds in evidence, and ask how
that sits beside beliefs about life's meaning.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, what does science offer, and what is it silent
   on? Today we complete the other two columns of the chart.
2. **Indigenous ways of knowing (15 min).** These build knowledge by observing a place
   closely over many generations, carrying it in **oral tradition**, law, and practice,
   and testing it against the long-term health of the land and the people. Worked
   example: Aboriginal cultural burning uses cool, careful fire to promote plant
   growth and reduce wildfire risk — knowledge held and applied over thousands of
   years (S-320, S-321). The reciprocity ethic — taking only what sustains, giving
   back — is one form of this knowledge (S-427). Fill in the column: how it asks (by
   long attention to a place), how it tests (by the land's health over generations),
   what it offers (deep, place-specific, relational knowledge).
3. **Artistic ways of knowing (15 min).** Art knows by **attention** and **making**: a
   painter sees a color the rest of us pass by, a song holds a feeling that a
   thermometer cannot, a story carries a truth about being human. It tests itself by
   fidelity (does it ring true?), craft (is it well made?), and resonance (does it move
   and teach?). Worked example: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander art and songline
   traditions carry knowledge of land, water, and law through story and image (S-085).
   Fill in the column.
4. **Compare without ranking (15 min).** Put the three columns side by side. Ask: how
   does each *ask*, how does each *test*, and what does each *offer*? Notice that the
   three answer different questions — and that a full human life needs all three.
5. **Close (5 min).** In your journal, write one sentence on what indigenous and
   artistic knowing each offer that science cannot.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Share one place or family knowledge of your own and tell how you came
  to know it, before filling in any formal vocabulary.
- **Extension:** Compare two Indigenous knowledge practices from different regions,
  noting what is shared (long observation, reciprocity) and what is particular to each
  place and people.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state how indigenous and artistic ways
  of knowing each generate and test knowledge, and give one thing each offers that
  science does not — without ranking the three?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed three-column comparison chart.

## Home connection

Ask an elder or neighbor in your own life: "What do you know about this place that
you learned by watching it, not from a book?" Write down their answer and how they
came to know it.
