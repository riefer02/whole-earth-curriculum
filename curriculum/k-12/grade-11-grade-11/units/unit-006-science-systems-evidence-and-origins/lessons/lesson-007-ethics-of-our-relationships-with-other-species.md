# Lesson 7 — Evidence and Meaning, and the Ethics of Other Species

## Summary

Learners draw the distinction the unit has been building toward: **evidence claims**
(how life actually changes, settled by observation) are different from **value
claims** (what life means and what we owe other species, settled by reasoning and
care). They then analyze the **ethics** of human relationships with other species,
weighing a species' **instrumental value** (useful to us) against its **intrinsic
value** (worth caring for in its own right), and meet a reciprocity ethic from an
Indigenous tradition.

## Objectives

- Distinguish the evidence for evolution from beliefs about life's meaning, and
  analyze the ethics of human relationships with other species. _(D06.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Two different sentences are often tangled together: "populations change over
generations" (a claim about *how things are*, testable and settled by evidence) and
"other animals deserve our care" (a claim about *how we ought to act*, settled by
reasoning and conscience). Evolution answers the first; it is silent on the second.
Untangling them lets you hold the science firmly *and* think clearly about what you
owe the creatures around you — the dog, the bird, the river, the forest — without
pretending one answers the other.

## Materials

- Evidence-vs-value and ethics organizer
- Science journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the organizer; prepare claim cards to sort.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, the evidence for evolution and common ancestry. Today
  we ask what that evidence does *not* decide.
- Prepare a respectful example of a reciprocity ethic toward the living world (e.g.,
  the ethic in Kimmerer's *Braiding Sweetgrass*, S-427).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **evidence claims
(descriptive — "populations change") and value claims (normative — "animals deserve
care") are different kinds of claim, settled by different means; the evidence for
evolution establishes the first and is silent on the second (S-303).** Then the ethics
analysis: a species can have **instrumental value** (what it gives us) and/or
**intrinsic value** (worth in itself), and different ethical stances weigh these
differently. Hold the value questions open (philosophy §11) — the lesson's job is to
help learners *reason* about them, not to decree an answer. Meet a reciprocity ethic
from an Indigenous tradition as a living way of relating (S-427), not as a specimen.

The **ethics** lens: this is the lesson's center — reasoning about what we owe others.
The **egalitarianism** lens: the question "who counts?" is an egalitarian question —
whether moral standing extends beyond the human. The **critical-thinking** lens:
learners separate the two claim types before arguing, so evidence is never stretched
to settle values. The **global** lens: many cultures hold different, serious ethics
toward other species; all deserve a fair hearing. Preview: Lesson 8 extends this
ethics to whole ecosystems — conservation and restoration.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6, what does the evidence show about how life
   changes? Today: what does that evidence *not* decide?
2. **Sort the claims (12 min).** Sort the cards into two piles: **evidence claims**
   (how things are — "populations change over generations," "fossils appear in ordered
   layers") and **value claims** (how we ought to act — "animals deserve care," "we
   should protect forests"). Notice they are settled differently: evidence by
   observation, values by reasoning and conscience. The evidence for evolution settles
   the first pile and is silent on the second (S-303).
3. **Instrumental and intrinsic value (12 min).** Ask of one species: is it valuable
   only because it is **useful to us** (instrumental value — food, medicine, work), or
   does it have **intrinsic value** (worth in itself, apart from use)? Different
   people, and different cultures, answer differently — and that difference is a
   *value* difference, not a scientific one.
4. **A reciprocity ethic (12 min).** Some traditions ground the relationship in
   **reciprocity**: the living world sustains us, so we owe it care in return — a
   relationship, not a resource (S-427). Compare this with the instrumental view: what
   changes in how you would treat a forest or a river?
5. **Argue a stance (10 min).** In your journal, write a short argument for one stance
   toward a species you know — instrumental, intrinsic, or reciprocal — naming your
   reasons and at least one reason someone might disagree.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "life evolved" and "life is
   precious" are different kinds of claim.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest cards and name what you owe one familiar animal,
  leaving formal vocabulary aside.
- **Extension:** Analyze a real trade-off (habitat versus development), weigh
  instrumental and intrinsic reasons on both sides, and argue a considered position.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner place a claim in the correct pile
  (evidence vs value), explain the difference, and argue a stance toward another
  species with reasons and a fair counterpoint?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed evidence-vs-value and ethics organizer.

## Home connection

Notice one way your household already treats another species — a pet, a plant, an
insect, a food animal. Write whether that treatment rests on instrumental value,
intrinsic value, or reciprocity.

## Resources

- On the evidence for evolution (the descriptive claim): Darwin, *On the Origin of
  Species* (1859) (S-303).
- On a reciprocity ethic toward the living world: Robin Wall Kimmerer, *Braiding
  Sweetgrass* (S-427).
- On worked examples and guided practice: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011).
