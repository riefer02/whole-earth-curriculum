# Lesson 11 — The Unit Circle and Radians

## Summary

Learners meet the **unit circle** — a circle of radius 1 — as the machine that turns
sine and cosine into functions of *every* angle, not just acute ones. They measure
angles in **radians** (the arc length on that circle), read the **coordinates** of a
point, and learn the **signs** of sine and cosine in each quadrant. They meet the
layered history of angle measurement.

## Objectives

- Use the unit circle and radians to find sine and cosine of any angle and to model
  periodic relationships. _(D05.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

A wheel, a clock, a planet's orbit, a dancer's spin — all turn *all the way around*,
not just partway. Angles measured in degrees stop at 360 and start over; the unit
circle lets an angle keep going forever, and sine and cosine keep repeating with it.
Measuring an angle in radians just means "how far along the rim has the tip traveled,
in units of the radius" — one natural way to measure a turn.

## Materials

- Unit circle problem sheet
- Compass and protractor (or a round object to trace)
- Math journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the problem sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 7–8, sine and cosine as repeating functions; from Grade 10,
  right-triangle trigonometry (D05.S3.10.01).
- Prepare a large unit circle for the wall and worked examples.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **the unit circle
has radius 1, so any angle θ lands at the point (cos θ, sin θ); a radian is the angle
whose arc equals the radius, so a full turn is 2π radians; sine is positive above the
x-axis and cosine positive to the right of the y-axis.** Teach the two moves
explicitly — *place the angle* (find the point) and *read the coordinates* (cos, sin)
— with worked examples and guided practice (S-011).

The **intellectual** lens: the circle generalizes sine and cosine from a triangle's
corner to any angle, and makes their repetition visible. The **global** lens: degrees
descend from Babylonian base-60 counting, while radians come from the circle's own
geometry — two languages for the same turn, both shared by everyone (S-291, S-429).
The **critical-thinking** lens: learners check a coordinate by asking which quadrant
the angle lands in and whether the signs match. The **environment** lens: every
orbit, tide, and season is an angle going around the circle — the unit circle is the
clock face of the natural world. Preview: Lesson 12 extends to general triangles.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10, sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse for a right triangle.
   Today the "hypotenuse" is always 1, so sin θ is just the height of a point.
2. **Meet the radian (12 min).** A **radian** is the angle whose arc equals the radius.
   On a circle of radius r, a full turn covers arc 2πr, so a full turn is 2π **radians**.
   Worked example: 180° = π radians, 90° = π/2, 60° = π/3, 45° = π/4, 30° = π/6.
3. **The unit circle (12 min).** On a circle of radius 1, an angle θ (measured from the
   positive x-axis) lands at the point **(cos θ, sin θ)**. Worked example: at θ = π/2
   the point is (0, 1), so cos(π/2) = 0 and sin(π/2) = 1; at θ = π the point is
   (−1, 0), so cos π = −1, sin π = 0. The signs follow the **quadrants**: sine is
   positive above the x-axis, cosine positive to the right of the y-axis.
4. **Special angles (10 min).** Read the familiar points: π/6 → (√3/2, ½), π/4 →
   (√2/2, √2/2), π/3 → (½, √3/2). Worked example: sin(π/3) = √3/2, cos(π/3) = ½.
5. **Guided practice (10 min).** With a partner: (a) convert 270° and 45° to radians;
   (b) find cos and sin of 3π/2 and 5π/4; (c) state the sign of sin θ in the third
   quadrant and why. Check each against the circle.
6. **Close (6 min).** Say why a full turn is 2π radians, and what the point (cos θ,
   sin θ) means.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work only with quadrantal angles (0, π/2, π, 3π/2) and the four sign
  regions first.
- **Extension:** Find sine and cosine of angles beyond 2π (e.g., 9π/4) using the
  period, and explain why the functions repeat.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner convert between degrees and radians,
  find (cos θ, sin θ) for special angles, and state quadrant signs, checked against
  the circle?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed unit-circle sheet, added to the
  geometry toolkit.

## Home connection

Trace a round object at home, mark its radius, and lay the radius along the rim to
see the radian. Estimate how many radii fit around the whole circle (it is about
6.28 — that is 2π).
