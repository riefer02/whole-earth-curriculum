# Lesson 8 — Reading Against Other Perspectives

## Summary

Learners practice reading a text **against other perspectives** — naming whose voice
the text leaves out and reconstructing what the text means from that position. They
learn that a counter-reading does not destroy a text but enriches it, and they
complete the unit's author-position analysis by reading the author *with* and *against*
other voices.

## Objectives

- Analyze how an author's cultural and historical position shapes a text, and read it
  against other perspectives — by asking whose voice is absent and what the text means
  from there. _(D04.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Every story has a silent character: the servant in the tale of the prince, the woman
in the story of the war, the colonized people in the colonizer's history, the quiet
sibling in the family legend. You know the feeling of being the one *not* spoken for —
or of reading a story about people like you written by someone who never asked you.
Reading against the text means letting the absent voice speak: not to burn the story,
but to see what it hid and what it could become. A text that survives its counter-
reading is stronger for it.

## Materials

- Counter-reading sheet (what it says / whose voice is missing / what it means from there)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Select a short text with a clear dominant voice and a clear absent voice.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, recall the four-question author-position probe. Today we
  read the text *against* positions the author did not include.
- Prepare the worked example marking one missing voice.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a text is shaped by
its author's position — and read against other perspectives, it reveals whose voice it
excluded; a counter-reading does not erase the text but completes the picture.** This
is the second half of the standard: reading the author *with* their position (Lesson 7)
and *against* other positions (Lesson 8). Frame counter-reading as *addition and
pressure*, never as destruction: the point is not to cancel a text but to notice the
frame around it. This protects learners from both naïve acceptance and reflexive
dismissal — the mature posture of the 9–12 band (development.md).

Model once, then let learners counter-read (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **egalitarian** lens is the spine: whose voices a text
includes and excludes is a question of power, and counter-reading is how the excluded
get heard. The **ethics** lens: reading against a text is fair when it is precise
("this passage assumes X") and unfair when it is a smear ("this author is simply bad")
— teach the difference. The **global** lens: a text that is foundational in one
culture may read very differently from another culture's position — hold both readings
side by side without ranking them. The **critical-thinking** lens: weigh whether the
counter-reading *overturns* the text's meaning or *deepens* it — often it does both at
once. Preview: Lesson 9 turns from reading to *writing* — your own extended original
work begins.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7: every author writes from a position. Today you
   ask what happens when a text is read from a position it never included.
2. **Meet the counter-reading (10 min).** Look at the counter-reading sheet. Three
   columns: **what the text says**, **whose voice is missing**, **what the text means
   from that voice's position**. Watch a worked example: a familiar story, marked with
   one silent character.
3. **Find the missing voice (12 min).** Read the selected text. Ask: who is spoken
   *about* but never speaks? Who is left out entirely? Name the voice, and write what
   their situation is.
4. **Reconstruct the counter-reading (12 min).** In that voice's position, re-read the
   text. What would they notice that the text hides? What would they say back to it?
   Write three sentences as if from that position — clearly labeled as an exercise in
   perspective, not a claim about a real person's views.
5. **Weigh the readings (8 min).** Compare the author's reading and the counter-reading
   side by side. Does the counter-reading overturn the text's meaning, deepen it, or
   both? Write one sentence of your verdict, with a reason.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: one text, many readings — and the strongest text
   survives being read against. Add this page to your author-position analysis. Next
   time: you become the author.

## Differentiation

- **Access (dyslexic, low-vision, blind):** Offer the text as audio or large print /
  braille / accessible digital text; a partner or screen reader may read it aloud, and
  the three columns may be filled by dictation or typing. The counter-reading — not the
  reading speed — is assessed.
- **Support:** Provide the missing voice already named; ask learners to write one thing
  that voice would notice or say.
- **Extension:** Find a real published retelling or counter-reading of a classic from
  another perspective, and compare it to the original in the three columns.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name an absent voice in a text,
  reconstruct what the text means from that position, and weigh whether the
  counter-reading overturns or deepens the text?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The counter-reading sheet, added to the portfolio as
  the "read against other perspectives" entry, completing the author-position analysis.

## Home connection

Tell an adult a story your family tells often, then ask together: *who in this story
never gets to speak?* Let the silent character talk for once.
