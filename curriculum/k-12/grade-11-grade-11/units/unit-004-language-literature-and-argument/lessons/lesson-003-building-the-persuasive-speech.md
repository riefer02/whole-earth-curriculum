# Lesson 3 — Building the Persuasive Speech: Claim, Reasons, Counterargument

## Summary

Learners build a persuasive speech as an **architecture of argument**: a claim,
reasons, and evidence — and, crucially, the *strongest counterargument*, stated fairly
and answered honestly. They learn the **steelman** (the opposite of a straw man) and
draft a speech scaffold in their home language, ready to be delivered in Lesson 4.

## Objectives

- Construct a persuasive speech that anticipates and fairly addresses counterarguments
  — beginning by building the argument's skeleton. _(D04.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

When you want a neighbor to help fix a shared wall, a school to change a rule, or your
family to try a new idea, you do not just state your wish — you give reasons, and you
silently know the objection already ("but it costs too much," "but we always do it the
old way"). A persuasive speech is that ordinary moment made deliberate: you say what
you want, why it is right, and — instead of hoping nobody brings up the problem — you
bring it up *yourself*, stated at full strength, and answer it. A position you cannot
state the best objection against is a position you do not yet understand.

## Materials

- Speech scaffold sheet (claim / reason / reason / evidence / strongest counterargument / honest response)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the six-box speech scaffold.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9–10, recall the claim-evidence-counterargument scaffold
  (D04.S3.06.01) and Toulmin's claim/data/warrant (S-236). Today we carry that
  scaffold into *spoken* persuasion, home language first.
- Prepare one worked example: a claim with a strong objection and an honest answer.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a persuasive speech
is built from claim, reasons, evidence, and — its mark of honesty — the strongest
counterargument, stated at full strength and answered fairly.** The scaffold follows
the argument model learners met in earlier grades (claim/reasons/evidence/counterargument,
Toulmin's claim/data/warrant/rebuttal, S-236), now aimed at *spoken* delivery. The
**steelman** principle — restate the other side's best version before answering — is
the ethical heart: it is the difference between persuading and manipulating.

Model the scaffold once, then let learners build their own (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens: a strong speech
*invites* the hardest objection rather than dodging it. The **ethics** lens:
persuasion that refuses to name the other side is pressure, not argument; fairness to
the counterargument is an ethical commitment, not a tactic. The **egalitarian** lens:
whose objections get to be heard is a question of power — a good speech makes room for
the quiet objection as well as the loud one. The **global** lens: the *forms* of
persuasive speech differ across cultures (a griot's performance, a formal whaikōrero,
a debate, a family council), but the demand to answer the other side fairly is shared.
Encourage learners to draft in their home language first — fluency of thought comes
first, translation later (Lesson 12). Preview: Lesson 4 turns the scaffold into a
delivery.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9–10: what made an argument *complete*? (A claim,
   reasons, evidence, and a response to the counterargument.) Today you build that for
   the *spoken* word.
2. **Meet the scaffold (10 min).** Look at the speech scaffold sheet. The boxes:
   **claim** (what should be true or done), **reason 1**, **reason 2**, **evidence**
   (something real that backs a reason), **strongest counterargument** (the best
   honest objection), **honest response** (your answer to it). Watch a worked example
   built together on a shared topic.
3. **Learn the steelman (8 min).** A **straw man** is the other side made weak so it is
   easy to knock down — a dishonest trick. The **steelman** is the other side stated at
   its *strongest*, so your answer actually means something. Write in your journal one
   example of each, in your own words.
4. **Choose your topic and claim (10 min).** Pick something you genuinely want to
   persuade someone of — a change at school, at home, or in your community. Write your
   claim as one clear sentence in your **home language**. Add two real reasons.
5. **Anticipate the objection (12 min).** Write the strongest counterargument you can
   think of — the one that makes you hesitate. Then write your honest response. If you
   cannot answer it, that is information: your position needs work, and that is
   allowed. Revise the claim or reason if the objection exposed a real weakness.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a speech that names and answers the best objection is
   stronger, and more honest, than one that hides from it. Keep your scaffold — next
   time you turn it into a delivery, out loud, in front of people.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a starter claim on a shared, familiar topic and one reason
  already written; ask learners to add the counterargument and response.
- **Extension:** Label each box with its Toulmin term (claim, data/grounds, warrant,
  rebuttal) (S-236) and add a second counterargument with its own response.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state a claim, two reasons, evidence,
  and — most importantly — a counterargument that is a genuine steelman, not a straw
  man, with an honest response?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed speech scaffold in the home language,
  added to the portfolio as the "constructed argument" entry.

## Home connection

Tell an adult your claim and ask them for the *strongest* objection they can think of.
Do not argue yet — just write it down. That is your steelman for tomorrow.
