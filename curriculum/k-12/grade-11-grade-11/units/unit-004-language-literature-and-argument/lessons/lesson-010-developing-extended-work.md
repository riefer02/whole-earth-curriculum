# Lesson 10 — Developing the Extended Work: Structure, Voice, Revision

## Summary

Learners develop their seed idea into a full **draft** — giving it structure and a
voice — and then **revise** it, reading aloud to find where the structure sags and
using a checklist to confirm the idea stays central from opening to landing. They
complete the sustained original work for the portfolio.

## Objectives

- Write extended, original work that develops a sustained idea or story — by carrying
  the seed through structure, voice, and revision to a completed work. _(D04.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

The first draft is you telling yourself the idea; the revision is you telling it to
someone else. A draft almost always has a sag — a part where even you, reading aloud,
lose interest, because the idea left the room and the sentences kept going without it.
Reading a draft back — aloud, in sign, or slowly to yourself — is the oldest and truest
editor: the channel you did not write with catches what the mind forgives.
To finish a long work is not to make it perfect but to make it *whole* — every part
earning its place in service of the one idea you planted.

## Materials

- Revision checklist (idea central / structure carries / voice is mine / each part earns its place / ending lands)
- Draft from Lesson 9 and home time

## Preparation

- Copy the revision checklist.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, recall the seed idea, the one question, and the first two
  moves. Today we grow them into a full draft and revise.
- Prepare a worked example: revise one sagging sentence from a shared draft.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an extended work is
finished not when it is long enough but when the idea is carried whole — structure and
voice serve the idea, and revision (especially reading aloud) is how the writer finds
where the idea dropped.** Structure and voice are craft skills: model them and give
guided practice (S-011). The read-aloud is a concrete, low-tech revision technique —
where the reader stumbles, the structure is weak. Feedback is "what serves the idea /
what to try next," never ranking (philosophy §8).

Model one revision, then let learners revise (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **intellectual** lens: coherence is the discipline — every
part must *earn* its place in the arc. The **critical-thinking** lens: revision is
self-critique made routine — the writer asks of their own sentences what they asked of
a text in Lessons 7–8. The **social-emotional** lens: sharing a draft is vulnerable;
read-alouds are in pairs or small circles, and "not yet" is information, not a verdict.
The **egalitarian** lens: every learner's voice is worth completing, and the work is
judged by whether it carries *its own* idea — not by whose idea is "better." Preview:
Lesson 11 adapts writing across registers — the same content re-clothed for a different
audience.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9: you planted a seed and wrote the first two moves.
   Today you grow it into a full draft and revise it whole.
2. **Meet structure and voice (10 min).** Learn the two craft levers: **structure** — the
   arc that carries the idea from opening to landing — and **voice** — the sound of *you*
   on the page. Watch a worked example: one sagging sentence, revised so it serves the
   idea again.
3. **Draft to the end (15 min).** Write your work through to an ending that lands where
   the seed pointed. Do not stop to polish — reach the end first. (If you need more time,
   carry it home; the work is yours.)
4. **Read aloud (10 min).** In pairs, read your draft aloud. Mark every place you stumble
   or lose interest. Ask your partner one question: "where did the idea leave the room?"
5. **Revise against the checklist (10 min).** Use the revision checklist: is the idea
   central throughout? Does the structure carry it? Is the voice mine? Does each part
   earn its place? Does the ending land where the seed pointed? Revise the weakest two
   spots now.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a work is finished when the idea is whole, not when it is
   long. Add the completed work (or its finished section, if it continues at home) to
   your portfolio. Next time: the same content in a different register.

## Differentiation

- **Access (non-speaking, deaf, hard-of-hearing, dyslexic, low-vision):** "Reading
  aloud" is one way to find a sag, not the only way. Read the draft silently and
  slowly, ask a partner or screen reader to read it back, or sign each paragraph and
  mark where the idea drops. Learners who write by voice may dictate the draft to a
  scribe or use speech-to-text. The revision check — does each part carry the seed
  idea? — is the same in every channel.
- **Support:** Revise for a single thing — does each part carry the idea — and cut or
  fix the one place it does not.
- **Extension:** Give one peer's draft structured feedback in the form "what serves the
  idea / one thing to try," and then apply the same lens to your own.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner carry the seed idea through a complete
  structure with a consistent voice, and revise at least one sag using the checklist?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed extended original work, added to the
  portfolio as the "extended original work" entry (the unit's central writing artifact).

## Home connection

Read your finished work aloud to someone who cares about you. Ask them only: *what did
you remember most?* — and see if it is the idea you meant to plant.
