# Lesson 7 — An Author's Place: Culture and History Shape a Text

## Summary

Learners analyze how an author's **cultural and historical position** — their time,
place, language, and relation to power — shapes the text they produce, using a
four-question probe. They read brief examples of authors who wrote across languages
and under pressure (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Gloria Anzaldúa, and others) and begin the
unit's author-position analysis.

## Objectives

- Analyze how an author's cultural and historical position shapes a text — by asking
  who wrote it, when and where, in what language, and under what power. _(D04.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

The same story told by two different people is two different stories. A river that a
farmer writes about as water for the fields, an engineer writes about as a flow to be
measured, and a poet writes about as a voice in the dark. Who you are — your place,
your time, your language, your relation to power — is not a stain on your writing; it
is the very lens through which the writing sees. To read deeply is to ask not only
*what does this say* but *who is saying it, from where, and to whom* — and to notice
that every author, including you, writes from somewhere.

## Materials

- Author-position sheet (who / when and where / in what language and for whom / how it shows)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Select one or two short author excerpts (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Gloria Anzaldúa, or
  another author whose position is clear) for the worked example.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5–6, recall how *language* shapes meaning. Today we ask how
  the *author's whole situation* shapes the text.
- Prepare the four-question worked example on one author.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a text is written
by a situated person — their time, place, language, and relation to power shape the
text, and a careful reader asks who wrote this, from where, in what language, and for
whom.** Two canonical examples carry this: Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, who argued that being
made to write in the colonizer's language distanced him from his home world and chose
to write in Gikuyu (S-424); and Gloria Anzaldúa, who lived and wrote *between*
languages — Chicano Spanish, English, and more — insisting her languages are her
identity, not a deficit (S-425). Present both as *their lived arguments*, held as
testimony to be weighed, never as a single verdict all learners must adopt (inquiry
over creed, philosophy §11). A learner who writes comfortably in one language is not
"wrong"; they are invited to *notice* their own position, whatever it is.

Model the four questions once, then let learners apply them (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **global** lens: choose authors from beyond
one region — a Persian poet, a West African epic's griot-keeper (S-269), a Kenyan
novelist, a Chicana essayist — so "position" is worldwide. The **egalitarian** lens:
whose position gets to be "literature" and whose is "folklore" is a question of power,
not merit. The **ethics** lens: reading an author's position faithfully means not
reducing a person to their situation — position *shapes*, it does not *determine*. The
**critical-thinking** lens: position is one lens among several; the text still speaks
beyond it. Preview: Lesson 8 reads the text *against* other perspectives — including
perspectives the author could not or did not include.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6: language shapes meaning. Today you step back:
   the *author's whole situation* shapes the text — their place, time, language, power.
2. **Meet the four questions (10 min).** Look at the author-position sheet. The four
   questions: **who wrote this?** **when and where?** **in what language, and for whom?**
   **how does that position show in the text?** Watch a worked example on one author —
   mark the clues of their position in a short excerpt.
3. **Read two positions (15 min).** Read brief excerpts (or summaries) of two authors
   who wrote across languages and under pressure. For each, fill the four questions.
   Ask: how did their *language* and their *relation to power* show up in the writing
   itself — not just in the topic, but in the choice of words, the mixing of languages,
   the audience they address?
4. **Name your own position (10 min).** Turn the probe on yourself: if you wrote a story
   or essay today, *who* would be writing it — from where, in what language or mix of
   languages, for whom, and under what pressures? Write three sentences.
5. **Connect position to the text (10 min).** Choose one of the two authors and write
   one precise sentence: "Because ___ wrote in ___ under ___, the text does ___" —
   naming a *specific* feature (a word choice, a rhythm, a mixing of tongues) that shows
   the position.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: every author — including you — writes from somewhere.
   Add this page to your author-position analysis. Next time: read that text *against*
   other perspectives.

## Differentiation

- **Access (dyslexic, low-vision, blind):** Offer the author excerpts as audio (read
  aloud by the facilitator or a partner) or in large print, braille, or accessible
  digital text; learners may answer the four questions aloud, dictate them to a scribe,
  or type. The *analysis* of position — not reading speed — is the standard.
- **Support:** Provide the author's position already stated in one sentence; ask
  learners to find one place in the excerpt where it shows.
- **Extension:** Compare Ngũgĩ's choice to write in Gikuyu with Anzaldúa's deliberate
  mixing of languages, and argue what each choice says about their different positions.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name an author's time, place, language, and
  relation to power, and point to one specific feature of the text where that position
  shows?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The author-position sheet, added to the portfolio as
  the "author-position analysis" entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult to tell you the story of a song or poem that mattered in their youth —
then ask: *who wrote it, when, where, and in what language — and can you hear that in
the words?*

## Resources

  *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) (S-424).
- On living and writing between languages: Gloria Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera*
  (1987), esp. "How to Tame a Wild Tongue" (S-425).
- On the griot as the keeper of a people's history: Niane, *Sundiata: An Epic of Old
