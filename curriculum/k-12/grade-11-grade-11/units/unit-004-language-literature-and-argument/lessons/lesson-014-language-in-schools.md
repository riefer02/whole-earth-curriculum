# Lesson 14 — Language in Schools: Equity and Inclusion

## Summary

Learners evaluate **school-language policies** — the language of instruction, tests,
and the playground — for **equity and inclusion**, asking whose home language matches
the school and whose does not. They distinguish subtractive from additive bilingualism
and draft what a fairer policy would do, completing the unit's language-policy
evaluation.

## Objectives

- Evaluate language policies — including language in schools — for their effects on
  equity and inclusion, and propose a fairer alternative. _(D04.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Picture sitting in a classroom where every lesson, every test, every instruction is in
a language you barely know. You are not less able — but you are *locked out* of showing
it. Now picture the reverse: the first years in your home language, the other languages
added *alongside* it, your home tongue treated as a foundation instead of a problem.
School language is the single policy that most decides who gets to learn at all. About
40 percent of people worldwide lack education in a language they understand (S-019) —
which means the classroom, for many, is a locked door with a lesson behind it.

## Materials

- School-language evaluation sheet (what language school is in / whose home language matches / what happens to each child / what a fairer policy would do)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the school-language evaluation sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 13, recall the five-question policy probe. Today we point it
  at the place learners know best — school.
- Prepare one worked example: a familiar school-language policy, evaluated.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **school-language
policy — the medium of instruction, assessment, and daily life — most directly decides
equity and inclusion, and a fair policy is *additive* (home language first, other
languages added) rather than *subtractive* (home language replaced and lost).** This is
the evaluative standard made personal: every learner has felt or witnessed what it
means for school to speak a language that is not yours. Hold the additive-over-
subtractive framing as the unit's *value* (multilingualism as wealth), while being
honest that *evidence* shows mother-tongue-based instruction supports learning —
anchored in the 40% fact (S-019) — and that the choice is often constrained by real
resources and histories. Keep both in view; do not pretend the fair policy is always
easy to enact.

Model the evaluation once, then let learners evaluate and propose (worked example →
guided practice → independent work; S-011). The **egalitarian** lens is the spine:
whose home language matches the blackboard, and whose is treated as an obstacle. The
**ethics** lens: a child punished or shamed for their home language is a wrong, not a
pedagogy — name it plainly. The **global** lens: school-language policy varies
worldwide — mother-tongue-first programs, colonial-language-only systems, bilingual
immersion — and none is the world's default. The **critical-thinking** lens: test the
*assumed value* ("children need one common language") against its *effects* on the
children it excludes. The **social-emotional** lens: this topic is close to the bone
for learners who have been told their language is "wrong"; hold the room gently, and
let no one be shamed here — including the monolingual learner, whose single language is
also to be honored, not mocked. Preview: Lesson 15 turns from *policy* to *practice* —
bridging between speakers right now.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 13: no language policy is neutral. Today you evaluate
   the policy closest to you — language in school.
2. **Meet the evaluation (10 min).** Look at the school-language evaluation sheet. The
   questions: **what language is school in?** **whose home language matches it, and
   whose does not?** **what happens to each child?** **what would a fairer policy do?**
   Watch a worked example evaluated together.
3. **Learn subtractive vs. additive (8 min).** A **subtractive** policy replaces a
   child's home language; an **additive** one builds on it and adds others. Write in
   your journal which your own schooling was closest to — and what that meant.
4. **Evaluate a real case (15 min).** Take your own school or a school system you know
   (or one you look up). Run the evaluation. Name one child the policy serves well and
   one it serves poorly.
5. **Propose a fairer policy (10 min).** Draft, in three to five sentences, a school
   language policy that is additive: home language first, other languages added
   alongside. Name one realistic obstacle and one way to meet it.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: school language is the policy that decides who learns at
   all — and it can be made fairer. Add this page to your language-policy evaluation.
   Next time: you stop analyzing policy and start *bridging* — interpreting between two
   speakers, live.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Evaluate a single given policy and answer only "whose home language
  matches, and whose does not?"
- **Extension:** Design the full additive policy — years, subjects, testing — and
  anticipate two objections to it, answering each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner evaluate a school-language policy for
  who it includes and excludes, and propose an additive alternative with at least one
  realistic obstacle named?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The school-language evaluation (with the proposed
  policy), added to the portfolio as the "language-in-schools" entry, completing the
  language-policy evaluation.

## Home connection

Ask an adult what language *their* school was in, and whether it was their home
language. Then ask: did that help or hold them back — and what would they have changed?
