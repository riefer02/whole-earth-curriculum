# Lesson 16 — The Live Bridge: Interpreting in a Real Setting

## Summary

Learners complete the unit with a **live bridge**: interpreting between two speakers of
different languages in a real setting, and recording what the bridge made possible.
They reflect on the whole unit and gather their portfolio artifacts — closing the
spiral that began with listening across a voice and ends with carrying meaning across
two.

## Objectives

- Bridge between speakers of different languages in a real setting, interpreting main
  ideas with accuracy and care — and reflect on what the bridge made possible.
  _(D04.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

All unit long you have crossed borders with your ears, your eyes, and your pen: you
listened through an unfamiliar voice, read a text against its missing voice, carried
your own words into a second language, and weighed the policies that decide whose
language counts. Today you do the thing itself: two people who could not reach each
other, and you — the bridge. Not because you are fluent in everything, but because you
know to listen for the main idea, render it faithfully, check back, and admit when you
are unsure. That is enough to let two humans meet.

## Materials

- Live-bridge record (setting / speakers and languages / main ideas carried / one checking-back moment / what the bridge made possible)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy the live-bridge record.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 15, recall the four moves — listen for the main idea, render
  it faithfully, check back, admit when unsure. Today you perform them live.
- Arrange a real setting and two willing speakers where possible, or use two speakers
  in the room with different languages or registers.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). This is the unit's capstone performance:
**a live bridge between two speakers of different languages in a real setting, with a
written reflection on what the bridge made possible.** The standard is *accuracy and
care*, not fluency (S-237): a learner who checks back and admits uncertainty has met
it fully. Where two real languages are not available, two *registers* or *ways of
speaking* serve as a faithful low-threshold setting (egalitarian lens). The live bridge
is also the unit's closing **retrieval and spacing** payoff — every earlier skill
returns here: listening across difference (Lessons 1–2), carrying meaning (5–6),
reading position (7–8), writing across languages (11–12), weighing policy (13–14),
bridging (15) (S-039).

Run the bridges in trios, then reflect (performance → reflection; S-011). The **ethics**
lens: the bridge must serve both speakers equally — never "winning" for one side,
never adding or dropping meaning. The **egalitarian** lens: every language in the room
is a full language, and the bridge honors both, ranking neither. The **global** lens:
this is a worldwide, ancient human act — in markets, clinics, courts, and families
everywhere, people carry meaning across. The **social-emotional** lens: celebrate what
the bridge made possible — connection — and name the care it took. The **community**
lens: the bridge is an act of mutual aid; reflect on how it strengthens the group, not
just the interpreter. Close by gathering the portfolio and honoring multilingualism as
wealth, home language first.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 15: the four moves — listen for the main idea,
   render it faithfully, check back, admit when unsure. Today you perform them live.
2. **Set the scene (8 min).** Form a trio (or a real setting with two speakers who need
   each other). Agree on the exchange: one speaker has something to say or ask; the
   other needs to understand and reply. You are the bridge.
3. **Perform the live bridge (20 min).** Interpret the exchange. Use all four moves.
   Carry the **main ideas**, not every word. When unsure, say so and check back.
   Let the exchange reach a real understanding — or honestly name where it could not.
4. **Record it (10 min).** Fill the live-bridge record: the **setting**, the **speakers
   and their languages**, the **main ideas** you carried, one **checking-back** moment,
   and **what the bridge made possible**.
5. **Reflect on the unit (8 min).** In your journal, write two or three sentences: what
   changed in how you listen, read, write, or bridge, from Lesson 1 to today? Which
   skill will you carry forward?
6. **Close (5 min).** Gather your portfolio artifacts — the listening log, the speech
   scaffold and delivery, the translation comparison, the author-position analysis, the
   extended work, the adaptations, the policy evaluations, and this live-bridge record.
   Remember: home language first, multilingualism as wealth — and you, the bridge.

## Differentiation

- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** The live bridge may be signed,
  written (passing notes), or spoken; fidelity and checking back are the standard in
  every channel. A non-speaking learner bridges by writing, typing (AAC), or signing; a
  deaf learner bridges between a signer and a speaker, or between two signed languages.
- **Support:** Bridge a short, simple exchange (one question and one answer) and record
  one checking-back moment.
- **Extension:** Bridge a fuller exchange with a moment of real ambiguity, and write
  what you would do differently next time — including drawing on more than one of your
  own languages (S-237).

## Assessment

- **Performance + self/peer:** Can the learner bridge a real exchange, carrying main
  ideas with fidelity, checking back, and admitting uncertainty — and reflect on what
  the bridge made possible?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The live-bridge record, added to the portfolio as the
  "live interpreting record" — the unit's closing multilingual artifact.

## Home connection

Offer to be the bridge one more time this week — in a shop, a message, a family call —
even for one sentence. Then tell someone what it felt like to let two people meet.
