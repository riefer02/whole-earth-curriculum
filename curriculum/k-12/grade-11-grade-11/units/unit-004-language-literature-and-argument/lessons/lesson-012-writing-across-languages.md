# Lesson 12 — Writing Across Languages: Translating Your Own Work

## Summary

Learners carry their own writing across a language border — translating a passage of
their extended work or composing directly in a second language — and record what is
**gained, lost, and changed**. They treat the second language not as a test but as a
second home for the idea, honoring the unit's commitment: home language first,
multilingualism as wealth.

## Objectives

- Adapt writing across languages and registers, including translation or writing in a
  second language — by carrying one's own words across a language border with care.
  _(D04.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

You have already felt this: a thing you can say perfectly in your home language comes
out differently in the language you are still learning — flatter, or sharper, or
somehow more true, because a new language lets you see your own thought from the
outside. Writing across languages is not a test you pass or fail; it is a second home
you build for an idea. The goal is not to sound "native" but to be *understood* — and
to learn, by the crossing, what your own words actually meant.

## Materials

- Across-languages sheet (original / rendering — gained, lost, changed)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-column across-languages sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, recall that translation gains, loses, and changes, and
  that tools give gist while humans give judgment. Today *you* are the translator.
- Prepare a worked example: one short passage, self-translated.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **adapting one's own
writing across languages is an act of authorship, not a test — the learner translates
a passage or composes in a second language, then reflects on gain and loss, holding
multilingualism as wealth.** Not every learner will be fluent in a second language;
the standard says "where possible" — a learner with one language translates *into a
register they know less* or *describes* what would change, and that is genuine
adaptation too. Never rank learners by how many languages they have (egalitarian lens).
Home language first: the original is written in the learner's strongest language
before any crossing (philosophy; unit overview).

Model once, then let learners cross (worked example → guided practice → independent
work; S-011). The **global** lens: self-translation is a living practice worldwide —
writers who publish in more than one language (Ngũgĩ translating himself between Gikuyu
and English, S-424) and communities that carry stories across tongues every day. The
**egalitarian** lens: a "second language" is not worth less than a first, and an accent
or an imperfect rendering is not a failure. The **intellectual** lens: the crossing is
a *lens* — seeing your own sentence in another grammar shows you what it assumed. The
**social-emotional** lens: writing in a second language feels exposed; normalize
"not yet," and praise the attempt as the standard itself. The **technology** lens:
tools may assist, but the learner's judgment is final — a dictionary helps, a machine
does not decide. Preview: Lesson 13 turns from individual writing to *policy* — whose
language is official, and what that does to people.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6: translation gains, loses, and changes — and
   the human, not the tool, makes the final call. Today *you* are the translator of
   *your own* words.
2. **Meet self-translation (10 min).** Look at the across-languages sheet: **original**
   (home language) and **rendering** (second language or translation), with a row for
   **gained, lost, changed**. Watch a worked example: one short passage carried across,
   marked for what changed.
3. **Choose your passage (5 min).** Pick a short passage from your extended work
   (Lessons 9–10), or a few sentences you care about, written in your **home language**.
   Write it in the left column.
4. **Carry it across (20 min).** Write the rendering in the right column — translate it
   into a second language you know, or compose the same idea directly in that language.
   Use a dictionary or tool as an aid, but the final words are yours. If you have one
   language, adapt it into a register you know less well and note what that crossing
   reveals.
5. **Record gain and loss (8 min).** In the bottom row, write one thing you **gained**
   (a meaning that became clearer, a sound that fit better), one thing you **lost** (a
   word with no equal, a rhythm that broke), and one thing that **changed**.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the second language is a second home for the idea, not
   a test. Add this page to your adaptation artifact. Next time: whose language gets to
   be official — and what that does to people.

## Differentiation

- **Access (non-speaking, deaf, hard-of-hearing, dyslexic, low-vision):** The
  across-languages sheet is written work — a learner may compose or translate by
  dictation (to a scribe or speech-to-text), by signing (with a written gloss), or in
  accessible digital text. The crossing — not the writing hand — is the standard.
- **Support:** Translate a single sentence with a dictionary, then name one thing that
  changed.
- **Extension:** Write two renderings of the same line and argue which keeps the
  meaning best and which keeps the music best — then combine them if you can.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner carry a short passage of their own
  writing across a language (or into an unfamiliar register) and name one thing gained,
  one lost, and one changed?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The across-languages sheet, added to the portfolio as
  the "across-language adaptation" entry.

## Home connection

Show your rendering to someone who knows the second language. Ask them only: *does it
say what I meant?* Then tell them what you think got lost — and what you found.
