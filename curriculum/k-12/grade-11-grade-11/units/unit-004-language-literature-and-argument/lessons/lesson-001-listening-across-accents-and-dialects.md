# Lesson 1 — Listening Across Accents and Dialects

## Summary

Learners meet the unit's first skill: **listening across differences of accent,
dialect, and viewpoint** — hearing the *main idea* a speaker is carrying rather than
grading the *voice* that carries it. They learn that accents and dialects are
complete, rule-governed systems, not "broken" versions of anything (S-167), name the
reflex of **linguistic prejudice**, and begin the unit's cross-accent listening log.

## Objectives

- Listen across differences — including accents, dialects, and viewpoints — and
  identify assumptions in what is said, beginning here with *hearing the message
  through an unfamiliar voice*. _(D04.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of a time you understood someone perfectly even though they spoke differently
from you — a relative from another region, a shopkeeper with another accent, a
friend switching to a second language, a voice on a crackly call. You did the work of
listening *through* the difference to reach the meaning. Now think of a time you or
someone else stopped listening the moment the accent sounded "wrong" — and how much
was missed. Every way of speaking is a complete system with its own rules and music
(S-167); the listener's real skill is to reach for the idea being shared, not to rank
the voice. That skill is the doorway to this whole unit.

## Materials

- Listening record sheet (speaker / way of speaking / main idea / what I almost misjudged)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four-column listening record sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9–10, recall that a speaker's word choice, tone, and delivery
  shape meaning (D04.S1.08.01) and that dialect and accent are natural variation, not
  error (D04.S4.07.02). Today we practice *listening across* that variation.
- Prepare one worked example: a short, familiar message said in two different ways of
  speaking.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **accent and dialect
are natural, complete, rule-governed variation — no way of speaking is "incorrect" —
and the skilled listener reaches for the main idea instead of grading the voice**
(S-167). Every language and dialect is a full system of sounds and grammar; the
varieties linguists call "non-standard" are no less systematic than the "standard"
ones — the standard is just the variety that happened to be written down and given
prestige. Linguistic prejudice (judging a speaker's intelligence or worth by their
accent) is a real, documented form of exclusion, not a neutral preference. Hold this
as *evidence about language* and *value about fairness* in the same breath, but keep
them distinct (philosophy §5).

Model the listening skill once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **global** lens: there are roughly 7,000
living languages and countless dialects (S-426) — plus signed languages, which are
complete languages with their own grammars, not "gestured" versions of speech — so
"one accent for everyone" is not how the world is. The **egalitarian** lens: whose accent is called "clear" and whose
is called "thick" is a question of power and history, not of clarity itself — the
same sounds are praised in one mouth and mocked in another. The **ethics** lens: to
listen fairly is to treat the speaker as a full person, not a specimen of a voice.
The **social-emotional** lens: noticing the reflex to tune out or mock — in *yourself*
— is self-awareness; invite, never force, sharing about a time you felt judged for
how you speak.

**Access.** "Listening" names *attention*, not the ear. Non-hearing learners run the
same skill through the eyes — reading two written renditions of one message, or
watching two signers with different signing styles, and catching the main idea from
the page or the hands. Provide any audio clip with a transcript or captions, and offer
record sheets in large print and high contrast. This principle — every skill open to
every mode of attending — carries through the whole unit, from listening across voices
to bridging between speakers.

Preview: Lesson 2 turns to the next layer — *assumptions* carried
inside what is said.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** Think back: how does a speaker's tone and delivery shape what
   you hear (D04.S1.08.01)? Today you train the listening side — hearing the *idea*
   through a voice that is not like yours.
2. **Meet the skill (10 min).** Look at the listening record sheet. The four columns:
   **speaker**, **way of speaking**, **main idea**, **what I almost misjudged**. Watch
   a worked example: one message ("the water must be carried before noon") said twice —
   once in a familiar way, once in an unfamiliar accent or dialect. Notice: the *idea*
   is identical; only the voice changed. Name the reflex to call one "clear" and the
   other "thick."
3. **Learn the linguistics (10 min).** Every accent and dialect is a complete system
   with its own rules — not "broken" and not "incorrect" (S-167). So are signed
   languages and creoles: a sign language is a full language with its own grammar
   (not "gestured speech"), and a creole is a complete language, not a "broken" form
   of another. There is no "correct accent," only a variety that gained social
   prestige. Write in your
   journal: one way of speaking from your own life that people sometimes judge, and
   why the judgment is about status, not about the voice itself.
4. **Practice in a circle (15 min).** In groups of three or four, take turns telling
   a short, true story — and try it in a different accent, dialect, speed, or
   language you partly know. Each listener, before adding their own story, must
   restate the previous speaker's **main idea** in one sentence. Fill your record
   sheet as you go.
5. **Catch the reflex (10 min).** In the "what I almost misjudged" column, write one
   honest moment where you almost tuned out or graded a voice. Then write the main
   idea you would have lost.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the message and the voice are two different things.
   Today you began the unit's cross-accent listening log — keep it growing all unit
   long. Next time: the assumptions hiding *inside* the words.

## Differentiation

- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, low-vision, non-speaking):** Catch the main idea
  from *any* channel — a transcript, a caption, a written rendering, or a signed
  account — and restate it in writing, sign, typing (AAC), or by pointing. Two
  *written* or *signed* renditions of one message carry the same skill as two spoken
  accents.
- **Support:** Provide a short list of three messages read aloud two ways; ask only
  "what is the main idea?" and "which voice felt easier, and why might that be?"
- **Extension:** Find a recording of a speaker whose accent differs from yours, note
  their main idea, and identify one feature of their way of speaking (a sound, a
  rhythm, a word) that you can describe without judging it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner restate a speaker's main idea across an
  unfamiliar accent or dialect, and name one reflex of linguistic prejudice they
  caught in themselves?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The first page of the cross-accent listening record,
  added to the unit portfolio as the opener of the "listening across differences" log.

## Home connection

Ask an adult you trust: *have you ever been judged for the way you speak — and how did
it feel?* Listen without interrupting, then tell them one main idea you heard in their
story.
