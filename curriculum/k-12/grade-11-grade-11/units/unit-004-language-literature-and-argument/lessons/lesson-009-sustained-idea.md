# Lesson 9 — Extended Original Work: A Sustained Idea

## Summary

Learners begin an **extended, original work** — analytical, narrative, or creative — by
finding one seed idea worth sustaining, naming the single question it explores, and
planning its shape. They draft the work's first moves in their home language, setting
up a multi-day writing arc that continues in Lesson 10.

## Objectives

- Write extended, original work that develops a sustained idea or story — beginning by
  finding and committing to one idea worth carrying. _(D04.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

A long work is not a short one stretched out; it is a seed that keeps on giving. A
single sentence — a question that nags you, an image that will not leave, a character
who wants something — can become an essay, a story, a memoir, if you let it grow
instead of rushing it. The difference between writing that runs out of breath and
writing that sustains is whether the *idea* itself has enough life in it to keep
opening. Today you find that idea and plant it.

## Materials

- Sustained-work planner (seed idea / genre / the one question / the shape / first two moves)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the sustained-work planner.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9–10, recall writing for real purposes and audiences
  (D04.S3.09.02) and crafting prose with deliberate style (D04.S3.10.01). Today we
  write *long* — one idea sustained.
- Prepare one worked example: a seed idea that opens into a whole work.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **an extended work is
built on one idea or story worth sustaining — the writer finds the seed, names the
question it explores, and lets it grow across the work.** The genre is the learner's
choice: analytical (an essay developing a claim), narrative (a story), or creative (a
poem-sequence, a hybrid). Home language first — thought flows in the language the
learner dreams in; translation and second-language work come later (Lesson 12). This
lesson launches a *multi-day arc*: Lesson 9 plants, Lesson 10 develops, revises, and
completes, with home time in between — the spacing itself is a science-of-learning
feature (S-039).

Model the planner once, then let learners plan their own (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **intellectual** lens: the discipline of
naming "the one question" separates a sustained work from a pile of sentences. The
**social-emotional** lens: writing long is vulnerable — the idea may be personal; make
the room safe for half-formed seeds, and allow learners to choose fiction or analysis
to keep distance if they need it. The **ethics** lens: an idea is a responsibility — a
writer chooses what to sustain and thereby what to amplify; invite learners to notice
what their idea values. The **global** lens: sustained works take many shapes across
cultures — the griot's epic (S-269), the essay, the poem-cycle, the family chronicle —
none is the "real" form. Preview: Lesson 10 develops the structure and voice, and
revises.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9–10: you have written for real purposes and crafted
   sentences on purpose. Today you write *long* — one idea sustained across a whole
   work.
2. **Meet the planner (10 min).** Look at the sustained-work planner. Its parts: **seed
   idea** (one sentence), **genre** (analytical / narrative / creative), **the one
   question** it explores, **the shape** it will take, and the **first two moves**.
   Watch a worked example: a seed that opens into a whole work.
3. **Find your seed (12 min).** In your journal, list three ideas that keep returning to
   you — a question, an image, a character, a memory, an argument. Choose the one with
   the most life in it. Write it as one sentence in your **home language**.
4. **Name the question (8 min).** Every sustained work is an answer to a question it
   keeps exploring. Write the one question your work is asking. If you cannot, the seed
   is not ready — pick another. Test it aloud in one breath.
5. **Plan the shape (10 min).** Decide the genre and sketch the work's arc: where it
   begins, where it turns, where it lands. Write the **first two moves** — the opening
   sentence and the move that follows it. (Do not write the whole thing yet.)
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a sustained work grows from one idea, over time. Keep
   the planner — between now and Lesson 10, let the seed grow in your mind. Next time:
   structure, voice, and revision.

## Differentiation

- **Access (non-speaking, deaf, hard-of-hearing, dyslexic, low-vision):** The "one
  breath" test may be done in sign, in writing, or in thought — the check is "is this
  one idea, not several." Learners who write by voice may dictate the planner to a
  scribe or use speech-to-text; the planner's *idea*, not the writing hand, is assessed.
- **Support:** Provide three ready seed ideas to choose from, and a starter question
  for each.
- **Extension:** State your idea as a thesis, then write the counter-thesis you will
  hold in tension across the work — the argument your essay or story will wrestle with.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner state one seed idea in a sentence, name the
  single question it explores, and sketch its shape with a first move?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The sustained-work planner, added to the portfolio as
  the "extended original work — planning" entry.

## Home connection

Before Lesson 10, let your seed grow: as you go about your day, notice one detail —
a sound, a phrase, a face — and jot it down. Your work is already rooting.
