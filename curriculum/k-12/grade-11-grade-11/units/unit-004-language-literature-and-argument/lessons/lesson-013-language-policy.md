# Lesson 13 — Language Policy: Whose Language Is Official?

## Summary

Learners evaluate **language policies** — official languages, colonial legacies, and
mother-tongue provisions — for their effects on **equity and inclusion**, using a
five-question probe. They learn that no language policy is neutral: every choice about
"which language counts" includes some people and excludes others.

## Objectives

- Evaluate language policies, such as official languages or language in schools, for
  their effects on equity and inclusion — beginning with *which language is official*.
  _(D04.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of every place a "right" language is assumed: the form you must fill in a
language that is not yours, the court that hears you only through an interpreter, the
sign you cannot read, the meeting you cannot follow. Now imagine the opposite: a
country where your language is the one on the signs and the forms and the laws. Which
language is "official" is not a matter of grammar — it is a decision about who gets to
participate fully and who must struggle to be heard. A language policy is a fairness
policy wearing a grammar's clothes.

## Materials

- Policy-evaluation sheet (what it says / who benefits / who is excluded / what value it assumes / is it fair)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the five-question policy-evaluation sheet.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, recall how language and power interact — whose language is
  used in law, medicine, and school (D04.S4.10.01). Today we evaluate the *policies*
  that decide.
- Prepare one worked example: a real or familiar language policy, run through the five
  questions.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a language policy —
which language is official, used in government, law, or public life — is never neutral;
it distributes inclusion and exclusion, so it must be evaluated for equity.** The
standard is evaluative: learners weigh effects, not memorize a verdict. Distinguish
*evidence* (what a policy actually says and does) from *values* (whether inclusion is
the aim — the unit holds it as a value, philosophy §5). Examples span the globe: colonial
languages kept as the language of government after independence (Ngũgĩ's account of
English vs. Gikuyu, S-424); official multilingual states; and the worldwide fact that
about 40 percent of people lack education in a language they speak or understand
(S-019). Present these as cases to weigh, not lessons to absorb.

Model the five questions once, then let learners evaluate (worked example → guided
practice → independent work; S-011). The **egalitarian** lens is the spine: ask *who
benefits and who is excluded* — the same sound, praised in one policy and erased in
another, is a question of power. The **ethics** lens: fairness asks whether every
person can be heard and understood in the places that decide their life. The **global**
lens: policies differ enormously — some states recognize many languages, others one —
and no single model is the world's. The **critical-thinking** lens: read a policy's
*assumed value* ("one common language unites us" vs. "many languages are wealth") and
test it against the effects. Preview: Lesson 14 zooms into the policy that touches
learners most directly — language in schools.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10: language and power interact — whose language is
   used in law, medicine, and school. Today you evaluate the *policies* that decide.
2. **Meet the five questions (10 min).** Look at the policy-evaluation sheet. The five
   questions: **what does the policy say?** **who benefits?** **who is excluded?**
   **what value does it assume?** **is it fair?** Watch a worked example: one real or
   familiar policy run through all five.
3. **Learn the scale (8 min).** Learn the global fact: about 40 percent of people lack
   education in a language they speak or understand (S-019). Write what that single
   number means for who can learn and who cannot.
4. **Evaluate a policy (15 min).** Choose a policy — a colonial language kept as the
   language of government, an official-language law, or a many-languages policy. Run
   the five questions. Name one group the policy includes and one it excludes.
5. **Name the assumed value (8 min).** Every policy stands on a value: "one language
   unites" or "many languages are wealth." Write which value your chosen policy assumes,
   and whether its effects match that value.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a language policy is a fairness policy. Add this page to
   your language-policy evaluation. Next time: language in schools — the policy that
   touches you most.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a single policy already described; ask only "who benefits?" and
  "who is excluded?"
- **Extension:** Compare a one-language policy with a many-languages policy and argue
  which better serves equity and inclusion — and what each trades away.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name what a language policy says, who it
  includes, who it excludes, and the value it assumes — and give a reasoned judgment on
  its fairness?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The policy-evaluation sheet, added to the portfolio as
  the "language-policy evaluation" entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult: *which language do the official signs, forms, and meetings use here — and
who has trouble with them?* Then decide together whether that seems fair.
