# Lesson 15 — Bridging Between Speakers: Interpreting with Care

## Summary

Learners practice **interpreting** between two speakers of different languages using a
four-move protocol — listen for the main idea, render it faithfully, check back, and
admit when unsure. They learn that interpretation is *carried meaning*, not
word-for-word exchange, and they prepare for a live bridge in Lesson 16.

## Objectives

- Bridge between speakers of different languages in a real setting, interpreting main
  ideas with accuracy and care — beginning with the protocol of faithful rendering.
  _(D04.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

Two people who do not share a language can still need each other badly: a patient and
a nurse, a buyer and a seller, a neighbor and a neighbor, a child and a grandparent
whose languages drifted apart. The person in the middle is not a dictionary — a
dictionary has no loyalty to meaning. The interpreter's job is to carry what the
speaker *means* across, faithfully, and to say honestly when the meaning will not
cross. Done well, it is one of the most quietly powerful things a human being can do
for another.

## Materials

- Interpreting protocol card (listen for the main idea / render it faithfully / check back / admit when unsure)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the four-move protocol card.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 5–6, recall that translation gains, loses, and changes, and
  that the human — not the tool — carries meaning with judgment. Today we interpret
  *live, between people*.
- Prepare a worked example: one short exchange, interpreted with a checking-back move.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **interpretation is
carrying meaning, not words — the interpreter listens for the main idea, renders it
faithfully, checks back with the speaker, and admits when unsure; accuracy and care,
not fluency, are the standard.** Interpretation is a real skill of **plurilingual
competence** — drawing on more than one language to mediate between people (S-237). A
learner who is not fluent in two languages can still practice the protocol by
interpreting between two *registers* or *ways of speaking* (child ↔ adult, formal ↔
informal), which is genuine bridging; the standard says "bridge between speakers of
different languages," and register-bridging is a faithful, low-threshold version of it
(egalitarian lens — never gate the skill behind fluency).

Model the four moves once, then let trios practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **ethics** lens is the spine: the interpreter's first
duty is to the *meaning* and the *speaker* — never to add, drop, or "improve" what was
said; admitting uncertainty is honesty, not failure. The **egalitarian** lens: a good
bridge treats both speakers as full persons, never as the "fluent" one and the "other"
one. The **global** lens: interpretation is ancient and worldwide — trade, diplomacy,
families, and courts have always needed people who carry meaning across. The
**social-emotional** lens: being interpreted can feel vulnerable; the interpreter
holds both speakers with care and patience. The **critical-thinking** lens: the
interpreter *decides* what is the main idea — that is a judgment call, and "check back"
is how it stays honest. Preview: Lesson 16 is the live bridge — a real setting, a real
exchange, recorded for the portfolio.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6: translation carries meaning with judgment, and
   the human is the carrier. Today you interpret *live, between two people*.
2. **Meet the four moves (10 min).** Look at the interpreting protocol card. The moves:
   **listen for the main idea** (not every word), **render it faithfully** (say what
   they meant), **check back** ("did I carry that right?"), **admit when unsure**
   ("I am not certain of this word — may I ask?"). Watch a worked example.
3. **Learn what interpretation is not (8 min).** It is not word-for-word swapping — that
   produces nonsense. It is carried meaning (S-237). Write in your journal the
   difference in your own words, with one example.
4. **Practice in trios (20 min).** In groups of three: a **speaker** (one language or
   register), a **listener** (who does not share it), and an **interpreter** (who
   bridges). Rotate roles. The interpreter must use all four moves at least once.
5. **Notice the hard moment (8 min).** Write one moment where you had to *check back* or
   *admit you were unsure* — and what happened when you did. That moment is the skill,
   not a mistake.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: the interpreter carries meaning, not words — with
   accuracy and care, not fluency. Add this page to your interpreting record. Next
   time: the live bridge, in a real setting.

## Differentiation

- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** Interpreting is not only spoken. A
  learner may interpret between two *written* texts, between a signer and a speaker, or
  between two registers in writing. "Listen for the main idea" means *attend* — watch
  the signer's hands and face, or read the line — and carry the meaning across in the
  channel the listener uses.
- **Support:** Interpret a single short sentence between two registers, using "check
  back" once.
- **Extension:** Interpret a longer exchange and manage one moment of real uncertainty
  aloud, then reflect on what "checking back" protected.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner render a speaker's main idea across a
  language or register, check back, and admit uncertainty when it arises?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The protocol card with a note of one practiced
  exchange, added to the portfolio as the "interpreting record" opener.

## Home connection

Find a moment at home where two people are not quite understanding each other — and
quietly carry the main idea across once, then ask both if you got it right.
