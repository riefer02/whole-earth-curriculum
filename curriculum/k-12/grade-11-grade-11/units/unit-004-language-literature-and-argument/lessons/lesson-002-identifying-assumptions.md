# Lesson 2 — Identifying Assumptions in What Is Said

## Summary

Learners move from *hearing through a voice* to *hearing beneath the words*: they learn
to identify **assumptions** — the unstated beliefs a spoken claim stands on — using a
three-part probe. They apply the probe to real claims from their week, across
different viewpoints, and add an assumption log to the unit portfolio.

## Objectives

- Listen across differences and identify assumptions in what is said — by naming the
  unstated beliefs a claim rests on, whatever the voice that speaks it. _(D04.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

When someone says "you're old enough to know better," a whole shelf of beliefs is
already assumed: that age brings duty, that you *did* know, that knowing is enough to
change behavior. None of it is spoken — it is carried silently under the words. Every
claim you hear, from a parent's rule to a leader's promise to an ad's slogan, stands
on assumptions like a house stands on its foundation. Learning to see the foundation
— before you agree or disagree — is what keeps you from being moved by words whose
support you never checked.

## Materials

- Assumption probe sheet (what is said / what must be true / what a skeptic questions)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-part probe sheet.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, recall that the message and the voice are different
  things. Today we look beneath the message for the beliefs it stands on.
- Prepare one worked example: a familiar claim with an obvious unstated assumption.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a spoken claim
carries unstated assumptions — premises the speaker takes for granted — and the
critical listener names them before agreeing or disagreeing.** Assumptions are not
automatically wrong; they are simply *unstated*. Some are fair and shared; some are
dubious and, once named, collapse. The skill is to surface them so they can be judged
on their merits (critical-thinking lens). This is the listening half of the standard:
identifying assumptions across accents, dialects, and viewpoints — not just in the
voices that sound like ours.

Model the probe once, then let learners practice (worked example → guided practice →
independent work; S-011). The **critical-thinking** lens is the spine: every claim has
a "what must already be true?" layer. The **egalitarian** lens: assumptions often
encode who is assumed to be normal, capable, or included — "everyone has a phone,"
"everyone celebrates this holiday," "a family means a mother and father." Naming those
assumptions is how exclusion becomes visible. The **ethics** lens: fair disagreement
requires naming the assumption you reject, not attacking the person who holds it. The
**global** lens: what is "obviously true" in one culture or language is a visible
assumption in another — probe across viewpoints, not only within your own. The
**social-emotional** lens: catching assumptions in yourself can feel exposing; keep the
tone gentle and treat "I didn't realize I assumed that" as a discovery, not a fault.
Preview: Lesson 3 builds the argument you will *speak* — and naming your own
assumptions is the first step to defending them.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1: the message and the voice are different. Today
   you dig under the message itself — to the beliefs it never says out loud.
2. **Meet the probe (10 min).** Look at the assumption probe sheet. Three questions:
   **what is said aloud?** **what must already be true for this to make sense?**
   **what would a listener who disagrees question first?** Watch a worked example:
   "You're old enough to know better" — what must be true? (Age brings duty; you knew;
   knowing is enough.) Name each assumption.
3. **Probe real claims (15 min).** Write three claims you actually heard this week —
   from family, a post, a speech, a song, an ad. For each, run the three questions.
   Underline the assumption that carries the most weight.
4. **Sort assumptions (10 min).** Mark each assumption as **fair and shared** (most
   people would accept it), **debatable** (reasonable people differ), or **hidden
   exclusion** (it quietly assumes one kind of person is "normal"). Write one of each
   kind.
5. **Probe across viewpoints (10 min).** Take one claim and imagine it spoken by two
   very different people — a grandparent and a younger sibling, a city neighbor and a
   rural neighbor. Does the same claim carry different assumptions in different
   mouths? Write what changes.
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: most of an argument lives under the surface, and you
   can see it. Add this page to your assumption log. Next time: you build the speech —
   and you will *state* your assumptions on purpose, not leave them hidden.

## Differentiation

- **Access (deaf, hard-of-hearing, non-speaking):** The probe runs entirely on *written*
  claims — a sentence on paper carries its assumptions just as fully as one spoken
  aloud; read it, or have it read back, and name the assumption in writing, sign, or
  typing (AAC).
- **Support:** Provide three prepared claims with the assumption already half-named
  ("This claim assumes that ____.") and ask learners to fill the blank.
- **Extension:** Take one public claim and write out the full chain of assumptions it
  depends on, then mark the weakest link and explain what would happen if it were
  removed.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name at least one unstated assumption behind a
  real claim, and say whether it is fair, debatable, or exclusionary?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The assumption probe sheet for one real claim, added
  to the portfolio as the "identifying assumptions" entry of the listening log.

## Home connection

Listen to one claim an adult makes this week — a rule, a plan, a worry — and gently
ask together: *what are we assuming when we say that?* See what naming it changes.
