# Lesson 5 — One Text, Many Translations

## Summary

Learners compare one short text across two or three translations and discover that
**translation is an art of choices**: every rendering gains, loses, and changes
something. They learn there is no single "perfect" translation, only decisions a
translator makes — and they begin the unit's translation comparison artifact.

## Objectives

- Compare texts across languages and translations, noting how translation shapes
  meaning — by seeing what each rendering gains, loses, and changes. _(D04.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of a proverb you know in your home language — one that people say with a smile
because the words themselves carry the meaning. Now try to say it in another language.
The words come out flat, or the joke dies, or the grammar insists on marking time or
gender the original never did. You have just met translation: it is not a copy, it is
a *re-making*. Every translator chooses which thread of meaning to keep and which must
be let go, because no two languages sort the world in exactly the same way (S-168). To
read a translation well is to read it as one voice's choices — not as the text itself.

## Materials

- Translation comparison sheet (gained / lost / changed)
- Learning journal

## Preparation

- Select two or three short translations of one text (a proverb, a poem line, a
  well-known opening) for the worked example.
- Retrieval: from Grade 9–10, recall that the same idea can be said in many languages
  (D04.S3.04.02, D04.S4.06.01). Today we ask what *changes* when it is.
- Prepare the worked example marked with one gain and one loss.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **translation shapes
meaning — it is an art of choices in which every version gains, loses, and changes
something, so a translation is read as one translator's decisions, not as the text
itself.** Words carry culture (S-167), and languages sort the world differently in a
moderate, well-supported sense (S-168) — so no translation is a perfect copy. Treat
this as *description of how language works*, and hold "translation is an art with gain
and loss" as the unit's guiding value — never ranking one language or one translation
above another (global + egalitarian lenses).

Model the comparison once, then let learners compare (worked example → guided practice
→ independent work; S-011). The **global** lens is the spine: choose examples beyond
one region's literature — a Persian line of Rūmī (S-419/S-352), a Sundiata proverb
carried by griots (S-269), a line from a sacred text, a market proverb — so learners
see translation as a worldwide practice, not a Western one. The **critical-thinking**
lens: compare *specific choices* — a word, a rhythm, a grammatical marker — not vague
"feelings" about the versions. The **egalitarian** lens: no language is "richer" or
"poorer"; each encodes its own distinctions (kinship terms, honorifics, animacy),
which is why translation gains *and* loses in both directions. The **ethics** lens: a
faithful translation owes the author care — a careless one can misrepresent a person
or a people. Preview: Lesson 6 asks who *does* the translating — machine or human —
and when each is trustworthy.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 9–10: the same idea can be said in many languages.
   Today you ask what *happens* to the idea on the way across.
2. **Meet the comparison (10 min).** Look at the translation comparison sheet: rows for
   **gained**, **lost**, **changed**. Watch a worked example — one short line in two
   translations. Mark what each version gains, what it loses, and what it simply
   changes.
3. **Learn why it happens (10 min).** No two languages sort the world in the same way
   (S-168): one marks time, another marks respect, another marks whether a thing is a
   person or an object. So a translator *must* choose. Write in your journal: one
   distinction your home language makes that another language you know does not (or
   the reverse).
4. **Compare two translations (15 min).** Take a second short text in two versions.
   Fill the sheet. Ask: which word choices differ? What does each translator seem to
   care most about — the sound, the literal words, or the feeling? Which meaning did
   each let go?
5. **Make the point precisely (10 min).** Write one sentence naming a specific gain and
   one naming a specific loss. Be exact — not "it sounds different" but "the word for
   'home' here also means 'fire,' and that second meaning is lost."
6. **Close (5 min).** Remember: a translation is a translator's choices, not the text
   itself. Add this page to your translation comparison. Next time: the machine
   translator and the human one.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide the two translations with the differing words already
  underlined; ask only "what is gained and what is lost?"
- **Extension:** Find a third translation of the same text and argue which of the
  three keeps a specific meaning best — and which keeps the sound best.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self):** Can the learner name one specific thing a translation gains,
  one it loses, and one it changes, and say which meaning the translator chose to
  keep?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The translation comparison sheet, added to the
  portfolio as the "cross-language and translation comparison" entry.

## Home connection

Ask an adult to say a favorite proverb or song line in your shared language, then try
together to carry it into another language you both partly know. Notice what refuses
to cross.
