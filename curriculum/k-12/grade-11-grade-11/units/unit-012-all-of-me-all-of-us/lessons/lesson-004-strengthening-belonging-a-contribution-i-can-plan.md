# Lesson 4 — Strengthening Belonging: A Contribution I Can Plan

## Summary

Learners pick a community they actually care about and plan one contribution that
strengthens belonging in it. The core move is honest: belonging is a circle with edges,
and edges are not random. Learners name **who is on the edge and why** — then design a
contribution that draws people in, with a first step they can take. This is the
D02.S3.11.02 artifact and the bridge to the Grade-12 summit of nested communities from
family to the global community of all life (D02.S3.12.01).

## Objectives

- Envision and plan a contribution that strengthens belonging in a community I care
  about. _(D02.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

Every group you belong to — a family, a neighborhood, a team, a faith or cultural
community, an online circle, a workplace — has an edge. Some people stand inside it
easily; others hover at the edge, half in and half out, because of language, money,
ability, age, or simply because no one has reached for them yet. Belonging is not a
given; it is something people build, one welcome at a time. This lesson asks you to look
at one circle you care about, see its edge honestly, and plan one contribution that
strengthens the circle — a small act, planned carefully, is enough to begin.

- Belonging-circle graphic (SVG)
- Writing journal or portfolio

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **belonging is not a
given — it is built, and it has edges; a contribution is a planned action that draws
people in, and naming who is on the edge is the honest first step.** The descriptive
research: even arbitrary group assignment triggers in-group favoritism, so "who is in and
who is out" runs deeper than intention (Tajfel's minimal-group work, S-232); and a
community's boundaries are often imagined and built rather than natural (Anderson, S-415,
on the nation as an "imagined community" — held as description, not insult). The
inclusion ideal — that settings should treat difference as ordinary rather than deficient
and remove barriers so every person is valued — is a value we hold (UNESCO, S-158; used as
a value frame, not a factual claim).

The **egalitarian** lens is central: notice whose belonging is limited by *structure* — a
stair where there could be a ramp, a language only some speak, a cost some cannot meet —
not by effort. The **ethics** lens: a contribution is a promise to act, and it should be
kept small and doable. The **global/anthropological** lens: communities take many shapes —
village, diaspora, chosen family, movement — and no shape is the default or the best; the
circle is one map, not the only one. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing a real
edge (who is actually left out) from a comfortable guess (who we assume is fine) is careful
description, not opinion. Model one worked example (S-011), then let learners plan. Hold
the tone hopeful — a small, specific contribution is enough to begin.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** Name one community you belong to and care about. Then ask: who is
   at the center of that circle, and who hovers at the edge? Say one name or one kind of
   person for each.
2. **See the circle (8 min).** Look at the belonging-circle graphic: a ring with a gap.
   Belonging has edges, and edges are not random — language, money, ability, age, or
   simply not being reached for can put a person at the edge. A contribution is the block
   that closes the gap.
3. **Name the edge honestly (12 min).** Choose one community you care about. Write: **who
   is at the center, who is at the edge, and why** — is the edge about structure (a
   barrier) or about reaching (no one has reached yet), or both? Be specific; "everyone is
   welcome" usually hides a real edge.
4. **Plan the contribution (15 min).** Design one contribution that strengthens belonging.
   Write: **(a) what** you will do (an action, not a feeling); **(b) who** it draws in;
   **(c) how** it removes a barrier or extends a welcome; **(d) the first step** you can
   take this week. Keep it small enough to actually do.
5. **Close (8 min).** In your portfolio, finish: "The circle I want to strengthen is ___,
   and one way I can draw someone in is ___." This is your belonging artifact.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fill-in frame — "In my ___, the center is ___, the edge is ___,
  and one thing I could do to draw people in is ___."
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the circle aloud — a ring of people with a gap,
  one figure placing a contribution block into the gap — and offer a tactile circle of
  stones with one gap the learner can close with a final stone.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Close a real gap in a circle of
  people or objects, and point to the person (or stone) you would draw in as a partner
  reads the prompts; the plan may be spoken or drawn.
- **Extension:** Plan for a *structural* edge — one barrier (a stair, a cost, a language)
  your contribution could remove or soften, and how you would check it actually worked.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name a community, its center and its edge,
  and design a specific, first-stepped contribution that strengthens belonging?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The belonging-circle contribution plan, as the community
  artifact of the capstone.

## Home connection

Ask an elder or neighbor: "Who do you remember being left at the edge of a community, and
what drew them in — or what could have?" Add what you learn to your plan.
