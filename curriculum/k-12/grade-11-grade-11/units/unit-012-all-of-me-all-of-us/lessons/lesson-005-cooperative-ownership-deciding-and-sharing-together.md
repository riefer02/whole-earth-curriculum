# Lesson 5 — Cooperative Ownership: Deciding and Sharing Together

## Summary

Learners examine cooperative ownership across the world — worker cooperatives, credit
unions, and communities governing shared resources — and contrast **"one member, one
vote"** with ownership held by a few. This year's deepening is the question of
**accountability**: not only *who* decides and benefits, but *what keeps the deciding
honest and the benefit shared over time*. This is the first half of the D02.S4.11.02
artifact.

## Objectives

- Examine examples of cooperative ownership and explain how shared deciding and benefit,
  kept honest by accountability, differ from ownership held by a few. _(D02.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of a group of people who share one well. If one person owned the well alone, they
could set the price and keep the profit; everyone else would drink on their terms. If
instead the people who use the well own it together — one vote each — then the water is
managed for all of them, and the benefit flows back to the users. But even a shared well
needs a way to check itself: who makes sure the person drawing for the group does not
quietly take extra, or that the well is cleaned and not over-drawn? That checking is
accountability. Credit unions, food co-ops, farming collectives, and shared housing are
versions of the same well, all over the world — and maybe near you.

## Materials

- "One member, one vote" graphic (SVG)
- Writing journal or portfolio

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from Grade 10's closing unit, worker co-ops, credit unions, and the
  charity–mutual aid–cooperative spectrum. Today we deepen cooperative *ownership*
  specifically and add the accountability question.
- Prepare a two-column contrast: ownership by a few vs. one member, one vote — plus a
  third row: who checks that it stays fair.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a cooperative is a
people-centred enterprise jointly owned and democratically controlled by its members —
run on "one member, one vote" — so the people who use or work in it also decide and
benefit, and accountability is what keeps that true over time.** The International
Cooperative Alliance (founded 1895) reports about 3 million cooperatives whose members
represent at least 12% of humanity (S-486). Mondragón, a federation of worker
cooperatives in the Basque Country, is a famous large example (S-487). Communities have
long governed shared resources — forests, fisheries, irrigation, grazing — by their own
rules for the common good (Ostrom, S-416). These are descriptive facts; "shared ownership
is a fairer way" is a value we examine, not assert.

The **egalitarian** lens is the heart of the lesson: who owns and decides determines who
benefits, and accountability is the guard that keeps power from re-concentrating. The
**ethics** lens: two systems can both be legal and yet serve very different ends. The
**global/anthropological** lens: cooperatives and shared resource-governance are not a
Western invention — Ostrom documented them across many societies, and cooperative forms
thrive on every continent. The **critical-thinking** lens: asking "who decides, who
benefits, and who checks?" about any system is careful analysis, not opinion. Distinguish
evidence from values: "cooperatives are widespread, member-owned, and use accountability
mechanisms" is descriptive; "that is a fairer shape for an economy" is a judgment learners
are invited to weigh. Model the contrast once (S-011), then let learners examine examples.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** What is the difference between a business owned by one person and
   one owned by everyone who works in it? Say it in your own words, then we will sharpen
   it — and add the question no one asked yet: who checks that it stays fair?
2. **See the difference (8 min).** Look at the "one member, one vote" graphic: a circle
   of people around a shared shop, farm, and bank, with an outer ring of accountability.
   In a cooperative, one member has one vote — not one vote per coin or per share. Who
   decides, who benefits, and who checks, when the answer is "everyone, together"?
3. **Examine real examples (20 min).** Choose two to study. **Worker co-ops:** the people
   who work in the business own it and elect its direction (e.g., Mondragón, S-487).
   **Credit unions:** members own the financial institution and share its benefit (the ICA,
   S-486, documents cooperatives worldwide). **Shared-resource commons:** communities
   managing forests, fisheries, or irrigation by their own fair rules (Ostrom, S-416).
   For each, write: what is shared, who decides, who benefits — and *what mechanism keeps
   it accountable* (an elected board, open books, a rule the group can change).
4. **Contrast (8 min).** Draw two columns: "owned by a few" and "owned by the members."
   Place each example, and note one thing that changes for the people when ownership
   changes — and one thing that accountability protects.
5. **Close (7 min).** In your portfolio, write one sentence: "When people own, decide, and
   hold themselves accountable together, what changes is ___."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fill-in frame — "In a ___, the people who ___ own it, decide by
  ___, the benefit goes to ___, and ___ keeps it honest."
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the graphic aloud — six people around a shared
  table holding a shop, a farm, and a bank, each with one equal vote, and an outer ring of
  accountability — and offer six stones placed evenly around a shared object with a string
  loop around all of them so "one member, one vote" and the accountability ring can be
  felt.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Sort pre-printed example cards into
  two piles — "owned by a few" and "owned by the members" — by placing or pointing, with a
  partner reading each card aloud.
- **Extension:** Compare a worker co-op with a conventional firm on one question — how
  ownership *and accountability* change who decides and who gains — and argue which serves
  the members better, with a reason.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name a cooperative form, explain "one
  member, one vote," and contrast who decides/benefits/checks in shared vs. concentrated
  ownership?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The examples-and-contrast page, as the first half of the
  cooperative-effort artifact.

## Home connection

Ask whether anyone near you belongs to a cooperative, credit union, or shared fund — and
how the deciding is shared and kept honest. If there is one nearby, find out one way its
members hold each other accountable.
