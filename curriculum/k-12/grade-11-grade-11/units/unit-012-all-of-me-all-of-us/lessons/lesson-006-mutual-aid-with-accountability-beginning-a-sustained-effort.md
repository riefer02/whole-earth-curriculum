# Lesson 6 — Mutual Aid with Accountability: Beginning a Sustained Effort

## Summary

Learners plan and begin a **sustained** mutual-aid or cooperative effort. They retrieve
the charity-vs-mutual-aid distinction (where the power sits), then add this year's
deepening: **accountability and sustainability** — a four-step loop (plan, act, check,
adjust) that keeps the effort honest and alive past the first flush of enthusiasm. This
completes the D02.S4.11.02 artifact and points to Grade 12's ethic of care (D02.S4.12.01).

## Objectives

- Plan and begin a sustained mutual-aid or cooperative effort with accountability.
  _(D02.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

When a neighbor's roof is damaged, charity is a one-way gift in which the giver keeps the
deciding; mutual aid is the whole street gathering — one brings tiles, one a ladder, one
cooks — and the neighbor gives back when the next roof fails. Both repair the roof, but
in the second the deciding and the dignity stay with everyone. Yet even the best first
gathering can fade: who comes next week, who holds the plan, who notices when one person
is doing everything and burning out? That is what accountability and sustainability mean —
the difference between a single warm day of help and a network that is still helping a
year later.

## Materials

- Mutual-aid accountability loop graphic (SVG)
- A one-page effort plan

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from Grade 10's closing unit, the charity–mutual-aid–cooperative
  spectrum and named traditions of communal work (harambee, gotong royong, mink'a/minga,
  barn raising). Today we deepen the charity vs. solidarity distinction *and* add the
  accountability loop that makes an effort last.
- Prepare the contrast: charity (one-way gift, power kept) vs. mutual aid (equals giving,
  receiving, and deciding together) — plus the sustainability question.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **mutual aid is people
meeting one another's needs together as equals — sharing the giving and the deciding — and
accountability is what makes a single act into a sustained effort.** Dean Spade's *Mutual
Aid* (S-345) makes the charity-vs-solidarity distinction precisely, and it is a
*descriptive* distinction: saying they differ is not saying "charity is bad." The term
"mutual aid" was popularized by Kropotkin's 1902 *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*
(S-163). Named traditions of communal work span the world: harambee (East Africa),
gotong royong (Indonesia), mink'a/minga (the Andes), and barn raising (North America),
among others (S-164) — present each as one named tradition in one place, never as "all
people there do this," and invite learners to verify local details with their own
community.

The **egalitarian** lens is central: who keeps the power is the difference between the two
forms, and a shared accountability loop (rotating roles, open plans, a named checker) keeps
power from re-concentrating inside even a well-meant effort. The **ethics** lens: dignity
matters as much as the roof. The **global/anthropological** lens: these traditions are
living practices on every continent, not ranked and not a Western export. The
**critical-thinking** lens: "what keeps this honest and alive past next month?" is careful
planning, not optimism. Distinguish evidence from values: "mutual-aid traditions are
widespread and documented, and accountability structures help cooperation thrive" is
descriptive (see also Slavin, S-147, and Johnson & Johnson, S-233, on cooperative
structures); "sharing the deciding is a fairer way to help" is a value learners weigh.
Model the plan once (S-011), then let learners plan.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** Think of a time neighbors or friends helped each other with no one
   in charge and no one being paid. Who decided how the help flowed — and did it last, or
   fade? Say it in one sentence.
2. **See the circle (8 min).** Look at the mutual-aid graphic: everyone gives, everyone
   receives, arrows run both ways — and a four-step loop (plan, act, check, adjust) wraps
   the outside. Compare it to a one-way gift. Where does the power sit in each, and what
   keeps the circle turning?
3. **Examine real examples (12 min).** Choose one. **Named traditions:** harambee in East
   Africa, gotong royong in Indonesia, mink'a/minga in the Andes, or barn raising in North
   America (S-164). **Modern networks:** neighborhood solidarity groups, disaster-response
   networks, or food-sharing circles. Write: who gives, who decides, how the help flows
   back — and what kept (or could keep) it going.
4. **Plan your own effort (15 min).** On your one-page plan, write: **(a) Plan** — one
   sustained mutual-aid or cooperative effort you could begin, tied to a real need; who
   gives, who receives, who decides. **(b) Act** — the first concrete action this week.
   **(c) Check** — how you will know, next month, that it is still working and no one is
   burning out. **(d) Adjust** — one thing you will change if the check says it is
   slipping. Keep it small enough to actually begin.
5. **Close (8 min).** In your portfolio, write one sentence: "The effort I will begin is
   ___, and what will keep it mutual *and* lasting is ___."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a fill-in frame — "In my effort, ___ gives, ___ decides, the help
  flows ___, and I will know it is still working when ___."
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the graphic aloud — five people in a circle
  passing things forward with arrows flowing both ways, wrapped by a plan-act-check-adjust
  loop — and offer a real circle where the group passes one object in both directions so
  reciprocity can be felt.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Build the plan with four cards or
  found objects in order and point to each as a partner reads the labels; the plan may be
  spoken or drawn.
- **Extension:** Add a second check and a rotation plan — one way to share the work so the
  effort does not rest on one person's shoulders.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner plan a mutual-aid or cooperative effort
  that names who gives/decides, includes a first action, a progress check, and an
  adjustment — and distinguish it from charity?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-page effort plan, as the second half of the
  cooperative-effort artifact.

## Home connection

Ask an elder about a tradition of communal help they know — a harvest, a wedding, a
recovery, a shared task — and how the help flowed back, and what kept it alive over time.
Add what you learn to your plan.
