# Lesson 1 — Values, Purpose, and a Meaningful Life

## Summary

Learners return to the year's identity work and name, in their own words, three things
that point the way: their **values** (what they hold as good), their **purpose** (the
direction they are leaning), and their sense of what a **meaningful life** looks like
for them. The three are held as a compass, not a map — a direction to steer by, never a
verdict. This is the first artifact of the closing portfolio and the first step toward
the Grade-12 handoff.

## Objectives

- Articulate my developing values, purpose, and sense of what a meaningful life looks
  like for me. _(D01.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of the last time you chose one path over another — what to study, who to stand
with, how to spend a free hour. Under that small choice there was a value: something you
hold as good, even when no one is watching. String a few of those choices together and
you begin to see a direction, a purpose. And the kind of life you are quietly aiming
toward — that is meaning. Whether you grow up by a river, in a city, on a farm, or
between places, these three — what you hold good, where you are leaning, and what a
good life looks like to you — are yours to name. This lesson asks you to say them, in
your own words, as they stand today.

## Materials

- Values–purpose–meaning compass (SVG)
- Portfolio folder or journal

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from Unit 1 this year (D01.S2.11.01 is anticipated there as "Feelings,
  Identity & Purpose"), and from Grade 10's closing unit (the considered position and the
  whole-person map). Today we add the *purpose* and *meaning* arms explicitly.
- Prepare three plain working definitions: a **value** is something you hold as good; a
  **purpose** is the direction your life is leaning, discovered by trying and reflecting;
  **meaning** is your sense of what makes a life worth living, in your own terms.
- Model one worked example of the three-arm compass before learners draft their own
  (worked example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land, warmly and without
pressure: **values, purpose, and meaning are three arms of one compass that the learner
holds — developing, revisable, and theirs to steer.** Identity is a developing story, not
a finished fact (Erikson, S-155); the selves a person imagines becoming — "possible
selves" — are the bridge between the present and future motivation (Markus & Nurius,
S-399); and a sense of purpose grows by trying, reflecting, and being supported, not by
sudden certainty — roughly one in five young people reports a clear sense of purpose, and
that is ordinary, not a failure (Damon, S-400). Hold all three as *description*, and the
line "you are allowed not to know yet" as a *value* we protect.

Hold the four lenses visibly. **Ethics:** naming what you hold good is the first step of
acting on it — values become ethics when they meet real choices. **Egalitarianism:** no
one's purpose outranks another's; a purpose may be quiet or loud, near or far, and a
meaningful life can look like care for a family or a movement, a craft or a calling.
**Global/anthropological:** what counts as a meaningful life differs across cultures — a
life of service, of craft, of kinship, of study, of prayer, of tending land — and no one
culture's answer is the default. **Critical-thinking:** distinguishing a value you have
*chosen and tested* from one you have merely *inherited* is an act of careful, honest
thought. Model once (S-011), then let learners draft. Keep the tone celebratory; this is
the heart of the capstone.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** Without looking back, name two things you remember from your
   identity and purpose work this year — a value you named, or a self you imagined
   becoming. Then check yourself: what did you get right, and what has shifted since?
2. **Meet the compass (8 min).** Look at the graphic: three arms — **values**, **purpose**,
   **meaning** — meet where you stand. A value is what you hold good; a purpose is the
   direction you are leaning; meaning is your sense of a life worth living. Which of the
   three is easiest for you to name today, and which hardest?
3. **Draft your compass (20 min).** Draw the three arms and write on each, in your own
   words. **Values:** two or three things you hold as good, with one line on how you
   *know* you hold them (a choice you actually made). **Purpose:** the direction you are
   leaning right now — and one thing you have tried that helped you discover it.
   **Meaning:** one or two sentences on what a good, worthwhile life looks like *for you*
   — not for anyone else. Leave room to revise; this is a compass, not a map.
4. **Read it back (8 min).** Read your compass once, quietly or to a partner. Underline
   the line you feel most certain of, and circle the line you are still deciding. Notice
   that both can be true at once.
5. **Close (7 min).** Write one sentence that begins: "The life I am learning to steer
   toward is one where…" Put it at the top of your portfolio. This is page one.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide sentence starters for each arm ("I hold as good…", "I am leaning
  toward…", "A worthwhile life for me looks like…") and ask for one line each.
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the compass aloud — three labeled arms meeting
  at a center where a figure stands — and offer a tactile version: three sticks or cords
  laid from a central stone in three directions, each labeled by touch.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Build the compass with three found
  objects placed from a center, and point to each as a partner or pre-printed label names
  it; the closing sentence may be a chosen symbol or drawing.
- **Extension:** Add a counter-consideration — one value you might hold differently in
  Grade 12, and a sentence on why revising it would be growth, not failure.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner name values, a leaning purpose, and a sense
  of meaning in their own words — and distinguish the arm they are certain of from the arm
  they are still deciding?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-arm compass with the closing sentence, as the
  first page of the closing portfolio.

## Home connection

Ask an adult who has known you a long time: "What value do you see me holding, even when I
do not say it?" Decide whether what they name belongs on your compass — and whether you
agree with it.
