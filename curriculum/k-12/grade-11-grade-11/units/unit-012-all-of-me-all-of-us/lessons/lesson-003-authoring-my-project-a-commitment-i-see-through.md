# Lesson 3 — Authoring My Project: A Commitment I See Through

## Summary

Learners take the purpose they articulated in Lessons 1–2 and attach it to something
*real*: a personal project or commitment they will author and see through. They build a
four-step loop — **commit, plan, act, check and adjust** — and learn that authorship is a
loop they steer, not a single heroic leap. This is the D01.S3.11.02 artifact of the
capstone and the bridge to the self-directed life goal of Grade 12 (D01.S3.12.01).

## Objectives

- Take authorship of a personal project or commitment and see it through with
  self-direction. _(D01.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

Think of the last thing you built or stuck with on your own — a piece of music, a repair,
a skill, a habit, a cause you kept showing up for. You did not wait for permission; you
picked a commitment, planned a way in, acted, and checked whether it was working. That is
authorship, and you already do it. This lesson names that skill and puts it on a
four-step loop, so you can steer one real commitment into Grade 12 — and beyond — on
purpose instead of by accident.

## Materials

- Authorship loop graphic (SVG)
- A one-page project plan

## Preparation

- Retrieval cue: from Grade 10's closing unit, the four-step learning plan (choose a goal,
  find resources, check progress, adjust); from this unit's Lessons 1–2, the purpose and
  its Grade-12 chapter. Today we attach the purpose to one real commitment.
- Prepare one worked example of the four-step authorship loop.
- Note the Grade-12 summit: exercising self-directed agency by setting, planning, and
  acting on a meaningful life goal with autonomy and accountability (D01.S3.12.01).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **authorship is a
four-step loop — commit, plan, act, check and adjust — and the learner is the author at
every step.** Specific, checkable commitments outperform vague wishes (Locke & Latham,
S-223); checking progress (self-testing) is one of the strongest learning techniques we
know (Dunlosky et al., S-039; Roediger & Karpicke, S-228); and the "check and adjust" step
is where mastery lives — "not yet" is information, never a verdict (Dweck, S-096 — held
honestly: growth-mindset effects are real but modest, and effort is not a guarantee).

The **ethics** lens: a commitment you can check honestly is a commitment you can be
trusted with — including by yourself. The **egalitarian** lens: resources are not evenly
distributed — a plan that names a fallback (a person, a free library, a neighbor's skill)
works for more lives than one that assumes money and devices. The **global** lens:
self-paced, low-resource schooling (Escuela Nueva, S-008) and teaching at the learner's
level (TaRL, S-009) show that self-direction works across very different places — it is
not a privilege of the few. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing a checkable
milestone from a vague hope is careful thought, not opinion. Distinguish evidence from
values: the loop and the power of checking progress are descriptive; "every learner
deserves to steer their own work" is a value we hold. Model the loop once (S-011), then
let learners plan.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (7 min).** When you stuck with something on your own this year, what did you
   do first, and how did you know it was working? Name the steps you already use — today
   we give them names.
2. **Meet the loop (8 min).** Look at the authorship-loop graphic. The four steps —
   commit, plan, act, check and adjust — repeat, like walking. A commitment that cannot
   be checked is only a wish; a check tells you the truth so you can steer.
3. **Author your plan (20 min).** On your one-page plan, write: **(a) Commit** — one
   real project or commitment, tied to your purpose, phrased so you could tell if you got
   there. **(b) Plan** — at least two resources: a person, a place, a skill, a tool; name
   a fallback if the first fails. **(c) Act** — the first concrete step you can take this
   week, however small. **(d) Check and adjust** — a milestone, a small test, a thing you
   can do or show; and what you will change if the check says "not yet."
4. **Peer-check (8 min).** Trade plans with a partner. Ask them one question: "How will
   you know it is working?" Help them make the check specific — and offer to be their
   accountability partner.
5. **Close (7 min).** Read your plan once and circle the step you feel strongest in. Put
   it in your portfolio — it is your Grade-12 commitment to yourself.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Provide a sentence starter for each step ("I commit to…", "I can draw on…",
  "My first step is…", "I will know it is working when…, and if not I will…").
- **Access (blind / low vision):** Describe the loop aloud — commit, plan, act, check and
  adjust — and offer four folded-paper panels or cards the learner can touch in order.
- **Alternative expression (non-writing, non-oral):** Build the plan with four cards or
  found objects placed in order and pointed to; a partner reads each label and the learner
  confirms with a nod, gesture, or pointing.
- **Extension:** Add a second milestone and a note on how you will seek help at the right
  moment — balancing independence with asking, the skill Grade 12 asks you to sustain.

## Assessment

- **Formative (self + peer):** Can the learner state a specific commitment, two resources
  with a fallback, a first step, and a concrete progress check with an adjustment?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The one-page project plan, as the self-direction artifact
  of the capstone.

## Home connection

Show your plan to an adult and ask them to be one of your resources — or to name someone
they know who could help. A commitment with a person in it travels further than one alone.
