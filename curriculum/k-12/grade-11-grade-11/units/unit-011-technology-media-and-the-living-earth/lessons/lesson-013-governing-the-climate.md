# Lesson 13 — Governing the Climate

## Summary

Learners analyze a global environmental agreement — the UN climate process and the
Paris Agreement — and separate its two hard problems: **coordination** (no country can
solve it alone, and each can wait for others) and **justice** (those who emitted most
and those who feel the impacts most are often not the same). They evaluate how the
agreement tries to hold both together.

## Objectives

- Analyze global environmental policy — a climate agreement — and the coordination
  and justice questions it raises. _(D10.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Climate change is the clearest example of a problem no one can solve alone. The
carbon one country burns warms every country, so acting alone is costly and waiting
is tempting — yet if everyone waits, everyone loses. That is the **coordination**
problem. And the problem has a second face: the countries that emitted the most over
two centuries are often not the countries feeling the worst impacts today. That is
the **justice** problem — who should pay, and who must be protected first. The
agreements nations sign are attempts to hold these two problems together in one
document, and reading them well means seeing both at once.

## Materials

- Policy-analysis organizer
- Stewardship journal
- Question cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the organizer; prepare the question cards.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 9–11, the cycles (and from Lesson 3, uneven distribution).
  Today we analyze the *policy* that responds to those systems.
- Prepare the policy facts: the IPCC is the authoritative intergovernmental body
  summarizing climate science, including who is most exposed and least able to adapt
  (S-005). The places that emitted most greenhouse gases over two centuries are often
  not the places that feel the worst impacts (Our World in Data, S-006). The UN
  climate process and the Paris Agreement are the central multilateral attempts to
  coordinate a response, with the principle of "common but differentiated
  responsibilities" (IPCC, S-005, as the authoritative frame). The idea that
  development and environment must be pursued together traces to the Brundtland
  Report's "sustainable development" (S-395).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a climate
agreement is an attempt to solve two problems at once — coordination (a shared system
no one can fix alone, where each can wait for others) and justice (the biggest
historical emitters and the most exposed are often not the same places; IPCC, S-005;
Our World in Data, S-006).** The agreement's central principle — "common but
differentiated responsibilities" — is the document's answer to the justice problem,
and it is exactly what makes the negotiation hard. The Brundtland framing of
"sustainable development" (S-395) is the older idea these agreements build on.

Two things to hold. First, teach the *structure* of the problem, not a scoreboard of
which country is good or bad — the point is that coordination and justice pull in
different directions, and policy is the art of holding both. Second, distinguish
*evidence* (what the science and data show — who emits, who is exposed) from *value*
(what is fair — who should pay), and mark plainly that "common but differentiated
responsibilities" is a *value* choice embodied in a document, not a scientific fact.

The **environment** lens is core (the system being governed). The **ethics** lens:
what the rich and the exposed owe each other. The **egalitarianism** lens: justice
*is* the second half of this lesson. The **global** lens is the frame itself —
multilateral policy. The **critical-thinking** lens: separating coordination from
justice so each can be analyzed, then seeing how the agreement braids them.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 9–11, what did you learn about how the carbon
   cycle connects everyone? Today: how do nations *govern* that shared system?
2. **Meet the two problems (12 min).** **Coordination** — the carbon one country
   burns warms every country; no one can solve it alone, and each can wait for others
   to act first. **Justice** — the places that emitted most over two centuries are
   often not the places feeling the worst impacts (S-006; S-005). Name each problem in
   your own words.
3. **Read the agreement (12 min).** The UN climate process and the Paris Agreement
   are the central attempts to coordinate a response, built on "common but
   differentiated responsibilities" (S-005) and the older idea of sustainable
   development (S-395). In your organizer, list the agreement's main features.
4. **Sort the questions (12 min).** With a partner, sort each feature: does it answer
   a *coordination* question or a *justice* question? Mark the ones that try to do
   both.
5. **Evaluate the hardest question (9 min).** Choose the single hardest question the
   agreement faces — coordination or justice — and write one paragraph on why it is
   hard and what would count as progress on it.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, why a climate agreement is not a
   science document but a *justice document* about a *scientific* fact.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one coordination problem and one justice problem, leaving the
  feature-sorting aside.
- **Extension:** Compare the climate agreement's structure to another global
  environmental agreement (e.g., the Convention on Biological Diversity, S-279) and
  note one coordination or justice question they share.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the two problems, connect them to
  the agreement's features, and evaluate the hardest question it raises?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed organizer with the "hardest question"
  paragraph, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home how they would divide responsibility for a shared problem that no
one caused alone — the litter on a shared street, or the shared air. Write one
sentence on their answer and one on whether it matches "common but differentiated
responsibilities."
