# Lesson 3 — Who Benefits, Who Is Harmed?

## Summary

Learners trace one everyday technology from extraction to disposal and map where its
**benefits** land and where its **harms** land — usually not the same people, and
usually not the same places. They analyze how this unevenness is distributed across
the world, naming extraction, supply chains, the digital divide, and externalized
costs.

## Objectives

- Analyze how the benefits and harms of technology are distributed unevenly across
  the world. _(D10.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

The object in your pocket is a map of the world, folded up small. The minerals in it
were pulled from one place, assembled in another, sold in a third, and may be dumped
in a fourth. The *good* of it — connection, information, work — usually lands close
to the buyer. The *cost* of it — polluted mines, low and unsafe pay, piles of
electronic waste — usually lands far away, on people who never chose it. This lesson
unfolds the map and asks the honest question it raises: who gets the benefit, and who
carries the harm?

## Materials

- Footprint map
- Stewardship journal
- Product-chain cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the footprint map; prepare the product-chain cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, governance; from Grade 10, media ownership and data.
  Today we ask a *distribution* question: not "does the technology work?" but "for
  whom does it work, and at whose cost?"
- Prepare the worked example: the phone. Its cobalt is sometimes mined in the
  Democratic Republic of the Congo under conditions that harm and exploit workers,
  including children (Amnesty International, S-398). Its benefit — connection,
  information, work — lands with the buyer. Model tracing this chain step-by-step
  before learners do their own (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a technology's
benefits and harms are not distributed evenly — the benefits tend to land near buyers
and the harms near the extraction and disposal ends of the chain, far from those who
buy (Amnesty, S-398).** Even the *benefit* side is uneven: hundreds of millions still
light the dark with flame and lamp while others carry instant light and instant
information in a pocket (Our World in Data, S-052); the "digital divide" is a
documented global pattern, not a phrase (World Bank, S-281). Hold the point as
*description and analysis*, not yet prescription — arguing for fairer access is
Lesson 4.

Keep the tone honest but not hopeless: the point is that the unevenness is *made by
choices* (who pays, where factories go, what is subsidized), and what is made can be
remade. Guard against two temptations at this age: guilt about owning things (the
lesson is about systems, not individual blame) and numbness ("it's all bad, so
nothing matters"). Neither is true; both are ways of looking away.

The **ethics** lens: who is responsible for a harm they never see? The
**egalitarianism** lens is the core here: the gap between who benefits and who is
harmed is a fairness question. The **global** lens: the chain crosses borders, but
accountability often stops at them. The **technology** and **environment** lenses:
the same device that connects people also mines, burns, and dumps. The
**critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing what the chain *is* (evidence) from what
we *owe* the people on its far end (value).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 1, what were the three forces that govern a
   technology? Today we ask a different question: *for whom* does a technology work?
2. **Unfold the map (12 min).** Work the phone example step-by-step (S-011). List the
   stops: extraction, factory, sale, use, disposal. At each stop, name one benefit and
   one harm — and *where* each lands. The cobalt: sometimes mined in conditions that
   exploit workers, including children (S-398). The benefit: connection, information,
   work — with the buyer.
3. **Trace your own product (15 min).** Choose an everyday technology (a piece of
   clothing, a battery, a food, a device). Arrange the product-chain cards in order,
   then draw the footprint map: benefits in one column, harms in the other, with the
   place each lands.
4. **Find the pattern (10 min).** With a partner, compare your maps. Where do the
   benefits cluster? Where do the harms? Write one sentence that states the general
   pattern you see.
5. **Name the divide (8 min).** Even benefits are uneven: some places have instant
   light and information; others still light the dark with flame and lamp (S-052). How
   does this second unevenness deepen the first?
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, why "who benefits, who is harmed?" is a
   different question from "does it work?"

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Trace only two stops (extraction and use) and name one benefit and one
  harm at each.
- **Extension:** Research the access divide for one technology (internet or
  electricity) and add a data point to your map with its source.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace a product's chain, place benefits
  and harms, state the general pattern, and name the digital divide?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed footprint map with the written pattern
  statement, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Choose one object at home and, with someone there, guess its chain: where was it
likely made, from what, and where will it go when it breaks? Write one sentence on
your guess and one on what surprised you.

- On the uneven spread of even basic benefits (light, energy access): Our World in

- On the digital divide: World Bank, *World Development Report 2016: Digital

- On worked examples and guided practice: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) (S-011).
