# Lesson 5 — How Propaganda Works

## Summary

Learners name the mechanisms of propaganda and information manipulation —
**dehumanization, repetition, scapegoating, fear, and false equivalence** — and
analyze them in a historical case, learning to see manipulation as a set of
identifiable techniques rather than a mysterious force.

## Objectives

- Analyze propaganda and information manipulation in a historical case, naming the
  techniques it uses. _(D10.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

You have almost certainly felt a message try to *make* you feel something before you
had a chance to think — fear, disgust, anger, loyalty. That is not an accident and
not new. Propaganda is old technology: the same handful of techniques have been used
for centuries to move people against their own better judgment. The good news is that
techniques can be *named*, and a named technique is one you can resist. This lesson
gives you the names and the habit of asking the three questions that break the spell:
who made this, what do they want me to feel, and what is left out?

## Materials

- Technique tracker
- Stewardship journal
- Technique cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the technique tracker; prepare the technique cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10, source evaluation and media ownership. Today we add the
  *mechanisms* of manipulation.
- Prepare plain definitions, with a worked example of each (S-011): **dehumanization**
  (portraying people as less than human, so harming them feels permissible),
  **repetition** (saying it until it feels true), **scapegoating** (blaming one group
  for everything), **fear** (making people too afraid to question), and **false
  equivalence** (treating unequal things as if they were equal). These are standard
  media-literacy categories (Center for Media Literacy, S-442; NAMLE, S-151).
- For the historical case, use radio propaganda leading up to the 1994 genocide in
  Rwanda (Britannica, S-494). Teach it with care, following the USHMM teaching
  guidelines: do not sensationalize, focus on the *mechanism* of persuasion, and
  protect the dignity of victims (S-495).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **propaganda is not
magic; it is a small set of identifiable techniques — dehumanization, repetition,
scapegoating, fear, false equivalence (Center for Media Literacy, S-442; NAMLE,
S-151) — and naming the technique is the first act of resistance.** The historical
case (radio propaganda in Rwanda, 1994) is a documented, well-attested instance of
these techniques doing real, catastrophic harm (Britannica, S-494). Teach it as
*analysis of a mechanism*, never as spectacle, and follow the USHMM teaching
guidelines: avoid gratuitous detail, center the humanity of victims, and hold
questions as questions (S-495).

Emotional safety: this is a heavy topic. The curriculum's stance is that the hardest
truths can be faced honestly *when met with intellectual tools and a sense of agency*
(see `docs/development.md`). The "agency" half is the point: learners leave with a
checklist they can use, not with fear. Preview Lesson 6 as the answer to "what
resists this?" — a free press and literacy.

The **ethics** lens: manipulation works by short-circuiting moral judgment. The
**egalitarianism** lens: propaganda's most common move is to single out a group —
noticing *who* is being scapegoated is a fairness skill. The **global** lens: the
techniques recur across cultures and eras; the case is one instance, not an
exception. The **technology** lens: new media accelerate the same old techniques.
The **critical-thinking** lens is the core here.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10, how did you check whether a source was
   reliable? Today we study the reverse: how people are made *not* to check.
2. **Meet five techniques (15 min).** Read the technique cards. **Dehumanization** —
   portraying people as less than human. **Repetition** — saying it until it feels
   true. **Scapegoating** — blaming one group for everything. **Fear** — making people
   too afraid to question. **False equivalence** — treating unequal things as equal
   (S-442; S-151). Work one example of each together (S-011).
3. **Sort examples (10 min).** With a partner, sort the example sentences to their
   technique. Argue the borderline ones: some messages use two techniques at once.
4. **Analyze the case (15 min).** Read the case summary: in the lead-up to the 1994
   genocide in Rwanda, radio broadcasts repeatedly dehumanized one group and urged
   violence against them (S-494). In your tracker, find and record each technique you
   can see at work. Which one does the heaviest work, and why?
5. **Name the antidote (8 min).** For each technique, write the question that resists
   it — for repetition, "is it true, or just familiar?"; for fear, "who benefits from
   my fear?"; for false equivalence, "are these two things actually equal?"
6. **Close (2 min).** Say, in your own words, why a *named* technique is a *weakened*
   technique.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work with three techniques only (dehumanization, repetition,
  scapegoating) and match one example each, leaving the case analysis aside.
- **Extension:** Find a current message and identify which techniques it uses,
  writing the resisting question for each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the techniques, sort examples with
  reasons, and identify the techniques in the historical case?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed technique tracker with the written
  antidote questions, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a message — an ad, a rumor, a broadcast — that they once
believed and later found was misleading. Write one sentence on which technique it
used, if they can say.
