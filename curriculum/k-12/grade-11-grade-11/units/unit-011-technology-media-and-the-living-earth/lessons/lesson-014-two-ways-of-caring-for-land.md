# Lesson 14 — Two Ways of Caring for Land

## Summary

Learners compare indigenous and other place-based stewardship practices — cultural
burning, common-pool rules, the Honorable Harvest, and re-greening movements — with
industrial resource use, and argue what each teaches about sustainability. Neither is
offered as the whole answer; the skill is learning from each without ranking people
or cultures.

## Objectives

- Compare indigenous and other place-based stewardship practices with industrial
  resource use, and what each teaches about sustainability. _(D10.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

There are, broadly, two ways humans have related to land. One is **place-based
stewardship**: people who know one place deeply, take what it can renew, and give
back — burning to keep a forest healthy, harvesting with rules of reciprocity,
managing a shared commons by agreed rules. The other is **industrial resource use**:
a one-way line — extract, make, use, waste — at a scale no single place can absorb.
Both are real, both are still practiced, and each *teaches* something the other
forgets. Stewardship teaches living within what a place can renew; industry teaches
that scale and speed carry costs that must be paid. The question is not which people
are better — it is what each way of living knows.

## Materials

- Comparison chart
- Stewardship journal
- Practice cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the comparison chart; prepare the practice cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 12 (the more-than-human world) and Lesson 13 (policy). Today
  we compare two ways of *living* with land.
- Prepare the practices, each real and documented. **Cultural burning**: Indigenous
  fire practices in Australia manage Country, reduce fuel load, and support life, as
  described by Indigenous-led organizations (Firesticks/Living Knowledge Network,
  S-321; CSIRO, S-320). **Common-pool rules**: communities across many societies have
  governed shared forests, fisheries, and irrigation by their own rules (Ostrom,
  S-416). **The Honorable Harvest**: the ethic of taking only what is offered and
  giving back, so that "all flourishing is mutual" (Kimmerer, S-427). **The Green
  Belt Movement**: Wangari Maathai's grassroots tree-planting that countered
  deforestation (S-059). **Industrial use**: the ecological footprint measures how
  much nature a person or place uses against how much there is (Global Footprint
  Network, S-277); industrial drivers — land and sea use change, extraction,
  pollution — are the leading causes of biodiversity loss (IPBES, S-437).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **two real ways of
relating to land — place-based stewardship (cultural burning, S-321/S-320;
common-pool rules, S-416; the Honorable Harvest, S-427; re-greening movements, S-059)
and industrial resource use (the ecological footprint, S-277; industrial drivers of
biodiversity loss, S-437) — each teach something about sustainability: stewardship
teaches living within what a place can renew; industry teaches that scale and speed
carry costs that must be paid.** The unit closes here because this is where the
whole year lands: neither worship nor fear of technology, but honest judgment about
which way of living sustains life.

Two things to hold firmly. First, do not romanticize or rank: place-based traditions
are living, contemporary, and specific — name the people and the practice (the
Firesticks/Living Knowledge Network, the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, the Green Belt
Movement), and do not reduce them to a generic "wisdom." Second, do not collapse into
"industry is bad": industrial methods feed and power billions, and the honest
question is what they cost and whether those costs are paid — not whether they exist.
The skill is *comparison with respect*, and the mature conclusion is that the two can
learn from each other rather than one "winning."

The **environment** lens is core. The **ethics** lens: each way of living embodies a
claim about what land is owed. The **egalitarianism** lens: industrial harms, like
technological harms, land on the least powerful — and stewardship knowledge has often
been suppressed by the very systems that now seek it. The **global** lens: the
practices come from every inhabited continent. The **critical-thinking** lens:
comparing without ranking, and naming what each teaches and what each forgets.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12, what is the more-than-human world? Today: two
   ways of living with it.
2. **Meet the practices (15 min).** Sort the cards. **Stewardship**: cultural burning
   that manages Country and reduces risk (S-321; S-320); common-pool rules for shared
   forests and fisheries (S-416); the Honorable Harvest — take only what is offered,
   give back (S-427); the Green Belt Movement's tree-planting (S-059).
   **Industrial use**: the one-way extract–make–use–waste line, measured by the
   ecological footprint (S-277), driving biodiversity loss (S-437).
3. **Fill the chart (12 min).** For each column, write what it *values*, what it
   *tends to forget*, and one real practice as an example.
4. **Argue what each teaches (12 min).** With a partner: what does stewardship teach
   that industrial use forgets? What does industrial use teach that stewardship, at a
   small scale, may not? Write one sentence for each direction.
5. **Test the combination (8 min).** Can the two be combined? Under what conditions
   — where scale is needed, where local knowledge is needed? Write one sentence of
   your judgment.
6. **Close (3 min).** Say, in your own words, why "neither is the whole answer" is a
   stronger conclusion than "one is right."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort three practices (one stewardship, one industrial, one chosen) and
  name one thing each teaches, leaving the combination aside.
- **Extension:** Research one place where a place-based practice and industrial use
  meet (e.g., cultural burning alongside modern fire management) and evaluate the
  result.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner sort real practices into the two
  columns, name what each teaches and forgets, and argue a reasoned judgment about
  combining them?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed comparison chart with the written
  "what each teaches" and "combination" judgments, opened in the stewardship journal
  as the unit's closing artifact.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about one way your family or community has cared for a place —
or one thing it has taken from a place faster than it can renew. Write one sentence on
what that teaches about sustainability.
