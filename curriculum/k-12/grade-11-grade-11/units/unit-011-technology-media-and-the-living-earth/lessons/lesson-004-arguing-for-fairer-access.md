# Lesson 4 — Arguing for Fairer Access

## Summary

Learners turn analysis into argument: they construct a **claim–evidence–proposal**
argument for fairer access to a technology, drawing on documented global inequality.
They meet three levers that change access — **policy, price, and public
infrastructure** — and learn to answer the hard question "who pays?" without
collapsing into resignation or slogan.

## Objectives

- Argue for fairer access to technology, drawing on evidence about unequal
  distribution. _(D10.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Yesterday you mapped where a technology's harms and benefits land. Today you do
something harder and more useful: you argue about what to do. Fairer access is not a
mood — it is a set of specific changes to **rules** (policy), to **cost** (price),
and to the shared systems everyone relies on (**public infrastructure**, like roads,
grids, and networks). The argument has a shape: here is the unfairness, here is the
evidence, here is the change, and here is who it helps. That shape is learnable, and
it is how ordinary people have won fairer access to water, electricity, medicine, and
information before you.

## Materials

- Argument builder
- Stewardship journal
- Three lever cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the argument builder; prepare the lever cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, the footprint map and the pattern of uneven distribution.
  Today we answer "so what should change?"
- Prepare the evidence base: global inequality in resources and access is driven
  largely by structural factors — where one is born, how work and pay are organized,
  inherited wealth, health, conflict — rather than individual worth (World Bank,
  S-234; UNDP Human Development Reports, S-283; World Inequality Report, S-464).
  Model one claim–evidence–proposal argument before learners build their own (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **arguing for
fairer access is a skill with a shape — claim, evidence, proposal — and the proposal
moves one of three levers: policy, price, or public infrastructure.** The evidence
that inequality is structural (not a measure of worth) is well documented (World
Bank, S-234; UNDP, S-283; World Inequality Report, S-464). A useful concept to offer:
Johan Galtung's *structural violence* — harm that comes not from a person's blow but
from the way systems are arranged (S-462). This is not a claim that any one policy is
correct; it is a name for why harms persist without a single villain.

Two things to hold with care. First, distinguish the *value* commitment — that fairer
access matters — from the *factual* claim — that access is uneven, which the data
show. Second, keep "who pays?" as a real question, not a dismissal: every proposal
has a cost, and a mature argument says who bears it and why that is fair. Guard
against slogan (empty) and against nihilism (paralyzing); the skill is a concrete
proposal with a cost named.

The **ethics** lens: an argument for fairness is a claim about what we owe each
other. The **egalitarianism** lens is core: fairer access is the direct answer to
uneven distribution. The **global** lens: the same proposal lands differently in
different places. The **technology** lens: the design of a technology shapes what
"access" even means. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing evidence from
value, and testing a proposal against its costs.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3: what pattern did you find in who benefits and
   who is harmed? Today we argue about what to change.
2. **Meet the shape of an argument (10 min).** An argument for fairer access has
   three parts: the **claim** (what is unfair and what should change), the **evidence**
   (the documented pattern), and the **proposal** (a specific change, with who it
   helps and who pays). Read a model: the evidence that access is uneven is structural
   (S-234; S-283; S-464) — a claim that "where you are born decides too much" is a
   description of systems, not a verdict on any person.
3. **Meet three levers (10 min).** **Policy** — who sets the rules. **Price** — who
   can afford it. **Public infrastructure** — the shared roads, grids, and networks
   everyone relies on. For each, name one real example that changed access to
   something people needed.
4. **Build your argument (15 min).** Choose a technology and a place. In your journal,
   write the claim, cite one piece of evidence (S-234; S-283; or S-464), and propose
   a change that moves at least one lever. Name who it helps *and* who pays.
5. **Stress-test it (10 min).** With a partner, take turns raising the strongest
   objection — "who pays?", "will it work here?", "who decides?" — and answering it.
   Revise one part of your argument as a result.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, what makes an argument for fairer
   access *strong* rather than just *loud*.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use the claim and proposal parts only, with one piece of evidence
  supplied, leaving the objection exchange aside.
- **Extension:** Write the argument in full with a second proposal, and compare the
  two proposals' costs and benefits explicitly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner build a claim–evidence–proposal
  argument, name the lever it moves, and answer a "who pays?" objection?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed argument builder (with the revision
  after stress-testing), opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time access to something essential (water, power,
schooling, medicine, a phone) was hard for them or their family, and what made it
easier — a rule, a lower price, or shared infrastructure. Write one sentence on their
answer.
