# Lesson 6 — A Free Press and Literacy

## Summary

Learners map the two defenses against manipulation — an **independent press** that
checks facts, and a **literate public** that questions what it reads — and practice
the verification habits of each. They analyze how these two defenses work together,
and what happens when either is missing.

## Objectives

- Analyze how a free press and literacy resist propaganda and information
  manipulation. _(D10.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Yesterday you saw how a message can be built to move people without convincing them.
Today you meet the two things that have always pushed back. The first is a **free
press** — reporters who can check a claim and print what they find without being told
what to say. The second is **literacy** in its fullest sense: not just reading the
words, but asking who wrote them, with what evidence, and what was left out. A free
press without a reading public is a voice in an empty room; a reading public without a
free press has nothing independent to read. Together they are how a society refuses
to be lied to — and they are both things people build, not things that happen by
themselves.

## Materials

- Defense map
- Stewardship journal
- Two defense cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the defense map; prepare the defense cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, the five techniques of manipulation. Today: what resists
  them.
- Prepare the verification habits: ask who made it, check the evidence, compare
  independent sources ("lateral reading"), and ask what is left out — the core of
  media and information literacy (UNESCO, S-217; NAMLE, S-151; Center for Media
  Literacy, S-442). Prepare the press-freedom data point: press and participation
  freedom is measured and varies widely across countries (V-Dem, S-461).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **two defenses resist
manipulation — a free press and a literate public — and each is a practice, not a
given (UNESCO Media and Information Literacy, S-217; NAMLE, S-151; Center for Media
Literacy, S-442).** Press freedom is real, measurable, and uneven across the world
(V-Dem, S-461) — which is *why* the second defense (a literate, questioning public)
matters in every context, including places where the press is not free.

The egalitarian turn: who gets to be "media literate" — and whose skepticism is
encouraged — is itself uneven. The lesson's point is not that individuals must simply
"try harder" (that would blame the reader for a system that misleads them), but that
literacy is a *public* good to be built and shared, alongside a free press. Keep both
defenses as structural, not just personal.

The **ethics** lens: checking a claim before repeating it is a small act of care for
others. The **egalitarianism** lens: literacy and a free press are distributed
unevenly, and that matters for who can be misled. The **global** lens: press freedom
varies by country; the habits travel anywhere. The **technology** lens: the same
tools that spread manipulation also spread verification. The **critical-thinking**
lens is the core here.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5, name two techniques of manipulation. Today: what
   resists them?
2. **Meet the two defenses (10 min).** A **free press** — reporters who can check a
   claim and publish what they find. A **literate public** — readers who ask who made
   it, with what evidence, and what is left out (S-217; S-151; S-442). Work one model
   claim through both defenses together (S-011).
3. **Map the practices (10 min).** In your defense map, list what each defense *does*:
   the press checks facts, asks for evidence, reports freely; the reader questions
   sources, weighs evidence, compares independent accounts.
4. **Verify a real claim (15 min).** Take a claim you have seen online. Apply the
   steps: who made it? what is the evidence? what does an independent source say?
   what is left out? Write a one-line verdict: checked and supported, checked and
   doubtful, or rejected.
5. **Find the gaps (10 min).** With a partner, ask: what happens when there is a free
   press but few readers who question? What happens when there are questioning readers
   but no free press? Write one sentence on which gap worries you more and why.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, why a free press and a literate public
   need each other.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only the "who made this?" and "what is the evidence?"
  questions on one claim, leaving the gap analysis aside.
- **Extension:** Research press-freedom conditions in one country (S-461) and write
  how a reader there would need to adapt their verification habits.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the two defenses, apply the
  verification steps to a real claim, and analyze what happens when one defense is
  missing?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed defense map with the one-line claim
  verdict, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

With someone at home, take one claim you both heard this week and run the verification
steps together. Write one sentence on what you found and one on which step was
hardest.
