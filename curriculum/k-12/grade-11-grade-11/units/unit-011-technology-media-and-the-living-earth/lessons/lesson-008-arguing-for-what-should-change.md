# Lesson 8 — Arguing for What Should Change

## Summary

Learners turn evaluation into argument: they construct a **change–who-it-helps–what-
it-costs** proposal for reform in how creators and platforms handle harmful
information, drawing on real directions — transparent rules, accountability and
appeals, and reader tools. They learn to answer the objections that any real reform
will face.

## Objectives

- Argue for what should change in the responsibilities of creators and platforms when
  information spreads harm. _(D10.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Yesterday you assigned responsibility along the chain. Today you do the harder, more
useful thing: you propose the change. Real reforms have real shapes. Some push for
**clear rules** — public, reviewable standards for what gets ranked and removed,
instead of secret ones. Some push for **accountability** — a way for people who are
harmed to appeal and get an answer and a remedy. Some push for **reader tools** —
teaching and technology so people can verify for themselves. None of these is free or
perfect; each has a cost and an objection. A strong argument for change names the
change, names who it helps, and names what it costs — and that is exactly what you
will build.

## Materials

- Proposal builder
- Stewardship journal
- Three direction cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the proposal builder; prepare the direction cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, the chain of responsibility. Today we propose the change.
- Prepare the three directions with a worked example of each (S-011): **clear rules**
  (transparent, reviewable moderation standards), **accountability and appeals**
  (a path for the harmed to be heard and to get remedy), and **reader tools**
  (verification literacy and technology). These are real, live directions in the
  governance debate around platforms (Britannica, S-481); the institutional ideal of
  accountable, transparent decision-making is a stated international goal (UN SDG 16,
  S-463).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **arguing for change
is a skill with a shape — the change, who it helps, what it costs — and the strongest
proposals draw on real directions: transparent rules, accountability and appeals,
and reader tools (Britannica, S-481; UN SDG 16, S-463).** The skill is not to have
the "right" answer but to make a proposal that can survive the objections any real
reform faces: who decides? who pays? will it work here? who might misuse it?

Keep the lesson from collapsing into either a utopia (a proposal with no cost is not
a proposal) or a shrug (every proposal has a cost, but that is not a reason to do
nothing — the harms themselves have costs). The mature stance: weigh *costs against
costs*, not cost against nothing. Distinguish *evidence* (what reform mechanisms
exist and do) from *value* (which harms matter most to prevent). The strong
institutions ideal is a stated international commitment (S-463) — a value frame, not
a claim that it is already achieved.

The **ethics** lens: a proposal is a claim about what we owe the harmed. The
**egalitarianism** lens is core: ask whether the change protects the powerless or
concentrates more power in the already powerful. The **global** lens: the same
proposal lands differently under different laws. The **technology** lens: the change
must actually reach the design of the tool, not just its surface. The
**critical-thinking** lens: testing a proposal against objections, not just
preferring it.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, where did you place the heaviest responsibility?
   Today you propose the change that answers it.
2. **Meet three directions (10 min).** **Clear rules** — public, reviewable standards
   for ranking and removal. **Accountability and appeals** — a path for the harmed to
   be heard and get a remedy. **Reader tools** — verification literacy and technology
   (S-481; S-463). Work one model proposal of each together (S-011).
3. **Choose a direction (5 min).** Pick the direction that best answers the harm you
   care most about, and say why in one sentence.
4. **Build the proposal (15 min).** In your journal, write: **the change** (specific,
   concrete), **who it helps**, and **what it costs** (money, freedom, speed — be
   honest). Name one person or group who would resist it, and why.
5. **Answer the objection (10 min).** With a partner, exchange proposals. Raise the
   strongest objection to theirs; answer the strongest objection to yours. Revise one
   part of your proposal as a result.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, what makes a proposal for reform strong
   rather than just well-meant.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Build the change and who-it-helps parts only, with the cost supplied,
  leaving the objection exchange aside.
- **Extension:** Write the proposal as a short policy memo — the change, the evidence,
  the cost, and the response to the strongest objection.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner build a change–who-it-helps–what-it-
  costs proposal, and answer the strongest objection to it?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed proposal builder (with the revision
  after the objection exchange), opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what one change they would make to how harmful online content is
handled, and who they think should decide it. Write one sentence on their answer and
one on whether you agree.
