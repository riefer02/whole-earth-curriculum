# Lesson 12 — The More-Than-Human World

## Summary

Learners explain the idea of the **more-than-human world** — the living community
that includes rivers, forests, animals, mountains, and people — and why some
traditions treat land, water, and other species as **beings owed care**, not mere
resources. They meet living traditions in their own words, held side by side as
accounts to understand rather than to rank.

## Objectives

- Explain the idea of the more-than-human world and why some traditions treat land,
  water, and other species as beings owed care. _(D10.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

In one way of seeing, the world is a warehouse of things: timber, water, land, fish —
resources to be used and replaced. In another way of seeing, the world is a
*community*: a river you drink from and are responsible to, a forest that shelters
you, animals who are your kin, a mountain that has been here longer than any of us.
That second way has a name — the **more-than-human world** — and it is not a new idea
or a fringe one. It is carried by living traditions on every inhabited continent.
This lesson asks you not to adopt or reject it, but to *understand* it well enough to
see what it changes: when a river is a *being*, harming it is not a cost — it is a
wrong.

## Materials

- More-than-human map
- Stewardship journal
- Being cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the more-than-human map; prepare the being cards.
- Retrieval: from Lessons 9–11, the cycles that connect all life. Today we ask a
  *relational* question about those connections: what are they owed?
- Prepare the accounts, held faithfully and without ranking. Robin Wall Kimmerer, a
  botanist and citizen of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, describes how learning
  Potawatomi plant words revealed a grammar that calls plants *persons*, not things —
  a language of respect and relationship (S-427). At Standing Rock, opposition to an
  oil pipeline under the Missouri River was grounded in the Lakota phrase *Mní
  wičhóni*, "water is life" (S-485). Inuit traditions treat the animals and land they
  live with as kin, not merely quarry (S-058). The scientific parallel: the living
  world is a web on which humans depend, and its loss falls first on those who live
  closest to it (IPBES, S-437).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the more-than-human
world is the idea that the living community includes rivers, forests, animals, and
mountains — not just people — and that these beings are owed care, not merely used;
this is a living idea in traditions across the world (Kimmerer on Potawatomi animacy,
S-427; Standing Rock's "water is life," S-485; Inuit kinship with land and animals,
S-058).** The scientific register runs alongside, not against, it: human life depends
on a living web, and its loss falls hardest on those closest to it (IPBES, S-437).
Hold these as *accounts to understand*, never to rank against each other or against
science — the philosophy here is inquiry over creed (see `docs/philosophy.md` §11).

Guard against two errors. First, exoticizing: these are living traditions of real,
contemporary peoples, not museum pieces or "ancient wisdom" to be borrowed as
decoration. Second, flattening: no single concept ("animacy," "personhood," "water
is life") stands for all Indigenous or place-based traditions — name specific
peoples and their specific words, as the sources do. The respect the curriculum owes
is to the *specificity*.

The **environment** lens is core. The **ethics** lens: what is owed to a being that
cannot speak in a courtroom. The **egalitarianism** lens: this is a challenge to the
idea that only humans matter — an *expansion* of the circle of care. The **global**
lens: the idea recurs across continents and traditions, each in its own form. The
**critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing "what a tradition holds" (a faithful
description) from "what I believe" (a value).

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 9–11, the cycles connect all life. Today: what do
   those connections *owe*?
2. **Meet two ways of seeing (10 min).** A **resource** is something to be used. A
   **being** is someone to be in relationship with — owed care, not just use. These
   are two different ways of seeing the same river.
3. **Meet the traditions (15 min).** Read the excerpts. Kimmerer: Potawatomi grammar
   calls plants *persons*, not things (S-427). Standing Rock: *Mní wičhóni* — water is
   life (S-485). Inuit traditions: animals and land as kin (S-058). For each, name
   *what* is treated as a being and *why* it is owed care.
4. **Draw your circle (12 min).** Choose a real place you know. Arrange the being
   cards — river, forest, animal, mountain, human, and one more you choose — into a
   circle, and draw lines of relationship among them. Where would you place yourself
   in this circle, and why there?
5. **Name what changes (8 min).** With a partner, ask: if a river is a being, what
   actually changes about a decision to dam it, divert it, or pollute it? Write one
   concrete difference.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, what the phrase "the more-than-human
   world" adds that "the environment" does not.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name three beings of a place and one thing each is owed, leaving the
  "what changes" step aside.
- **Extension:** Compare how two traditions you read describe care for the
  non-human, and write where they agree and where they differ — without ranking them.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner explain the more-than-human world,
  name specific traditions and their concepts, and identify what treating a being as a
  being changes in practice?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed more-than-human circle with the
  written "what changes" reflection, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home whether there is a place — a tree, a river, an animal, a garden —
they feel responsible to, and why. Write one sentence on their answer and one on
whether you share it.
