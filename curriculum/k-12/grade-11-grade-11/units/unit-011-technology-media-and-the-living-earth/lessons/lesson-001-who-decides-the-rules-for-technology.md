# Lesson 1 — Who Decides the Rules for Technology?

## Summary

Learners name the three overlapping ways a technology is governed — **law,
standards, and markets** — and trace how each shapes who may use a technology, how,
and at what cost. They begin the unit's first analysis: "governance" is not one ruler
but a set of forces that often pull against one another.

## Objectives

- Analyze how technologies are governed by law, standards, and markets.
  _(D10.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of one technology you touch every day — a phone, a bus, a medicine, a way to
send money. It feels like a thing, but it is also a stack of decisions other people
made: a law said what it must not do, a **standard** made its parts fit with other
parts, and a **market** set its price and who could buy it. The phone in your hand
works because thousands of people agreed on invisible rules long before you bought
it. Naming those rules is the first move of this unit — because a rule you cannot see
is a rule you cannot question or change.

## Materials

- Governance map
- Stewardship journal
- Three role cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the governance map; prepare the three role cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D10), media ownership and platform power; from Unit 3
  this year (D09), power and accountability. Today we name the *mechanisms* — law,
  standards, markets — through which power over technology is actually exercised.
- Prepare plain definitions: **law** (statutes and courts and property rights like
  patents), **standards** (agreed technical rules that let parts work together), and
  **markets** (buyers, sellers, and price). Model each with a worked example first
  (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a technology is
governed by more than one force — law, standards, and markets — and these forces
overlap, conflict, and leave gaps (Britannica's social-media article documents
platforms setting rules and moderating content, S-481; the World Bank's Digital
Dividends report frames regulation as one of the "analog complements" that decide
whether technology spreads benefit or widens gaps, S-281).** A concrete, age-safe
worked example of law governing technology is the 1850s sewing-machine patent disputes
— several rival patents over one device, resolved only when the holders pooled them
(Britannica, S-062). Hold "governance" as *description* — who actually decides — not
yet as *recommendation* (that comes in Lesson 2).

The **ethics** lens: every governance choice is a choice about who is protected and
who is not. The **egalitarianism** lens: notice whose rules get written down and
whose do not. The **global** lens: standards and markets cross borders while laws
often stop at them — a mismatch worth naming, not resolving yet. The **technology**
lens is the core here: the artifact and its rules are one thing. The
**critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing *who decides* (evidence) from *who should
decide* (value). Preview: Lesson 2 takes up the debate over regulating one.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10: who owned and shaped the media you studied?
   Today we ask a finer question: by what *mechanisms* is a technology governed?
2. **Meet three forces (12 min).** **Law** — statutes, courts, and property rights
   such as patents. **Standards** — agreed technical rules that let parts fit
   together. **Markets** — buyers, sellers, and price. Work a model example first
   (S-011): the sewing machine of the 1850s. Several inventors held rival patents on
   one device; the machines could not be freely made until the holders pooled their
   patents (S-062). Which forces do you see at work?
3. **Map a familiar technology (15 min).** Choose one you use daily. In your
   journal's governance map, write one real example in each column: a *law* that
   shapes it, a *standard* that makes it work with other things, and a *market*
   that sets its price or its buyers.
4. **Find the gap (10 min).** With a partner, ask: where do the three forces
   *conflict*? (A patent against an open standard; a law that stops at a border while
   a market crosses it.) Write one gap and one sentence on who fills it — if anyone.
5. **Argue a quick case (8 min).** Whose voice is easiest to hear in each force —
   a government, a company, or an ordinary user? Say why, in one sentence per force.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say, in your own words, why "who governs this technology?"
   has more than one answer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Map only two forces for one technology and name one example each,
  leaving the gap analysis aside.
- **Extension:** Compare the governance of one technology in two different countries
  and name which force — law, standards, or markets — differs most and why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner define law, standards, and markets,
  map a real technology through all three, and name one gap or conflict?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed governance map with the written gap
  reflection, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a rule, standard, or price that shapes a technology they
use for work or daily life. Write one sentence on what they told you and one on
which of the three forces it belonged to.
