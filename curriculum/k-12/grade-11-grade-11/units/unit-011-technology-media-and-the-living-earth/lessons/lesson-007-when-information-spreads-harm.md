# Lesson 7 — When Information Spreads Harm

## Summary

Learners trace how harmful information travels — from a **creator**, through a
**platform's** ranking and amplification, to an **audience**, and finally to real
**harm** — and evaluate where responsibility sits at each link. They learn to
distinguish legal from ethical responsibility, and to see platform design as a choice
rather than a fact of nature.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the responsibilities of creators and platforms when information spreads
  harm. _(D10.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Not everything that spreads is true, and not everything that spreads is harmless. A
lie can move faster than a correction, and the harm it does — to a person's
reputation, to a group's safety, to public trust — is real. When that happens, who
answers for it? The person who wrote it? The platform whose design showed it to
millions? The millions who shared it? This lesson refuses the easy answer ("nobody"
or "everybody") and instead asks you to think carefully about the *shape* of the
chain — because where you place responsibility determines what you would change.

## Materials

- Chain-of-harm map
- Stewardship journal
- Four role cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the chain-of-harm map; prepare the role cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5 (techniques) and Lesson 6 (defenses). Today we ask who is
  *responsible* when harm spreads anyway.
- Prepare the mechanism facts: platforms rank and recommend by engagement, set and
  enforce rules, and moderate content (Britannica, S-481); personalization can
  invisibly surround a person with what confirms their views — the "filter bubble"
  (Pariser, S-482); synthetic media and deepfakes are now cheap to make and hard to
  detect (WITNESS, S-393). Model the chain analysis on one example before learners do
  their own (S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **when information
spreads harm, responsibility is distributed across a chain — creator, platform,
audience — and the platform's share is shaped by design choices (ranking by
engagement, personalization, moderation), not by accident (Britannica, S-481;
Pariser, S-482; WITNESS, S-393).** The skill is *evaluation*: assign responsibility
with reasons, and separate **legal** responsibility (what a law could enforce) from
**ethical** responsibility (what a person or company ought to do even if no law
forces them).

Two things to hold. First, avoid the false poles: "the platform is just a neutral
pipe" (denies the design choice) and "the platform is guilty of everything" (ignores
creators and audiences, and ignores that harm existed before platforms). The mature
view is a *distribution* of responsibility with reasons. Second, distinguish
*evidence* (what a platform's design actually does) from *value* (what we think it
owes). Both belong in the analysis; do not blur them.

The **ethics** lens: responsibility is a moral concept before it is a legal one. The
**egalitarianism** lens: harm falls hardest on those with the least power to correct
the record — ask whose reputation is cheapest to destroy. The **global** lens: the
same platform operates under different laws in different places. The **technology**
lens is core: the harm's reach is a property of the design. The **critical-thinking**
lens: reasoning about responsibility, not just assigning blame.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lessons 5–6: what are the techniques of manipulation, and
   what resists them? Today: when harm spreads anyway, who answers?
2. **Meet the chain (12 min).** A harmful claim moves from a **creator**, through a
   **platform** that ranks and recommends it, to an **audience** that believes and
   shares it — and harm lands on real people. Model the analysis: platforms rank by
   engagement, personalize what you see, and set the rules for what stays up (S-481;
   S-482). Synthetic media like deepfakes make the creator's work easier and the harm
   harder to detect (S-393).
3. **Assign responsibility (12 min).** In your chain-of-harm map, write, at each link,
   what that actor *could have done* differently — and what they did do. Use one real
   case or a constructed example.
4. **Sort legal from ethical (10 min).** With a partner, mark each responsibility as
   *legal* (a law could require it) or *ethical* (it ought to be done even without a
   law). Where do they diverge, and why does that gap matter?
5. **Argue the heaviest link (10 min).** Stand by your answer: which link carries the
   heaviest responsibility, and why? Listen to a partner who chose differently; note
   their strongest point.
6. **Close (6 min).** Say, in your own words, why "the platform is neutral" is a
   claim to be tested, not assumed.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Assign responsibility at two links only (creator and platform) with one
  reason each, leaving the legal/ethical sort aside.
- **Extension:** Research one platform's actual moderation rules (S-481) and evaluate
  how well they match the responsibilities you assigned.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace the chain, assign responsibility
  at each link with reasons, and separate legal from ethical responsibility?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed chain-of-harm map with the "heaviest
  link" argument, opened in the stewardship journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they shared something online that turned out to be
false or harmful. Write one sentence on what they felt responsible for, if anything,
and one on what you think they were responsible for.
