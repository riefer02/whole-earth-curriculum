# Lesson 3 — What Makes a Life Good?

## Summary

Learners compare four traditions' answers to the question "what makes a life good?" —
Greek **_eudaimonia_** (flourishing through virtue), Confucian **_ren_** (benevolence
and right relationship), South Asian **_dharma_** (duty and harmony with one's role),
and Buddhist **_nirvana_** (freedom from suffering). They notice what each values and
warns against, and evaluate the accounts without ranking any above another.

## Objectives

- Evaluate competing accounts of what a good life means, drawing on more than one
  cultural tradition. _(D09.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

Every culture, and every person, carries some answer to "what makes a life good?" —
whether it is spoken aloud or shown in how people spend their days. Is a good life one
of comfort, of honor, of usefulness to others, of peace of mind, of freedom? You have
almost certainly absorbed an answer from the life around you without ever being asked
to name it. This lesson names four old and serious answers from different parts of the
world — not to make you choose one, but to let you see your own answer more clearly by
setting it beside others.

## Materials

- Good-life comparison chart
- Justice journal
- Four idea cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the comparison chart; prepare the four idea cards.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D09.S1.10.02), critiquing moral arguments for hidden
  assumptions. Today we compare whole accounts of the good life.
- Prepare plain definitions: *eudaimonia* (flourishing — a life lived well through
  practiced virtue, Aristotle); *ren* (benevolence and right relationship); *dharma*
  (duty, rightness, and harmony with one's role); *nirvana* (release from suffering
  and craving). Each is a tradition's own concept, described faithfully, not judged.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **the question "what
makes a life good?" has many serious, ancient answers, and they are not all the same —
flourishing through virtue (Aristotle, SEP Virtue Ethics, S-458), benevolence and
relationship (Confucius, S-468), duty and harmony (dharma, S-469), and release from
suffering (Buddhist ethics, S-467).** The methodological point: **these are *accounts
to evaluate*, held side by side, none the default and none ranked above another.** Do
not let the lesson become "which is best?" — the skill is *comparison*, and the respect
is that each tradition is a living answer for real people.

The **ethics** lens: each account is a claim about what is right and worth pursuing.
The **egalitarianism** lens: no single tradition is treated as the standard against
which others are measured — that is itself a fairness commitment. The **global** lens:
these four are drawn from three continents and many centuries, and they stand for
many more (Indigenous, Islamic, and other traditions appear in Lesson 4 and beyond).
The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing "what a tradition *says*" (evidence)
from "what I think is *worth living for*" (value). Preview: Lesson 4 asks the same
question of whole societies.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** In Grade 10 you critiqued moral arguments for hidden
   assumptions. Today: what is one answer to "what makes a life good?" you have heard
   or felt, from your own life?
2. **Meet four answers (15 min).** Read the four idea cards. **Flourishing
   (*eudaimonia*)** — a life lived well by building virtues through practice (S-458).
   **Benevolence (*ren*)** — a life good through right relationship and care for
   others (S-468). **Duty and harmony (*dharma*)** — a life good by doing what one's
   role and order require (S-469). **Freedom from suffering (*nirvana*)** — a life
   good by loosening craving and reaching peace (S-467).
3. **Fill the chart (12 min).** For each tradition, write what it *values* and what
   it *warns against* (e.g., flourishing warns against a life of mere pleasure;
   benevolence warns against cold self-interest).
4. **Compare (10 min).** With a partner, find one place two traditions *agree* and one
   place they *disagree*. Be precise: is it a real disagreement, or different words
   for a shared insight?
5. **Evaluate (8 min).** In your justice journal, write which account most *challenges*
   your own view and why — not "which is best," but which makes you think hardest.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "the good life" has more than one
   answer, and why that matters for how we treat people who live differently.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work with two of the four traditions and name one value each, leaving
  the comparison aside.
- **Extension:** Read a short excerpt from one tradition's primary text and evaluate
  how well the summary captures it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four accounts, state what each
  values and warns against, and compare two of them precisely?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed comparison chart with the "what
  challenges me" reflection, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what they think makes a life good, and which of the four ideas —
flourishing, benevolence, duty, or peace — comes closest to their answer. Write one
sentence on their answer and one on how it compares to yours.
