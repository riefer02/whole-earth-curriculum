# Lesson 1 — The Shape of Our Choices

## Summary

Learners name the difference between the choices a person can **imagine** and the
choices actually open to them, and trace the **social and economic structures** —
**rules, resources, and roles** — that shape that gap. They begin the unit's first
argument: how much of what we do is chosen freely, and how much is shaped by
structures we did not build ourselves.

## Objectives

- Analyze how social and economic structures shape which choices people can make, and
  begin to argue about responsibility. _(D09.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

Think of one real choice you made this week — what to eat, how to get somewhere,
whether to study or rest. You made it, but you did not make it in a void. What you
could afford, what your family and school expected, what the law allowed, what your
body could do, and who was around you all narrowed the menu before you chose. Some
people face a wide menu; others a short one. Naming what shapes the menu is the first
step of this unit — because you cannot change a structure you cannot see.

## Materials

- Structure-and-choice map
- Justice journal
- Example slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the structure-and-choice map; prepare the example slips.
- Retrieval: from Grade 10 (D09.S1.10.01), applying ethical reasoning to a technology
  or environment question; from Unit 3 this year (D02), empathy for distant others.
  Today we add the word *structure*.
- Prepare a plain definition: a **structure** is a stable pattern — a law, a market, a
  family form, a school — that makes some choices easy and others hard (worked
  example first: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **we choose, but we
choose inside structures — rules, resources, and roles — that shape the menu of
options (concept, held as description, not as a claim that choice is an illusion).**
Then the egalitarian turn: **those structures distribute resources unevenly, so the
menu is not the same length for everyone (UNDP Human Development Report, S-283).** Keep
the two ideas held together — agency *and* structure — rather than collapsing into
"everything is determined" or "everything is free choice." Both extremes are false and
both are tempting at this age; hold the tension as the learning.

The **ethics** lens: who is responsible for an outcome when choice is shaped is
exactly the open question we carry into Lesson 2. The **egalitarianism** lens: notice
whose menu is longer and whose is shorter. The **global** lens: structures differ from
place to place (a farm's rules, a city's market, a small island's transport) — none is
the default. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing the *choices I can
imagine* from the *choices actually open to me* is an act of careful description, not
opinion. Preview: Lesson 2 takes up responsibility head-on.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Grade 10: when you reasoned about a technology or
   environment question, what did you weigh — consequences, duties, or virtues? Today
   we add a new lens: **structure**.
2. **Meet "structure" (10 min).** A structure is a stable pattern that makes some
   choices easy and others hard. Work a model example first (S-011): a teenager in a
   small village and a teenager in a large city both want to keep learning. What
   rules, resources, and roles shape how each one *actually* can? Name the three:
   **rules** (laws, customs), **resources** (money, time, transport, health), and
   **roles** (student, worker, caregiver, eldest sibling).
3. **Sort the slips (12 min).** With a partner, sort each everyday moment by how much
   structure shapes it: "mostly my free choice," "mostly shaped by structure," or
   "both." Argue the borderline ones and be ready to say why.
4. **Argue an edge case (8 min).** Is *who you can be friends with* shaped by
   structure? Is *whether you go hungry*? Choose one, argue, then agree on a place.
5. **Draw the map (12 min).** In your justice journal, draw the wide fan of choices
   you can *imagine*, then the narrower set actually open to you — and label the
   rules, resources, and roles that narrow it. Write one sentence on which force
   shapes your choices most.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "I chose it" is not the whole story
   — and what is missing.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest slips and name the three forces with one word
  each, leaving the edge cases aside.
- **Extension:** Investigate one structure (e.g., schooling, transport, or land
  ownership) in two different places and compare how it widens or narrows choices.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name rules, resources, and roles, sort
  examples with reasons, and draw the gap between imagined and actual choices?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed structure-and-choice map with the
  written reflection, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about one choice they wanted to make but could not, and what
structure — a rule, a cost, a role — stood in the way. Write one sentence about what
they told you and one about what surprised you.
