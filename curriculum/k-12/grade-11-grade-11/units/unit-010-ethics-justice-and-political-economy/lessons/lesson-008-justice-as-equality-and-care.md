# Lesson 8 — Justice as Equality and Care

## Summary

Learners evaluate two further theories of justice: **equality** (fair shares, with the
question of whether equal means identical) and **care** (meeting needs and honoring
relationships). They see where equal treatment and meeting needs pull apart, meet the
idea that justice may be about relationships and dignity rather than distribution
alone, and weigh the strengths and limits of each lens against the freedom lens from
Lesson 7.

## Objectives

- Evaluate equality-centered and care-centered theories of justice. _(D09.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

Two children are given the same sandwich. One is full; the other has not eaten all
day. Equal — but is it just? A classroom gives every student the same book; one
student cannot read it without help. Equal — but is it just? Equality says "the same
for everyone." Care says "to each what they need." These two answers usually point the
same way, but sometimes they pull apart — and when they do, we are forced to ask what
justice is really about: identical shares, or people whose needs and relationships are
actually met?

## Materials

- Equality-and-care organizer
- Justice journal
- Scenario slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the two-panel organizer; prepare the scenario slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 7, the freedom lens and its limits. Today we add two rival
  answers: equality and care.
- Prepare plain summaries: equality/egalitarian justice — fair shares, with the debate
  over whether equal means identical or proportional (SEP Distributive Justice,
  S-388); care ethics — justice as meeting needs and honoring relationships and
  dignity, rooted in the experience of caregiving.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **equality and care
are two more theories of justice, and they can pull apart from each other and from
freedom — equality asks "are shares fair?" and care asks "is each person met?"
(SEP Distributive Justice, S-388; care ethics as a distinct tradition).** The
methodological point: **these are lenses to evaluate, not answers to adopt.** Note
carefully: care ethics names a tradition associated with feminist moral philosophy
(Gilligan, Noddings) and with caregiving experience, and it is presented as one
serious account among several — not as a "women's" or "soft" alternative, but as a
full theory of what justice owes.

The **ethics** lens: each theory is a claim about what people are owed. The
**egalitarianism** lens: the equality-vs-need tension *is* the egalitarian question —
notice that "the same" can quietly favor the already-advantaged. The **global** lens:
different societies have weighed equality and need differently in distributing land,
water, schooling, and medicine; none is the default. The **critical-thinking** lens:
distinguishing "what the theory *says*" (evidence) from "what I think is *just*"
(value). Preview: Lesson 9 puts all three lenses — freedom, equality, care — on one
current issue.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 7, what was the freedom lens's limit? Today we meet
   two answers to that limit.
2. **Meet the two lenses (12 min).** **Equality** — justice as fair shares, with the
   open question of whether equal means identical or proportional (S-388). **Care** —
   justice as meeting needs and honoring relationships and dignity, not distribution
   alone.
3. **Sort the slips (12 min).** Place each scenario in the "equal shares" panel, the
   "to each what they need" panel, or both. Argue the cases where the two pull apart.
4. **Argue an edge case (10 min).** Is giving everyone "the same" sometimes *unfair*?
   Take the classroom-support or the ramp case and argue, then agree on a placement.
5. **Weigh all three (8 min).** In your justice journal, line up the three lenses —
   freedom, equality, care — and write one thing each explains that the others miss.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "equal" and "just" are not the same
   word — and what care adds that equality alone cannot.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest slips and state each lens in one sentence.
- **Extension:** Argue whether care can be a full *theory of justice* or only a
  virtue, using a concrete case and conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner state each lens, sort cases where they
  pull apart, and name what each lens explains that the others miss?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The three-lens comparison written in the justice
  journal, opened as part of the unit's theories-of-justice work.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home whether sharing should always be equal, or by need. Write one
sentence on their answer and one on a time they saw the two pull apart.

## Resources

- On distributive justice and the equal-shares debate: SEP, Distributive Justice

  ethics tradition as discussed in SEP virtue ethics and related entries (S-458).
- On worked examples and guided practice for analysis: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark
  (2006) (S-011).
