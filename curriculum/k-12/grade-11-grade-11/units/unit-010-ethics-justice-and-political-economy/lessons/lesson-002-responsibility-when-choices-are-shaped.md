# Lesson 2 — Responsibility When Choices Are Shaped

## Summary

Learners take the structure-shapes-choice insight from Lesson 1 and turn it into an
argument about **responsibility**. They weigh **individual responsibility** against
**structural responsibility**, meet the idea of **moral luck** (being credited or
blamed for things not fully in our control), and defend a reasoned position on where
responsibility falls — holding the question open rather than settling it.

## Objectives

- Analyze how social and economic structures shape which choices people can make, and
  argue about responsibility. _(D09.S1.11.01)_

## Connection

A worker takes a job with long hours and low pay because it is the only work in town.
A driver without insurance causes an accident after missing a payment they could not
afford. A student fails a test for which they had no quiet place to study. In each
case, someone *chose* — and something *shaped* the choice. When we ask "who is
responsible?", we are really asking two questions at once: what did this person do,
and what did the world around them set in motion? Most real answers sit in the middle,
and arguing well means holding both questions at once.

## Materials

- Responsibility organizer
- Justice journal
- Case slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the responsibility organizer; prepare the case slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 1, the three forces (rules, resources, roles) and the gap
  between imagined and actual choices.
- Prepare a plain definition of **moral luck**: we are credited or blamed for things
  that were partly outside our control — a useful concept, not a verdict on anyone
  (worked example first: S-011).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **responsibility has
(at least) two seats — the person who chose and the structure that shaped the choice —
and mature moral argument refuses to reduce a case to only one (a conceptual
distinction, not a claim about any specific case).** The egalitarian turn: **the
question "who is responsible?" is not neutral; it is often used to blame the poor for
poverty or to excuse the powerful. Naming both seats is a fairness move (UNDP,
S-283).** Hold the question open: the lesson's point is not to reach one answer but to
argue well and notice what each answer assumes.

The **ethics** lens: responsibility is the heart of moral judgment — who answers for
what. The **egalitarianism** lens: watch where "responsibility" talk lands — does it
fall harder on people with shorter menus? The **global** lens: a far-away consumer and
a far-away worker are linked by a supply chain neither fully controls (Amnesty cobalt
report, S-398). The **critical-thinking** lens: test each argument for the hidden
assumption that the chooser was free. Distinguish evidence from value: *what happened*
is evidence; *who should answer for it* is a value judgment.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What are the three forces from Lesson 1 — rules, resources,
   and roles? Give one example of each shaping a choice.
2. **Meet moral luck (10 min).** Work a model case (S-011): two drivers both look away
   for a second; one hits a child, the other does not. The act is the same; the
   outcome differs. Are they equally to blame? Introduce **moral luck**: what we are
   credited or blamed for is partly shaped by what we did not control.
3. **Argue the cases (15 min).** In pairs, place each case slip on a continuum from
   "the person is responsible" to "the structure is responsible," with "both" in the
   middle. For each, name *what the person chose* and *what shaped the choice*.
4. **Weigh the two seats (10 min).** For one case, write an argument for the person's
   responsibility *and* an argument for the structure's — then say where you would
   place the line, and why.
5. **Watch the power (8 min).** Who in your case has more power to change the
   structure? Who is more likely to be blamed? Notice how the word "responsible"
   moves between them.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "who is responsible?" is not a
   question with one fixed answer — and what you would need to know to answer it well.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Argue only the clearest case and use the two-column organizer to list
  "person" and "structure" facts before choosing.
- **Extension:** Analyze the cobalt supply chain (S-398) and argue whether a consumer
  far down the chain bears responsibility, conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name both seats of responsibility,
  place a case on the continuum with reasons, and concede a counterargument?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The two-sided written argument with a stated line and
  reason, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they were blamed (or credited) for something partly
outside their control. Write one sentence on what they said and one on whether you
think the blame or credit was fair.
