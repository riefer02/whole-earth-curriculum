# Lesson 13 — One Conflict, Many Sides

## Summary

Learners analyze a real conflict from multiple perspectives — a land-and-water
dispute between farmers and herders — and propose a just and peaceful path forward.
They map each party's needs and interests, distinguish needs from positions, meet
restorative justice as a tradition of repair rather than revenge, and craft a path
that names every party's needs and the obstacles it must overcome.

## Objectives

- Analyze a real or historical conflict from multiple perspectives and propose a just
  and peaceful path forward. _(D09.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Most conflicts look simple from one side and impossible from none. The farmer sees
the herders' animals trampling the crops; the herders see the fences closing off the
water their animals have always needed; the official sees two groups who will not
talk; the neighbor sees a dispute that is now everyone's danger. Each side is partly
right and partly blind. Analyzing a conflict honestly means holding all the stories at
once — and then asking not "who wins?" but "what would be both just *and* peaceful for
everyone here?"

## Materials

- Multi-perspective conflict map
- Justice journal
- Conflict case cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the conflict map; prepare the case cards (a neutral outline of the
  dispute, with no preselected villain).
- Retrieval: from Lesson 12, what solidarity requires of citizens. Today we test it in
  the hardest place — conflict.
- Prepare the concepts: **needs** (what a party cannot do without — water, safety,
  a livelihood) vs **positions** (what a party demands); **restorative justice**
  (repairing harm and relationships rather than only punishing, S-161, S-022).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a conflict is best
analyzed from all its sides at once — each party has needs and interests, and the
distinction between *needs* and *positions* is the key that unlocks a path forward
(conceptual tool, modeled with a worked example, S-011).** Then the constructive turn:
**a just and peaceful path is one that names every party's needs and repairs harm,
drawing on restorative justice as a real tradition (Zehr, S-161; IIRP, S-022).** The
case is deliberately neutral and non-sensational; the skill is analysis and proposal,
not taking sides or assigning blame.

The **ethics** lens: "what is just here?" is the question, opened not closed. The
**egalitarianism** lens: a just path must not silently favor the stronger party —
ask who has more power and whose needs are heard. The **global** lens: this kind of
land-and-water dispute recurs across the world's drylands and farmlands; it is no one
region's problem and no one region's fault. The **critical-thinking** lens:
distinguishing the *facts* of the case (evidence) from the *judgment* of what is just
(value), and separating needs (shared ground) from positions (the obstacle). Preview:
Lesson 14 closes the unit by evaluating nonviolence and peacekeeping as the
instruments of a just peace.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 12, what does solidarity require of a citizen?
   Today: what happens when solidarity breaks — a conflict — and how do we find a way
   back?
2. **Meet the case (8 min).** Read the neutral outline: farmers and herders dispute
   land and water in a season of scarcity. Note who is involved, what each wants, and
   what is at stake.
3. **Map the sides (12 min).** Fill the conflict map: for each party — farmer, herder,
   official, neighbor — write their **needs** (what they cannot do without) and their
   **positions** (what they demand). Notice where needs overlap even when positions
   clash.
4. **Distinguish needs from positions (10 min).** With a partner, find one *need* the
   farmer and herder actually share (water, safety, a future) and one *position* that
   blocks them from seeing it.
5. **Propose a path (12 min).** In your justice journal, write a path forward that is
   both just and peaceful: what would be repaired, who would be heard, what rule or
   agreement could hold, and one obstacle it must overcome. (Consider repair, not only
   punishment — S-161, S-022.)
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "who wins?" is the wrong question —
   and what the right one is.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Map two parties' needs and positions and write a one-sentence path
  forward.
- **Extension:** Weigh whether a "just" path and a "peaceful" path ever conflict, and
  argue what to do when they do, conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner map every party's needs and positions,
  separate needs from positions, and propose a just and peaceful path with one
  obstacle?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed conflict map and written path — the
  unit's multi-perspective conflict analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a disagreement they once saw settled peacefully. Write one
sentence on what made it peaceful and one on whether it was also fair.
