# Lesson 4 — What Makes a Society Good?

## Summary

Learners move from the good **life** to the good **society**, comparing four visions:
the Haudenosaunee **Great Law of Peace** (consensus and the long view), the **African
Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights** (collective rights and duties), Confucian
**harmony** through roles and ritual, and the Buddhist ideal of a ruler who serves the
**welfare of all beings**. They evaluate how each decides, whom it includes, and what
holds it together — held side by side, none the default.

## Objectives

- Evaluate competing accounts of what a good society means, drawing on more than one
  cultural tradition. _(D09.S1.11.02)_

## Connection

A good *society* is harder to picture than a good *life*, but you already carry a
picture of it: the kind of world you hope to grow old in, where people are treated a
certain way and decisions are made a certain way. Is it a world of peaceful agreement,
of guaranteed rights, of harmony between roles, or of care for everyone's
well-being? Different peoples have built their shared life on different answers. This
lesson sets four of those answers beside each other — so you can see, and question,
the picture you have been carrying.

## Materials

- Good-society comparison chart
- Justice journal
- Four vision cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the comparison chart; prepare the four vision cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, four accounts of the good life. Today we scale up to the
  good society.
- Prepare plain descriptions: the Great Law of Peace (Haudenosaunee Confederacy —
  decisions by council, with care for future generations); the African Charter (adds
  peoples' and collective rights and individual *duties* to the human-rights idea);
  Confucian harmony (a society ordered by reciprocal duties in family and community);
  Buddhist welfare (the ideal of a ruler who serves all beings' well-being).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **visions of a good
society are real, old, and diverse — consensus and the long view (Haudenosaunee Great
Law of Peace, S-142), collective rights with duties (African Charter / Banjul
Charter, S-465), harmony through roles (Confucius, S-468), and welfare for all
(Buddhist kingship ideal).** The methodological point: **these are visions to evaluate
by *who they include* and *how they decide* — the egalitarian question — not to rank by
which is "most advanced."** Guard against treating any one (especially a Western one)
as the standard; the lesson deliberately centers non-Western and Indigenous visions.

The **ethics** lens: each vision is a claim about how people *ought* to live together.
The **egalitarianism** lens: ask of every vision — whom does it include, whom does it
leave out, and who actually gets to decide? The **global** lens: the four are drawn
from North America, Africa, and Asia — three continents, many centuries, all living
traditions. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing "what a tradition *says*"
(evidence) from "what I think is *just*" (value). Preview: Lesson 5 turns from
visions to the machinery of justice, power, and law.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** What four accounts of the good life did you meet in Lesson 3?
   Now: what would a good *society* built on each look like?
2. **Meet four visions (15 min).** Read the four vision cards. **Consensus and peace**
   — the Haudenosaunee Confederacy decides by council, holding the good of the seventh
   generation in view (S-142). **Rights and duties** — the African Charter pairs
   people's and collective rights with each person's duties to family and community
   (S-465). **Harmony and roles** — Confucian society orders itself through reciprocal
   duties in family and community (S-468). **Welfare for all** — the Buddhist ideal of
   a ruler whose first duty is the well-being of all beings.
3. **Fill the chart (12 min).** For each vision, write *how it decides*, *whom it
   includes*, and *what holds it together*.
4. **Ask the fairness question (10 min).** With a partner, for each vision ask: who
   gets a voice, and who might be left out? Which vision most clearly protects the
   weakest members?
5. **Evaluate (8 min).** In your justice journal, write which vision you find most
   just, and one limit or risk of that same vision — no vision is perfect.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why there is no single "good society,"
   and what that means for how we build one together.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Work with two visions and name how each decides and whom it includes.
- **Extension:** Read the African Charter's addition of *duties* and argue whether
  pairing rights with duties strengthens or weakens the idea of rights.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the four visions, state how each
  decides and whom it includes, and identify a limit of each?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed comparison chart with the "most just /
  its limit" reflection, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home what their picture of a good society is — who decides, and who is
cared for. Write one sentence on their answer and one on how it compares to the four
visions.
