# Lesson 6 — Changing an Unjust Law

## Summary

Learners argue for how an unjust law or system should be changed, using real cases
from three continents: the **1930 Salt March** and **1881 Parihaka**, South Africa's
**1952 Defiance Campaign**, the **1955–56 Montgomery bus boycott**, and the **1966
Wave Hill walk-off**. They map each onto a path of **notice → organize → act →
change**, weigh nonviolent action against working only within the law, and defend a
reasoned position.

## Objectives

- Analyze the relationship between justice, power, and law, and argue for how an
  unjust law or system should be changed. _(D09.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

An unjust law is not an abstract idea; it is a rule you can *feel* — a pass people
must carry, a bus seat they must not take, a wage they must accept, a land they may
not return to. People in every part of the world have faced such rules and asked the
same question you are asking now: *how do we change it?* Some worked only through the
courts and the ballot; others broke the law openly and accepted the consequences,
forcing the rest of society to see the law's unfairness. There is no single right way —
but there are real paths, and this lesson walks them.

## Materials

- Change-path organizer
- Justice journal
- Case cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the change-path organizer; prepare the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 5, the justice-power-law diagram and where they come apart.
  Today we ask *how to change* an unjust law.
- Prepare the cases accurately: the Salt March (India, 1930, Gandhi's nonviolent
  march to make salt in defiance of the salt tax, S-390); Parihaka (Aotearoa New
  Zealand, 1881, peaceful village resistance under Te Whiti-o-Rongomai and Tohu
  Kākahi, S-144); the Defiance Campaign (South Africa, 1952, deliberate nonviolent
  defiance of unjust pass laws, S-146); the Montgomery bus boycott (USA, 1955–56,
  S-324); the Wave Hill walk-off (Australia, 1966, Gurindji stockworkers led by
  Vincent Lingiari, S-206).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **changing an unjust
law or system is a path with identifiable steps — notice the harm, organize with
others, act (often nonviolently), and press for change — illustrated by documented
cases (Salt March S-390; Parihaka S-144; Defiance Campaign S-146; Montgomery S-324;
Wave Hill S-206).** The methodological point: **these are cases to *learn the steps
from*, not a single template to copy — each people chose its own way, and each paid
real costs.** Hold the ethical question open: is it right to break an unjust law
openly? Let learners argue both sides; do not answer for them.

The **ethics** lens: the question of civil disobedience — when breaking a law is the
right thing to do — is a live moral question, opened not closed. The **egalitarianism**
lens: in every case, the law protected some and burdened others; ask who benefited and
who paid. The **global** lens: the five cases span India, Aotearoa, South Africa, the
USA, and Australia — many peoples, one recurring human problem, no single nation's
triumph. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing the *facts* of each case
(evidence) from the *judgment* of which strategy was right (value). Preview: Lesson 7
turns to the theories of justice behind these struggles.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 5: where do law and justice come apart? Give one
   example. Today: how do people close that gap?
2. **Meet the path (8 min).** Change has steps: **notice** the harm, **organize** with
   others, **act** — through courts, votes, protest, or nonviolent law-breaking — and
   press for **change**. No step is guaranteed; the path is climbed, not given.
3. **Jigsaw the cases (15 min).** Each small group takes one case card and maps it
   onto the four steps: what was noticed? how did people organize? what did they do?
   what changed? Teach your case to the class.
4. **Compare strategies (10 min).** Which cases worked *within* the law, and which
   *broke* it openly? What did the law-breakers accept in return (arrest, risk)?
5. **Argue the ethics (8 min).** With a partner, argue: is it right to break an unjust
   law openly and accept the penalty? State a reason for and a reason against, then
   your own position.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why changing an unjust law takes both
   courage *and* company — and what makes a change last.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Retell one case as a story and mark only the "notice" and "act" steps.
- **Extension:** Compare two cases and argue which strategy fit its situation better,
  naming what each risked and gained.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner map a case onto the four steps, compare
  legal and nonviolent strategies, and defend a position on civil disobedience?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The mapped case with the two-sided argument on
  law-breaking, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home whether they have ever seen a rule changed — at work, in a
community, or in a country. Write one sentence on what changed and one on how it was
changed.
