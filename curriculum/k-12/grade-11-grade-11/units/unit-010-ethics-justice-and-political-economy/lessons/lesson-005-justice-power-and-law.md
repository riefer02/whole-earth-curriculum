# Lesson 5 — Justice, Power, and Law

## Summary

Learners map the relationship between **justice, power, and law** — three things that
usually travel together but can come apart. They see that law is power made into
rules, that justice is the standard by which law and power are judged, and that power
can serve justice or defy it. They meet **structural violence** — harm built into
systems — and begin the argument about how to change an unjust law or system.

## Objectives

- Analyze the relationship between justice, power, and law. _(D09.S2.11.01)_

## Connection

Every school, every home, and every country runs on some mix of three things: *power*
(who can make others do things), *law* (the written and unwritten rules), and
*justice* (whether the rules and the power are fair). Usually the three overlap — a
fair rule, backed by legitimate power, gives us justice. But they come apart more
often than we like: a law can be unjust, power can serve itself instead of the law,
and justice can demand something the law has not yet written down. Seeing how the
three fit — and where they split — is how you learn to tell "this is the law" from
"this is right."

## Materials

- Justice-power-law diagram
- Justice journal
- Three circles + example slips (low-tech: paper/ground)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the three-circle diagram; prepare the example slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 4, visions of a good society. Today we look at the *machinery*
  — how power and law actually work, and how justice judges them.
- Prepare plain definitions: **power** (the capacity to make things happen, including
  over others); **law** (rules with authority and, usually, enforcement); **justice**
  (the standard of fairness by which power and law are judged).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **justice, power, and
law are three distinct things that usually overlap but can come apart — and the
overlaps are where the interesting trouble is.** Then the critical turn: **law is not
the same as justice; power is not the same as right. A law can be unjust, and power
can serve itself (a conceptual point, illustrated with cases, not a claim about any
specific state).** Introduce **structural violence** — harm built into systems rather
than done by one hand (Galtung, S-462) — and note that power is distributed unevenly
across societies (V-Dem, S-461), without ranking any society above another.

The **ethics** lens: "is this law just?" is the moral question at the center. The
**egalitarianism** lens: ask of every law and power — who does it serve, and who does
it leave out? The **global** lens: different societies organize the three differently;
none is the default. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing "what the law
*is*" (fact) from "what it *ought* to be" (value). Preview: Lesson 6 argues for
changing an unjust law.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 4, what holds a good society together? Today: what
   actually *makes* rules stick — and what makes them *fair*?
2. **Meet the three (10 min).** Define **power**, **law**, and **justice**. Note the
   usual picture: fair rules + legitimate power = justice. Then ask where the three
   can come apart.
3. **Sort the slips (15 min).** Place each example into the three-circle diagram:
   "justice alone," "power alone," "law alone," or an overlap. Argue the hard ones:
   is a protest against an unjust law *lawful*? *just*? *powerful*?
4. **Label the overlaps (10 min).** With a partner, label each overlap: *power + law*
   can be order — or oppression; *justice + law* is law made fair; *power + justice*
   is change for fairness.
5. **Meet structural violence (8 min).** Some harm is not done by one hand but built
   into systems — a rule that quietly keeps some people out (S-462). Name one example
   from the slips and say what makes it *structural*.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "it is the law" does not settle the
   question "is it just?"

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only the clearest slips and label one overlap; leave structural
  violence for later.
- **Extension:** Analyze one real law, past or present, and argue whether it is just,
  naming who it serves and who it leaves out.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish the three, place examples
  in the overlaps with reasons, and explain structural violence in one case?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The labeled three-circle diagram with a written
  example where the three come apart, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a rule they must follow that they think is unfair. Write one
sentence on the rule and one on whether it is a question of power, law, justice — or
all three.
