# Lesson 14 — Nonviolence and Peacekeeping

## Summary

Learners evaluate the ethics of nonviolence and of peacekeeping, arguing about when
and how each can succeed. They meet nonviolent civil resistance through documented
campaigns (the Salt March, the Defiance Campaign, the Montgomery bus boycott, the
Chipko movement) and peacekeeping as an impartial third party standing between sides,
and weigh the strengths and limits of both — including the difference between the
absence of war and a just peace.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the ethics of nonviolence and of peacekeeping, and argue about when and how
  they can succeed. _(D09.S4.11.02)_

## Connection

There are two ways people refuse violence without being passive. One is **nonviolent
resistance**: ordinary people act — march, boycott, refuse to obey an unjust law — and
accept the cost, so that the injustice cannot hide. The other is **peacekeeping**: an
impartial third party stands between two sides so that violence cannot restart while
the two learn to talk. Both are strategies, not magic — they succeed in some
conditions and fall short in others. Judging *when and how* they work is the last, and
one of the hardest, questions of this unit.

## Materials

- Nonviolence-and-peacekeeping organizer
- Justice journal
- Case cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the organizer; prepare the case cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 13, the just and peaceful path you proposed. Today we examine
  the *instruments* that walk it.
- Prepare the cases accurately: the Salt March (S-390); the Defiance Campaign (S-146);
  the Montgomery bus boycott (S-324); the Chipko movement (S-328); Gandhi's satyagraha
  ("truth-force," nonviolent insistence on truth, S-205). Prepare peacekeeping plainly:
  a third party, invited and impartial, that monitors, separates, and protects while
  parties negotiate — with the honest caveat that it has also failed.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **nonviolence and
peacekeeping are two real strategies for meeting conflict without violence — nonviolent
resistance by ordinary people (Salt March S-390; Defiance Campaign S-146; Montgomery
S-324; Chipko S-328; Gandhi's satyagraha, S-205), and peacekeeping by an impartial
third party — and both are to be evaluated, not idealized.** The honest caveat: **they
succeed under some conditions and have failed in others; and "no war" is not yet
"just peace" (Galtung's positive/negative peace, S-462).** Hold the question open:
when nonviolence is *right* and when it is *enough* is a judgment, not a settled fact.

The **ethics** lens: the morality of refusing violence — and of standing between armed
sides — is the lesson's core. The **egalitarianism** lens: ask of nonviolence whether
it can work for the powerless, and of peacekeeping whether it truly protects the weak
or only freezes an unfair peace. The **global** lens: the campaigns span India, South
Africa, the USA, and India again (Chipko) — nonviolence is no single nation's
tradition. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing the *fact* that a campaign
succeeded (evidence) from the *value* that nonviolence is always right (a value to
argue). This lesson closes the unit; tie it back to Lesson 1's question about
structure and choice — nonviolence is one way people change structures.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 13, what path forward did you propose — and what
   obstacle stood in its way? Today: what *instruments* walk that path?
2. **Meet nonviolent resistance (12 min).** Read the case cards. In each, ordinary
   people acted without violence — marched (S-390), defied unjust laws (S-146),
   boycotted (S-324), embraced trees to protect a forest (S-328) — and accepted the
   cost. Gandhi called this **satyagraha**, "truth-force" (S-205).
3. **Meet peacekeeping (8 min).** Peacekeeping is different: a third party, invited and
   impartial, stands between sides — monitoring, separating, protecting — while the
   two negotiate. Its aim is to stop violence so a just peace can be built.
4. **Weigh success and failure (12 min).** With a partner, place each strategy on a
   "when it succeeds / when it falls short" line. Name one *condition* that helps each
   (e.g., nonviolence needs discipline and numbers; peacekeeping needs consent and
   resources).
5. **Distinguish peace from just peace (8 min).** No war is not yet justice. Galtung
   calls the mere absence of fighting "negative peace," and a peace with justice
   "positive peace" (S-462). Which do the cases you read move toward?
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why nonviolence is not weakness — and
   why peacekeeping is not the same as peace.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Name one nonviolent action and one thing peacekeeping does, leaving the
  conditions debate aside.
- **Extension:** Argue whether nonviolence can work against a ruler who answers
  protest with force, using a real case and conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner describe nonviolence and peacekeeping,
  name a condition for each's success and failure, and distinguish negative from
  positive peace?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed organizer with the written judgment —
  the unit's ethics-of-nonviolence evaluation.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a conflict they saw resolved without violence — by people
standing together, or by someone stepping between the sides. Write one sentence on
what happened and one on whether it lasted.
