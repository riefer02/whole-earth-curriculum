# Lesson 12 — Solidarity Across Distance

## Summary

Learners evaluate **solidarity** — acting with others across difference and distance —
and argue for what it requires of citizens. They distinguish solidarity from mere
sympathy, meet real practices of mutual aid and humanitarian solidarity from around
the world, and defend a position on why (and how far) a citizen's care should extend
to people unlike and far from themselves.

## Objectives

- Evaluate solidarity (acting with others across difference and distance) and argue
  for what it requires of citizens. _(D09.S3.11.02)_

## Connection

It is easy to act with people who look like us, live near us, and share our lives. It
is harder to act with a stranger across a border, an ocean, or a difference we do not
understand — harder, and often exactly where it matters most. **Solidarity** is not
feeling sorry for someone far away; it is *acting with* them, as if their cause were
our own, even when we will never meet. This lesson asks what that actually costs a
citizen, and why the answer has real consequences for how we live together in one
shared world.

## Materials

- Solidarity organizer
- Justice journal
- Example cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the bridge organizer; prepare the example cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 11, the supply chain that links a far-away worker to a
  nearby buyer — solidarity is the human bond that makes that coordination possible.
- Prepare the examples: the Red Cross/Red Crescent movement (neutral, voluntary
  humanitarian aid in crises, S-329); the Green Belt Movement (a grassroots movement
  that spread across African countries and planted over thirty million trees, S-059);
  mutual-aid traditions and worker alliances across borders.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **solidarity is
acting with others across difference and distance — not feeling for them, but acting
with them — and it is a practiced, documented thing, not a slogan (Red Cross/Red
Crescent, S-329; Green Belt Movement, S-059).** The argument at the center: **what
solidarity requires of citizens — time, risk, sharing power, listening, refusing to
look away — and how far that requirement extends is a live question, held open.** Do
not answer it for them; the skill is arguing a position and conceding a
counterargument.

The **ethics** lens: what we owe strangers is a moral question, opened not closed.
The **egalitarianism** lens: solidarity is the answer to the who-benefits/who-is-left-
out questions of the last two lessons — it refuses to accept that distance or
difference ends our responsibility. The **global** lens: the examples span continents;
solidarity is planetary by nature. The **community** lens: solidarity begins near
home (a mutual-aid network, a neighbor) and extends outward — it is not either/or.
The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing the *fact* that solidarity happens
(evidence) from the *value* that it should (commitment). Preview: Lesson 13 turns to
conflict — the test where solidarity and justice are hardest.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 11, what links a far-away worker to a nearby
   buyer? Today: what makes that link a bond of *obligation*, not just a chain of
   goods?
2. **Distinguish solidarity from sympathy (8 min).** Sympathy feels *for* someone.
   Solidarity acts *with* them. Name the difference with one example each.
3. **Meet real solidarity (12 min).** Read the example cards. The Red Cross/Red
   Crescent gives neutral, voluntary aid in crises (S-329). The Green Belt Movement
   grew from one person's planting to over thirty million trees across African
   countries (S-059). Notice: in each, people acted with strangers across distance.
4. **Argue the requirement (12 min).** With a partner, argue: what does solidarity
   require of a citizen — time, money, risk, listening, sharing power? And how far
   should it extend — to the neighbor, the stranger, the future generation?
5. **Write it (10 min).** In your justice journal, write one thing solidarity requires
   of *you*, one thing that makes it hard, and one counterargument to your own
   position that you can honestly concede.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why solidarity with people unlike
   ourselves is not a luxury — and what it would ask of you this year.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Distinguish sympathy from solidarity and name one real example of each.
- **Extension:** Argue whether we owe solidarity to distant strangers when our own
   community is struggling, conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish solidarity from sympathy,
  name what it requires of citizens, and defend a position with a conceded
  counterargument?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The written argument on what solidarity requires —
  the unit's solidarity evaluation.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they helped a stranger, or a stranger helped them.
Write one sentence on what happened and one on what it cost them.
