# Lesson 7 — Justice as Freedom

## Summary

Learners evaluate a freedom-centered theory of justice: that a just society protects
**liberty**, and that freedom means having real **capabilities** — actual chances to
learn, be healthy, participate, and choose work — not merely the absence of
interference. They meet liberal justice (Rawls) and the capabilities approach (Sen),
test the lens against scenarios where someone is "free on paper" but not in practice,
and weigh its strengths and limits.

## Objectives

- Evaluate a freedom-centered theory of justice. _(D09.S2.11.02)_

## Connection

"Freedom" is a word everyone reaches for, but it hides two different ideas. *Freedom
from* is the absence of interference — no one stops you. *Freedom to* is the presence
of real chances — you actually can read, travel, work, be well. A person can be "free
from" a great deal and still be unable to do much, if they lack money, health, or
education. A freedom-centered theory of justice says: a just society is measured by
whether people are *really able* to do and be the things that make a life — not just
by whether no one is stopping them. That distinction changes everything.

## Materials

- Freedom-lens organizer
- Justice journal
- Scenario slips (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the freedom-lens organizer; prepare the scenario slips.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 6, how people changed unjust laws — struggles often fought in
  the name of freedom. Today we ask what *freedom* means as a theory of justice.
- Prepare plain summaries: Rawls's liberal justice — basic liberties for all, and
  inequalities allowed only if they help the least advantaged (S-459); Sen's
  capabilities approach — development and justice as what people can *actually do and
  be* (S-460).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a freedom-centered
theory of justice has two readings — "freedom from" (no interference) and "freedom
to" (real capability) — and the capability reading is what makes freedom a test of
justice rather than a slogan (Sen, S-460; Rawls's liberal account, S-459).** The
methodological point: **this is one theory among several, to be evaluated, not the
answer.** The distinction between "freedom from" and "freedom to" is a conceptual tool,
not a political verdict; present both readings fairly.

The **ethics** lens: what people are owed — freedom — is a moral claim, opened not
closed. The **egalitarianism** lens: the capability test exposes where "equal freedom"
is not equal in practice, because resources and health are uneven (UNDP, S-283). The
**global** lens: capability thinking arose partly from comparing development across
countries (Sen); no one society is the default model of freedom. The
**critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing "what the theory *says*" (evidence) from
"what I think a just society *owes*" (value). Preview: Lesson 8 adds equality and
care as rival answers.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 6, why did people break unjust laws? Many said:
   "for freedom." Today: what *is* the freedom justice demands?
2. **Meet the two freedoms (10 min).** **Freedom from** = no one interferes. **Freedom
   to** = real chances to do and be. Work a model case (S-011): a person is "free to
   travel" but has no money for the fare — free *from*, but not free *to*.
3. **Meet the theories (12 min).** Rawls: a just society secures basic liberties for
   all and lets inequalities stand only when they help the least advantaged (S-459).
   Sen: justice is what people are *actually able* to do and be — their capabilities
   (S-460).
4. **Test the lens (12 min).** For each scenario slip, ask: is this person free *from*
   or free *to*? What capability is missing? What would a just society owe them?
5. **Weigh the limits (8 min).** With a partner, name one thing the freedom lens
   explains well and one thing it may miss. (Clue: can freedom alone capture care for
   the vulnerable?)
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "you are free" can be true on paper
   and false in practice — and what justice would add.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Distinguish only "freedom from" and "freedom to" with clear examples,
  leaving the theories aside.
- **Extension:** Apply Rawls's difference principle to one current policy and argue
  whether it meets the test.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner distinguish the two freedoms, apply the
  capability test to a scenario, and name a limit of the freedom lens?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed freedom-lens diagram with the scenario
  judgment and limit, opened in the justice journal.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a time they were "free from" something but still could not
do what they wanted. Write one sentence on their story and one on what was missing.
