# Lesson 10 — A Problem We Can't Solve Alone

## Summary

Learners analyze a global collective-action problem: a shared resource — the climate,
the air, the oceans — that each person and nation has a reason to use freely and a
reason to protect, but that can be used up when everyone acts alone. They meet the
**free-rider** temptation and the **coordination** that answers it, ground the idea in
real emissions data (who emits, who suffers), and reason about what coordination
across nations requires.

## Objectives

- Analyze a global collective-action problem, such as climate change, and the
  coordination needed to solve it. _(D09.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

A pond is shared by many families. Each family, alone, has a good reason to take a
little extra water for its own fields — and each family's "a little extra" is small.
But if every family reasons the same way, the pond runs dry, and everyone loses. This
is a **collective-action problem**: what is sensible for each person alone is ruinous
for everyone together. The climate, the air, and the oceans are ponds on a planetary
scale. Solving them cannot be done by good intentions alone; it needs *coordination* —
agreements, rules, and institutions that change what each actor finds sensible.

## Materials

- Collective-action organizer
- Justice journal
- Commons game tokens + shared pond (low-tech)

## Preparation

- Prepare the commons game (a shared pool of tokens; a rule introduced halfway).
- Copy or draw the organizer.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 9, applying theories of justice to a current issue. Today we
  scale up to problems only the whole world can solve.
- Prepare the facts (general data authorities): historical emitters and present
  emitters differ; the places that emitted most over two centuries are often not the
  places that suffer worst today (Our World in Data, S-006; IPCC, S-005).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **a collective-action
problem is one where each actor's reasonable self-interested choice produces a
collective ruin — the commons is used up — and the answer is coordination (rules and
institutions), not just virtue.** Ground it: **climate change is a real collective-
action problem, and who emitted most historically is not the same as who suffers most
today (Our World in Data, S-006; IPCC, S-005) — so coordination raises a fairness
question, not just a technical one.** The commons game is the worked example; play it
before the abstraction (S-011).

The **ethics** lens: what each of us owes the shared world is a moral question. The
**egalitarianism** lens: the who-emits / who-suffers gap is the fairness heart of the
climate problem — coordination that ignores it is not just. The **global** lens: the
commons is planetary; no single nation's action, however large, is enough alone. The
**environment** lens: the "pond" is the real biosphere — atmosphere, ocean, forest,
soil. The **critical-thinking** lens: distinguishing the *facts* (who emits, who
suffers) from the *values* (what each owes). Preview: Lesson 11 applies the same frame
to workers and supply chains.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 9, what did your three-lens verdict leave
   unsettled? Today we meet problems no *individual* — however just — can fix alone.
2. **Play the commons (12 min).** A shared pond of tokens. Round 1: take freely each
   turn. Round 2: a rule is agreed that limits each turn. Compare what happens to the
   pond, and to the group, in each round.
3. **Name the problem (10 min).** What you saw is a **collective-action problem**: the
   sensible choice alone (take more) ruins the shared thing. The temptation to take
   while others protect is **free-riding**. What answered it? A **rule** — coordination.
4. **Scale it up (12 min).** The climate is the same problem at planetary scale. Each
   nation has reason to burn fuel for its own growth; all together we warm the planet.
   Note the fairness gap: the places that emitted most over two centuries are often
   not the places suffering worst now (S-006; S-005).
5. **Design coordination (10 min).** In your organizer, name one shared rule or
   institution that could coordinate nations on climate — and one reason it is hard.
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why "everyone should just do the right
   thing" is not enough — and what coordination adds.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Play the game and name the free-rider temptation; leave the global
  scaling to discussion.
- **Extension:** Read Ostrom's finding that communities can govern commons with their
  own rules (S-416) and argue what it teaches for a *global* commons.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner name the collective-action structure
  (shared resource, free-rider, coordination), ground it in the climate facts, and
  propose a coordination rule with one obstacle?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed collective-action organizer — the
  unit's collective-action-problem analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home about a shared thing (a kitchen, a water source, a budget) that
took coordination to protect. Write one sentence on the rule that worked and one on
what happened without it.

- On communities governing commons with their own rules: Ostrom, Governing the Commons
  (S-416).
