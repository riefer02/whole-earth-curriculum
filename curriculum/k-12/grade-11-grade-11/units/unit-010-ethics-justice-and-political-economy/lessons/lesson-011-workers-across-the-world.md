# Lesson 11 — Workers Across the World

## Summary

Learners analyze labor rights as a global collective-action problem. They trace a
product through its supply chain — mine, factory, ship, shop, home — and ask at each
link who benefits and who bears the cost. They meet the documented reality that some
of the goods we use are made by workers far away whose labor is underpaid or unsafe,
and reason about the coordination (unions, cooperatives, agreements) that could make
work fair across borders.

## Objectives

- Analyze a global collective-action problem, such as labor rights, and the
  coordination needed to solve it. _(D09.S3.11.01)_

## Connection

The phone in your hand, the shirt on your back, the food on your table — each one
arrived through many hands you will never meet. Somewhere upstream, a miner, a
seamster, a farmer, a sailor did the work that made it. When that work is underpaid or
unsafe, the harm is real but invisible to the person downstream who buys the finished
thing. Making work fair across borders is not something any one worker — or any one
buyer — can do alone. It is a coordination problem, exactly like the pond in Lesson 10,
but the shared resource is human dignity.

## Materials

- Supply-chain organizer
- Justice journal
- Chain cards (low-tech: paper)

## Preparation

- Copy or draw the supply-chain organizer; prepare the chain cards.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 10, the collective-action structure (shared resource,
  free-rider, coordination). Today we apply it to labor.
- Prepare the facts: some of the minerals in phones, such as cobalt, are sometimes
  mined in conditions that harm and exploit workers, including children (Amnesty,
  S-398 — hedged with "sometimes"); cooperatives are worker-owned enterprises run on
  one-member-one-vote (ICA, S-486).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The idea to land: **labor rights is a
collective-action problem along a global supply chain — each link's cost and benefit
fall on different people, often far apart, and fairness across borders needs
coordination (unions, cooperatives, and agreements).** Ground it with documented
evidence: **some minerals in phones are sometimes mined in exploitative conditions
(Amnesty cobalt report, S-398 — held with the "sometimes" hedge, and told without
graphic detail); cooperatives are a real, documented alternative form of worker
ownership (ICA, S-486).** Keep the tone factual and hopeful: the lesson names real
harm and pairs it with real agency.

The **ethics** lens: what the far-away worker is owed is a moral question the buyer
cannot simply set aside. The **egalitarianism** lens: the chain concentrates benefit
downstream and cost upstream — naming that is the fairness move. The **global** lens:
the chain crosses borders; the worker and the buyer may share one product and no
common language. The **technology** lens: the product itself (a phone, a battery) is a
technology whose making carries a hidden human cost. The **critical-thinking** lens:
distinguishing the *facts* of the chain (evidence) from the *judgment* of what fair
work requires (value). Preview: Lesson 12 turns from coordination to solidarity — the
human bond that makes coordination possible.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 10, what three parts make a collective-action
   problem? Today: the shared resource is human dignity.
2. **Trace the chain (12 min).** Arrange the chain: **mine → factory → ship → shop →
   home.** At each link, name who *benefits* (money, goods) and who *bears the cost*
   (hours, danger, low pay).
3. **Meet the evidence (10 min).** Some of the minerals in phones, such as cobalt, are
   sometimes mined in conditions that harm and exploit workers, including children
   (S-398). Notice: the harm is far upstream; the buyer downstream rarely sees it.
4. **Name the problem (8 min).** Why can't one worker — or one buyer — fix this alone?
   Because each is one link in a chain they do not control. This is a coordination
   problem.
5. **Design coordination (12 min).** In your organizer, name one way to coordinate
   across the chain — a union, a cooperative, an agreement or label — and one reason
   it is hard to make stick. (Consider: a cooperative is worker-owned, one-member-one-
   vote, S-486.)
6. **Close (4 min).** Say, in your own words, why fair work across borders is a
   *shared* problem — and what one thing you, a buyer, could actually do.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Arrange the chain and name who benefits and who bears cost at two links.
- **Extension:** Compare unions and cooperatives as strategies for fair work, and
  argue which fits a supply-chain problem better, conceding a counterargument.

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self):** Can the learner trace the chain, name where cost and
  benefit fall, and propose a coordination mechanism with one obstacle?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The annotated supply-chain organizer — part of the
  unit's collective-action-problem analysis.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to trace one thing in your house back through its chain of makers.
Write one sentence on what they found and one on what surprised you.
