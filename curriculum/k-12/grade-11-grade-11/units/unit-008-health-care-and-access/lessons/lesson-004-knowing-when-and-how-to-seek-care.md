# Lesson 4 — Knowing When and How to Seek Care

## Summary

Learners build the skill of matching a health need to the right response:
**self-manage** at home, book a **routine** appointment, seek **urgent** care soon, or
call **emergency** help now. They sort scenarios onto an urgency ladder, learn that
emergency numbers are country-specific, and rehearse the help-seeking words —
including for mental-health needs.

## Objectives

- Decide the appropriate level of response to a health need, and describe how to get
  help at each level. _(D03.S4.11.01)_

## Connection

Almost everyone faces the same small decision many times a year: is this a "rest and
water" day, a "book an appointment" week, or a "call for help now" moment? Getting the
match right matters in both directions — treating every sniffle as an emergency wastes
care and frightens people, while calling a serious sign "nothing" delays help. This
lesson is not about becoming a doctor; it is about knowing which door to knock on, and
what to say when you do.

## Materials

- Urgency-ladder sheet
- Scenario cards

## Preparation

- Copy the urgency ladder and scenario cards; prepare a local emergency-number card.
- Retrieval: from Lesson 3, the pathway into care (home → community → clinic →
  hospital). Today we add *when* to use each door.
- Confirm the emergency number(s) that apply locally (e.g., 112, 999, 000, 911, or a
  national number) so learners record the correct one.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The ideas to land: **health needs sit
on an urgency ladder — self-manage, routine, urgent, emergency — and the skill is
matching need to response.** Two anchored facts: **emergency numbers are set
country-by-country (there is no single universal number; e.g., 112 across much of
Europe, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US/Canada, 119/110/10111
elsewhere — S-195)**, and **basic first aid — and knowing when a "minor" injury is not
minor — is part of knowing when to seek care (S-082, S-313).** Keep it safety-first:
help-seeking is *part* of first aid, never a sign of failure, and learners rehearse
words, not medical procedures they are not trained to do.

The **physical** lens: reading the body's signals — what is serious, what is not. The
**critical-thinking** lens: judging urgency without panic and without denial. The
**social-emotional** lens: mental-health needs belong on the same ladder; seeking help
for a mind is as normal as for a body. Watch the emotional register — normalize both
"it is okay to rest" and "it is okay to ask." Preview: Lesson 5 turns to the rights a
learner has once they walk through the door.

## Procedure

1. **Recall (5 min).** From Lesson 3, what are the doors into care in your place?
   Today: *when* do you use each one?
2. **Meet the ladder (10 min).** Four rungs: **self-manage** (rest, fluids, a cool
   cloth, a day of gentle routine), **routine** (book an appointment — a check-up, a
   nagging ache, a medicine question, a mental-health check-in), **urgent** (see
   someone soon — a fever that will not break, a cut needing stitches, a worry that
   will not settle), and **emergency** (call now — trouble breathing, heavy bleeding,
   no response, severe pain).
3. **Sort the scenarios (12 min).** With a partner, place each scenario on a rung and
   defend it. Watch the edge cases: a "small" burn, a headache that is suddenly much
   worse, a low mood that will not lift for weeks. Notice that mental-health needs sit
   on the *same* ladder as physical ones.
4. **Your emergency number (6 min).** Emergency numbers are set country by country —
   there is no single universal number (S-195). Write the one that applies where you
   live, and memorize the words: your name, where you are, what is wrong.
5. **First aid and when to get help (10 min).** Basic first aid for a minor cut or
   burn — clean water, a clean cover, cool (never ice) on a burn — is only the first
   step; the second is knowing the signs that mean "this needs professional help"
   (S-082, S-313). Rehearse aloud: "This cut is deep and will not stop bleeding — I
   need help."
6. **Practice the call (8 min).** Role-play calling for help with a partner: state the
   number, your location, what is wrong, and what help you need. Swap roles.
7. **Close (4 min).** Write your own one-line rule for "when to self-manage" and your
   one-line rule for "when to call now."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Sort only two categories — "rest at home" and "get help" — and practice
  saying the emergency number clearly.
- **Extension:** Build a personal "my red flags" list — the specific signs, for a
  condition or situation relevant to you or a loved one, that mean "seek care now."

## Assessment

- **Formative (peer + self + performance):** Can the learner place a scenario on the
  correct rung, justify it, state their local emergency number, and rehearse the
  help-seeking words?
- **Portfolio artifact (unit):** The completed urgency ladder with the recorded local
  emergency number and the two written rules.

## Home connection

At home, confirm the emergency number with an adult and write it somewhere everyone
can see. Add one "get help now" sign your household agrees on (e.g., trouble
breathing) and one "rest at home" sign (e.g., a mild cold).
