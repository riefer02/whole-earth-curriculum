# Lesson 7 — A Family Story or Tradition

## Summary

Learners identify a story or tradition that belongs to their own family — a holiday,
a meal, a song, a way of welcoming, a tale told again and again — and share it with
the group. They begin to say what it means to them, while honoring that every family
has a different shape and story, and every one is worth telling.

## Objectives

- Name a family story or tradition and tell what it means to them. _(D01.S2.01.02)_

## Connection

Every family carries stories and traditions — ways of celebrating, eating, greeting,
or remembering that get passed down like treasures. A tradition might be a special
meal, a song at bedtime, or a story your grandparent tells. These things belong to
*your* family, and they are part of what makes you, you. People everywhere keep
traditions, and each one is different.

## Materials

- A share-a-bag for each learner (or any small container)
- Drawing paper and crayons
- Chart paper for listing traditions

## Preparation

- Send home (or explain) the share-a-bag: each learner brings one small object that
  stands for a family story or tradition.
- Set out drawing supplies and chart paper.
- Arrange a sharing circle.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Honor every family shape — a family
may be two parents, one parent, grandparents, guardians, siblings, or chosen kin.
Traditions may be cultural, religious, seasonal, or simply a family's private habit;
none is "better" than another. If a learner cannot name one, offer gentle examples
(a bedtime routine, a birthday way, a favorite food). Keep sharing warm and
non-competitive; there is no ranking of traditions.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think: what does your family do that other families might not?
   A special food? A song? A way of saying hello or goodbye?
2. **Share-a-bag (15 min).** Show the object you brought and tell the story or
   tradition it stands for. Listeners ask one gentle question each ("Who taught it to
   you?" "When do you do it?"). A learner who does not want to speak aloud may show
   the object, act the tradition out with gestures, or have a partner tell it — the
   tradition is shared, not the speaking.
3. **What it means (10 min).** Say one sentence about what this tradition means to
   you: "This means ___ to me because ___." Draw a picture of it.
4. **Our traditions wall (10 min).** Add your tradition to the group's chart. Look at
   the list — so many different families, so many different stories.
5. **Close (5 min).** Traditions are like family treasures. Yours is worth telling,
   and so is everyone else's.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a picture prompt; scribe the learner's "what it means" sentence.
- **Extension:** Ask a family member for the story's *origin* — where it began — and
  share one new detail.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one family story or tradition and
  say something it means to them?
- **Performance:** The learner shares their tradition with the group.

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you the story of a family tradition — where it came from
— and share what you learned next time.
