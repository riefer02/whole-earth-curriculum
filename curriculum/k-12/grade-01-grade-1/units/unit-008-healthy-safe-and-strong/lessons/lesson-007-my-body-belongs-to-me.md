# Lesson 7 — My Body Belongs to Me

## Summary

Learners discover, gently and without fear, that their body belongs to them. They learn
that "no" and "stop" are always okay, that they may decline a hug or handshake and be
respected, and that if a touch makes them feel unsafe or uncomfortable they can tell a
trusted adult. The message is empowering, never frightening, and never places fault on
the child.

## Objectives

- Know that their body belongs to them and tell a trusted adult if someone makes them
  feel unsafe or uncomfortable. _(D03.S4.01.02)_

## Connection

You have spent this unit caring for your body — washing it, feeding it, dressing it.
Caring for your body also means knowing one big, true thing: **your body belongs to
you**. It is yours. Because it is yours, you get to say who touches you and how, and you
get to say "no" or "stop" any time. Today you will practice saying what is true: my body
belongs to me.

## Materials

- A doll or stuffed animal for gentle, pretend role-play
- Paper and crayons
- A "yes / stop" picture card

## Preparation

- Choose a soft doll or stuffed animal to stand in for bodies during practice, so
  role-play stays gentle and pretend.
- Prepare a simple "yes / stop" picture card a learner can point to.
- Read the facilitator note fully, including the safeguarding guidance, before teaching.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **This is the unit's most sensitive
lesson. Read this note fully and prepare yourself before teaching.**

- Keep it gentle and brief. The message is empowering — **"tell a trusted adult"** — never
  "be afraid of everyone." Do not use frightening or graphic content, and do not go
  beyond "tell a trusted adult" for any question about unsafe touch.
- Do not use anatomical names. Stay with "the parts of your body that your clothes
  cover are private — they belong only to you." Nothing more is needed at this age.
- Model and practice consent in your own classroom today: a child may decline a hug or
  a handshake, and that refusal is respected. Greetings differ around the world — hugs,
  handshakes, joined palms, bows, cheek-kisses — but consent applies to all of them: a
  child may decline any touch they do not want. Say "no" or "stop" is always okay, and
  when a friend says it, we stop right away and listen.
- **Never imply a child is at fault.** If anything makes a child feel unsafe or
  uncomfortable, it is never their fault, and they are never in trouble for telling.
- Keep all practice with the doll or stuffed animal, never with real bodies.
- **If a child discloses abuse or an unsafe situation:** stay calm; believe them; thank
  them for telling you; do **not** promise secrecy; do not press for details or ask
  leading questions; tell your designated safeguarding lead and follow your local
  child-protection procedure immediately.
- Honor every way of saying no: a learner who does not use a speaking voice may sign,
  point to the "stop" card, raise a hand, or show a picture. A learner who is blind or
  has low vision practices the words aloud and is told the "yes/stop" card by touch. A
  learner who is deaf or hard of hearing practices in the sign language they use and
  points to the "yes/stop" card; keep all demonstrations where they can be clearly seen.
- This lesson builds on Kindergarten's "My Body Is My Home" and "Asking for Help"
  (U.00.002) and leads into the next lesson on trusted adults.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Put your hand over your heart. This body is yours — your only
   one, your whole life long. It belongs to you. You have cared for it in this unit, and
   caring for it also means knowing it is yours.
2. **Your body belongs to you (10 min).** Because your body is yours, no one may touch
   you in a way you do not want. The parts of your body that your clothes cover are
   **private** — they belong only to you. Other people's bodies belong to them, too, and
   we treat every body with care and respect.
3. **"No" and "stop" are always okay (15 min).** You may always say "no, thank you" or
   "stop" — even to a grown-up, even to someone who loves you, even to a friend — if a
   touch or a game makes you uncomfortable. People around the world greet in many ways —
   a hug, a handshake, a kiss on the cheek, joined palms, or a bow — and whatever the
   greeting, your body is still yours: you may choose to skip a hug, a handshake, or any
   touch you do not want. And when a friend says "no" or "stop," we stop right away and
   listen.
   Practice with the doll: say "no, thank you" and "stop" in a clear, calm voice — or
   sign, or point to the "stop" card. Every way of saying no works.
4. **Safe and uncomfortable feelings (10 min).** A safe touch is one you welcome — a hug
   you asked for, a high-five, holding hands on a walk. An uncomfortable touch is one
   that makes your body feel "no" inside — your tummy tight, your heart fast, wanting to
   get away. If anything makes you feel unsafe or uncomfortable, you can say "stop" and
   **tell a trusted adult**. It is never your fault, and you are never in trouble for
   telling.
5. **Draw "my body, my rules" (5 min).** Draw one thing only you decide about your body —
   who you hug, when you say stop, who helps you wash.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "My body belongs to me. My no matters. I can
   always tell a trusted adult."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Role-play with the doll and practice one phrase many times; model the
  words and a calm voice.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Practice "no/stop" with a sign, a raised hand, or a point
  to the "yes / stop" card.
- **Extension:** Name two trusted adults you could tell if something felt wrong — a
  bridge to the next lesson on asking for help.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner say (or show, in their own way) that their
  body belongs to them, that "no/stop" is always okay, and that they can tell a trusted
  adult?
- **Self:** The learner names one person they could tell if something felt unsafe or
  uncomfortable.

## Home connection

With a trusted grown-up, practice saying "my body belongs to me" and "no, thank you."
Talk together about the trusted adults you could tell if something ever felt wrong.
