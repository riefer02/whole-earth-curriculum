# Lesson 8 — Trusted Adults and Asking for Help

## Summary

Learners name their trusted adults at home, at school, and in their neighborhood, and
practice asking for help clearly when they feel unsafe or uncomfortable. They learn
that asking for help is a strength and that telling is never wrong — the gentle capstone
of the unit's safety strand.

## Objectives

- Know that their body belongs to them and tell a trusted adult if someone makes them
  feel unsafe or uncomfortable. _(D03.S4.01.02)_

## Connection

If you fell and hurt your knee, you would tell a grown-up. If something about your body
or a touch made you feel unsafe or uncomfortable, you would tell a grown-up too — that
is just as important, and just as brave. Some grown-ups are **trusted adults**: people
who listen to you and help you. Today you will name your trusted adults and practice
asking for help.

## Materials

- A "help hand" template (a hand outline)
- Crayons
- Scenario cards (a cut, feeling sick, feeling scared or unsafe, feeling lost)

## Preparation

- Prepare a "help hand" template — a hand outline with five fingers.
- Prepare simple scenario cards of times to ask for help.
- Read the facilitator note fully, including the safeguarding guidance, before teaching.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). **Read this note fully before teaching.**

- Keep the tone warm, calm, and reassuring. Asking for help is a strength, never a
  weakness, and telling is never wrong.
- Help each learner name trusted adults who are realistically available to them. If a
  learner names no one at home, gently guide them toward a teacher, nurse, school
  counselor, or neighbor — without pressure and without implying anything about their
  family.
- Frame "keep telling" supportively, as persistence, not as "adults won't believe you":
  if the first grown-up is busy or doesn't understand, tell another one, and another,
  until someone listens and helps.
- Honor every way of asking: speaking, signing, pointing, showing a picture, or tapping
  a shoulder all work. A learner who is deaf or hard of hearing practices in their sign
  language or points to the scenario and help cards; face the learner for every
  demonstration so the words are visible on the lips and hands.
- **If a child discloses abuse or an unsafe situation:** stay calm; believe them; thank
  them for telling you; do **not** promise secrecy; do not press for details or ask
  leading questions; tell your designated safeguarding lead and follow your local
  child-protection procedure immediately.
- The "my trusted adults" help hand is a unit portfolio artifact; keep learners'
  drawings. This lesson builds on Kindergarten's "Asking for Help" (U.00.002) and closes
  the unit's safety strand.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Remember last time: your body belongs to you, and if something
   makes you feel unsafe or uncomfortable, you tell a trusted adult. Today we will learn
   *who* your trusted adults are and *how* to ask for help.
2. **Who is a trusted adult? (10 min).** A trusted adult is a grown-up who listens to
   you and helps you. At home, that might be a caregiver, a grandparent, an aunt, or an
   uncle. At school, a teacher, a nurse, or a counselor. In your neighborhood, a trusted
   neighbor. You get to choose who you trust — a trusted adult is someone you feel safe
   with.
3. **How do I tell? (15 min).** Practice the words in a clear, calm voice: "I need
   help." "I feel unsafe." "Someone touched me in a way I didn't like." You can also
   sign, point, show a picture, or tap a shoulder — every way of asking works. Take
   turns with a partner or a doll, one of you asking, one listening.
4. **Keep telling until someone helps (5 min).** If the first grown-up is busy or
   doesn't understand, tell another one — and another — until someone listens and helps.
   Telling is never wrong, and you are never in trouble for telling.
5. **Draw your "help hand" (10 min).** Trace your hand. On each finger, draw or write
   one trusted adult you could tell. This is your help hand — keep it where you can see
   it.
6. **Close (5 min).** Say it together: "Asking for help is a strength. I am never in
   trouble for telling. Keeping my body safe is my right."

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two or three scenarios and practice one help phrase many times; model
  the words and a calm voice.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Practice asking with a sign, a picture card, or a point;
  fill the help hand with drawn faces rather than words.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to add a third trusted adult and to practice telling
  two adults in order, as they would if the first were busy.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least two trusted adults and
  practice asking for help clearly, in their own way?
- **Portfolio:** The "my trusted adults" help-hand drawing becomes a unit portfolio
  artifact.

## Home connection

With a grown-up, name your trusted adults and practice saying "I need help" in a clear,
calm voice. Keep your help hand somewhere you can see it.
