# Lesson 11 — A Complete Sentence

## Summary

Learners learn that a complete written sentence has three parts: a capital letter
at the start, a full thought, and an end mark. They practice finding what is
missing and fixing broken sentences.

## Objectives

- Compose one or more complete sentences about a topic or picture, using a capital
  letter and an end mark. _(D04.S3.01.02)_

## Connection

A written sentence is like a package with a clear start and a clear finish. The
capital letter is the lid you open at the top, and the end mark is the bottom that
tells you the sentence is done. Without them, a reader does not know where a
thought begins or ends — like a road with no signs. Today you will learn to mark
the start and the finish of a sentence.

## Materials

- Example sentences written large (one complete, one "broken")
- Sentence-fixing strips

## Preparation

- Write one complete sentence and one broken sentence (missing capital or end
  mark) large.
- Prepare fixing strips with missing capitals or end marks.
- Plan to model fixing one sentence.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Note a small truth with care: the "capital letter and end
mark" convention belongs to writing systems that use case and punctuation — honor
that other writing systems signal beginnings and endings differently, and never
rank them. Teach the learner's own system's conventions, with explicit modeling
and guided practice (philosophy §13). Keep it concrete and playful. A learner may
"fix" a sentence by dictating to a scribe, choosing a capital or end-mark card,
typing, or using a gesture (punch the air for the capital, tap for the end mark) —
the convention, not the handwriting, is the goal.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Look at two sentences: one complete, one broken. Can you
   spot the difference?
2. **Meet the three parts (10 min).** A complete sentence has three parts: a
   **capital letter** to open it, a **full thought** in the middle, and an **end
   mark** — a period (.), a question mark (?), or an exclamation mark (!) — to
   close it. Say the three parts and point to each in the complete sentence.
3. **Find what is missing (10 min).** Look at the broken sentence. Is the first
   letter a capital? Is there an end mark? Point to what is missing.
4. **Fix the sentence (15 min).** Take a fixing strip. Find the missing capital or
   end mark, write it in, and read the complete sentence aloud. Do this for a few
   sentences, alone or with a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** A capital opens the sentence and an end mark closes it. Now
   your readers will know exactly where your thought starts and ends.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Fix only the missing end mark today, with the capital already in
  place; point and read together.
- **Support (motor-limited):** Fix the sentence by dictating to a scribe, choosing
  a capital or end-mark card, or using a gesture for each part.
- **Extension:** Fix sentences missing both a capital and an end mark, and choose
  the right end mark (period, question, or exclamation) for each.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify and add a missing capital
  letter or end mark to make a complete sentence?

## Home connection

Find one written sentence at home (a label, a book, a message) and point to its
capital letter and its end mark.
