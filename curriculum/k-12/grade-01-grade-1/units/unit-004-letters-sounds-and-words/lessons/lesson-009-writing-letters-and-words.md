# Lesson 9 — Writing Letters and Words

## Summary

Learners practice forming the marks of their own writing system — letters or
characters — and write simple words, following explicit modeling and guided
practice. Writing turns a spoken idea into marks that last.

## Objectives

- Write letters and simple words to express an idea, and read back what they
  wrote. _(D04.S3.01.01)_

## Connection

A spoken word vanishes as soon as it is said. A written word stays — on paper, on
a slate, in the sand — so someone far away or years later can read it. When you
learn to form the marks of your writing system, you learn to keep your words. That
is how letters, recipes, and stories have traveled across the world.

## Materials

- A writing surface and tool (paper and pencil, or sand tray and stick)
- Model cards of a few marks and simple words

## Preparation

- Choose 2–4 marks and 1–2 simple words in the learners' home writing system.
- Prepare model cards or write the marks large to copy.
- Set out writing surfaces for each learner.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Use **explicit instruction and guided practice** (philosophy
§13): model each mark's strokes, then guide. Remain **script-agnostic** — a
learner's system may be Latin, Arabic, Devanagari, Chinese characters, Cyrillic,
Ethiopic, Hangul, or another; teach the strokes of the learner's actual system and
never rank scripts. Encourage slow, careful forming over speed. Writing is about
keeping the idea, not perfect marks: a learner may also dictate to a scribe, choose
marks on a letter board, type on any device, or trace with the whole arm, a foot,
or a mouth stick — handwriting is never judged as "correct."

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Say a word you know. It was sound a moment ago, and now it
   is gone. Today you will learn to write marks so your word stays.
2. **Watch and trace (10 min).** Watch a mark being written slowly. Notice where it
   starts, which way it moves, and where it stops. Now trace it in the air with
   your finger, then on your writing surface.
3. **Write the mark (10 min).** Write the mark yourself, several times. Say its
   sound as you write it. Slow and careful beats fast and messy.
4. **Write a word (15 min).** Put your marks together to write a simple word. Look
   at the model, then write it. Say the word when you finish — does the writing
   match what you said?
5. **Close (5 min).** You just kept a word. Every time you write, you are doing
   what people have done for thousands of years — leaving a mark that lasts.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Trace over a dotted model; write just one mark today.
- **Support (motor-limited):** Dictate to a scribe, choose marks on a letter board,
  type, or trace with a whole-arm, foot, or mouth-stick movement — the mark is the
  idea, not the hand shape.
- **Extension:** Write a second word using the same marks, and write your name.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner form the marks of their writing
  system and write a simple word that matches what they say?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** A sample of the learner's written marks and word.

## Home connection

Write one word at home and show it to someone — tell them what it says, then keep
it somewhere safe.
