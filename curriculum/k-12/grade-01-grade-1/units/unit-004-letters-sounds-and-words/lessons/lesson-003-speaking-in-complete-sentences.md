# Lesson 3 — Speaking in Complete Sentences

## Summary

Learners learn that a complete sentence has a "who" and a "what happened." They
build sentences from simple frames, then share an idea of their own in a full
sentence so others can understand them.

## Objectives

- Speak in complete sentences to share an idea or experience so others can
  understand. _(D04.S1.01.02)_

## Connection

When you say only one word — "mango" — people guess what you mean. When you say a
whole sentence — "I like mangoes" — they know exactly. A complete sentence has a
who and a what happened, and it helps your idea travel clearly to another person.

## Materials

- A sentence frame chart ("I like ___", "I saw ___")
- Picture cards of familiar things
- A soft ball or beanbag (optional)

## Preparation

- Write or display two simple sentence frames.
- Gather picture cards (or plan to point at real objects).
- Arrange a circle for the sentence game.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Prepare the frames and cards, then guide the game. The
goal is completeness, not a "correct" accent or dialect — every way of speaking is
honored. Gently stretch one-word answers into sentences by asking "Who? What
happened?" without correcting the learner's voice. A complete sentence can also be
signed, built with picture cards, or spoken through a communication device — a
"who" and a "what happened" arranged in any of these ways is still a complete
sentence.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Say one word about something you like. Now
   listen as a friend guesses what you meant. One word leaves a lot of guessing.
2. **Meet the sentence (5 min).** A complete sentence has two parts: a **who** and a
   **what happened**. Look at the frame "I like ___." The who is "I"; the rest
   tells what happened.
3. **Build sentences (10 min).** Using a frame, fill in the blank. Say the whole
   sentence out loud: "I like rice." "I saw a goat." Say each one clearly.
4. **Sentence game (15 min).** Pass the ball (or point in turn). When it reaches
   you, finish a frame with your own idea and say the complete sentence. Everyone
   listens, then the next person goes.
5. **Share your own idea (5 min).** Without the frame now, tell the group one thing
   about your day in a complete sentence. Remember the who and the what happened.
6. **Close (5 min).** Complete sentences help your ideas reach other people. The
   clearer you speak, the more you can share.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Keep the frame visible and accept two-word completions at first,
  then say the full sentence together.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Build the sentence by signing it, pointing to picture
  cards in order, or using a device — the "who" and "what happened" can be shown,
  not only said.
- **Extension:** Stretch one idea into two complete sentences ("I like mangoes. They
  are sweet.").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner share an idea in a complete sentence
  with a clear who and what happened?

## Home connection

Tell someone at home one complete sentence about your day, and ask them to tell you
one back.
