# Lesson 1 — Kind and Unkind Choices

## Summary

Learners discover that "kind" and "unkind" are words for *choices we make*, not
labels for *who a child is*. They name one kind choice and one unkind choice in
simple, everyday situations and tell which is which, held as a question to explore —
not a rule to obey.

## Objectives

- Name a kind choice and an unkind choice in a simple situation and tell which is
  which. _(D09.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

Think of a time someone helped you up when you fell, or shared something with you.
That warm feeling came from a **kind choice**. Think of a time someone grabbed a toy
out of your hands or said a mean word. That hurt came from an **unkind choice**.
Choices are things people *do*, not things people *are* — and we can all choose
again. Everywhere in the world, in every home and every classroom, people make kind
and unkind choices every day.

## Materials

- Simple-situation picture cards (a dropped snack, two children wanting the same
  swing, someone left out of a game, someone being helped up)
- Crayons and paper

## Preparation

- Prepare or draw a set of simple-situation picture cards showing everyday moments.
- Arrange a circle for gathering and acting out.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a self-directed
learner. The central commitment is **name the act, never the child**: say "that
choice was unkind," never "you are a bad child." Unkind choices are things everyone
makes sometimes, and we can choose again. Kind and unkind are offered as a question
("what does this choice do for the other person?") rather than a fixed rule. Accept
every learner's example — there is no single right answer, and a choice can be kind
in one situation and unkind in another. A learner who does not speak aloud names the
choice by pointing, signing, gesturing, or placing a marker on a card; a learner who
is deaf or hard of hearing reads each situation from the picture cards or the
acted-out scene and names the choice by signing or pointing; a learner who is blind
or low-vision hears each situation described and names the choices aloud or by
touch; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner to act out the
choice. Do not require anyone to speak in front of the group; a learner may always
pass.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit comfortably. Think of a time someone was kind to you and a
   time someone was unkind. How did each one feel?
2. **Meet the words (10 min).** A **kind choice** helps someone feel cared for. An
   **unkind choice** hurts someone's feelings or body. These words name the *choice*,
   not the person — everyone can choose again.
3. **Name which is which (15 min).** For each situation card, name one kind choice
   and one unkind choice, and tell which is which (say it, sign it, point to it, or
   act it out). Wonder together: what does each choice do for the other person?
4. **Draw your kind choice (10 min).** Draw one kind choice you could make — for a
   friend, a family member, or an animal. If drawing is not your way, build it from
   found objects, act it out, or have a partner draw or scribe while you direct.
   Tell a partner what it is and why it is kind.
5. **Close (5 min).** Kind and unkind are choices, and a kind choice is always
   possible. Tomorrow you can choose again.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer just two situations; model one sentence "That choice is kind
  because…" with a word and a gesture. A non-speaking learner points to a card and
  nods or shakes; a learner with limited movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to think of one choice that could be kind in one
  situation and unkind in another (for example, shouting to warn of danger vs.
  shouting at a friend) and wonder why.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one kind and one unkind choice in
  a simple situation and tell which is which?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "my kind choice" drawing (or a built, acted, or
  partner-scribed version), kept in the learner's portfolio.

## Home connection

Tonight, notice one kind choice and one unkind choice around you — in your home, on
the street, or in a story. Tell a grown-up which is which.
