# Lesson 7 — Words for My Feelings in a Conflict

## Summary

Learners practice saying how they feel during a small, gentle conflict using the
sentence "I feel… when… I need…" They learn that feelings can be named in many ways
— spoken, signed, pointed to, or acted out — and that no one has to speak if they do
not want to.

## Objectives

- Use words to say how they feel during a conflict and listen when a friend says how
  they feel. _(D09.S4.01.01 — saying how I feel)_

## Connection

When two people want the same thing, feelings can get big fast — mad, sad, scared,
or left out. **Conflict** is just a word for when two people want different things at
the same time. It is not bad to have a conflict; what helps is using words to say
how you feel. Everywhere in the world, people solve small conflicts by saying how
they feel and what they need. Today you will practice saying how *you* feel.

## Materials

- A feelings picture board (happy, sad, mad, scared, left out, calm)
- An "I feel… when… I need…" sentence strip
- Two puppets or soft toys

## Preparation

- Prepare the feelings board and the three-part sentence strip.
- Set up a small space for acting out a gentle conflict with puppets.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). This lesson teaches **expressing feelings, not
winning**. Conflict is named as normal and okay — two people wanting different
things — never as a child "being bad." No child is ever the problem; the conflict is
the thing we solve together. Keep the conflict gentle (both want the same ball, the
same swing) and free of blame. "Use your words" means *however a learner expresses
themselves* — spoken words, sign, a gesture, pointing to a feeling on the board, eye
gaze, or a communication board; a grown-up or peer can voice what they point to or
sign. Learners who are deaf or hard of hearing follow the strip by sight and sign;
learners who are blind or low-vision hear the sentence named and touch a textured
feeling card; learners with a motor difference can point with a partner's help or
direct the partner to move the puppets while they show the feeling. Never require
anyone to speak or perform in front of the group; a learner may always pass. The
sentence is a scaffold, not a script to obey — wonder together about other words
that could work. (This lesson's partner, listening, comes next.)

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time two people wanted the same thing. What
   feelings came up?
2. **Meet the feelings (10 min).** Look at the feelings board. Name each feeling
   (say, sign, or point). A **conflict** is when two people want different things at
   once — it is okay, and it can be solved.
3. **Learn the sentence (10 min).** Practice: "I **feel**… (name a feeling) **when**…
   (what happened) I **need**… (what would help)." Say it, sign it, or point to each
   part on the strip.
4. **Act it out (15 min).** With the puppets, show a small conflict — both want the
   same ball. Take a breath, then say how you feel: "I feel mad when I do not get a
   turn; I need a turn too." A learner who does not move the puppets can point to the
   feeling or direct a partner.
5. **Close (5 min).** Naming your feeling is the first step in solving a conflict.
   Words can carry a big feeling so it does not hurt anyone.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only "I feel…" first, pointing to a face on the board; a
  non-speaking learner points to the feeling. Model the full sentence word by word.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to try a second feeling for the same conflict and
  notice how the need changes ("I feel scared… I need a grown-up to help").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner use words (or a supported way) to say
  how they feel during a conflict?
- **Performance:** The acted-out "I feel… when… I need…" with the puppets, reflected
  on aloud.

## Home connection

At home, when a feeling gets big, try "I feel… when… I need…" with someone you trust.
