# Lesson 3 — Fair and Unfair Sharing

## Summary

Learners practice a fair way and an unfair way to share a toy or snack, name which is
fair, and meet two ideas side by side as an open question: fair can mean the *same
amount* for everyone, and it can also mean *what each person needs*.

## Objectives

- Describe a fair way and an unfair way to share a toy or snack and say which is
  fair. _(D09.S2.01.01)_

## Connection

Think of a snack being passed around, or two children who both want the same ball.
**Sharing** means deciding how a thing goes to everyone who wants or needs it. A
**fair** share is one where everyone is thought of; an **unfair** share is one where
someone is left out or gets everything. People everywhere share food, water, and
turns — a family passing a bowl of rice, children taking turns on a swing, neighbors
sharing a harvest. Today you will try it yourself and say which way is fair.

## Materials

- A countable snack or toy to share (crackers, seeds, blocks, or toy pieces)
- A sharing picture set (splitting evenly, one person taking all, taking turns,
  giving each what they need)

## Preparation

- Gather a pile of something countable to share (or found objects outdoors).
- Prepare or draw pictures of fair and unfair ways to share.

## Facilitator note

Written to the learner ("you"). Hold two ideas open, not as a rule: **equality** —
fair is the same amount for everyone — and **equity** — fair is what each person
needs (a smaller child may need help reaching, a hungrier person may need more food).
Both are real ideas about fairness; the lesson wonders about them without deciding
for the learner. There is no single "correct" share. Keep examples gentle: no one is
punished or shamed for an unfair share; unfairness is simply named and then made
fair. Name the *act* ("that share left someone out"), never the child. Learners from
more communal traditions may share without counting ("we just share"); honor that as
a fair way too, not a lesser one. A learner who does not speak aloud names a fair or
unfair share by pointing or signing; a learner who is blind or low-vision feels and
counts the objects by touch; a learner with a motor difference can direct a partner
to move the objects. A learner may always pass.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a time something was shared. How did it feel to get
   a fair share — or an unfair one?
2. **Meet fair and unfair (10 min).** Look at the pictures. Name a **fair** way
   (everyone thought of) and an **unfair** way (someone left out or taking all).
   Which is which?
3. **Try it (15 min).** With your pile of snacks or toys, make a fair share and an
   unfair share. Say, sign, or point to which is fair and why.
4. **Wonder: same or what-each-needs? (10 min).** Try two fair ideas. First, give
   everyone the same amount. Then, think: does anyone need something different — a
   smaller child who cannot reach, a friend who is very hungry? Wonder together which
   is fairer, and why it might be both.
5. **Close (5 min).** Sharing is thinking of everyone. Fair can mean the same amount,
   and it can mean what each person needs — a question to keep wondering about.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice only "everyone gets some" with a small pile, counting out one
  each. A non-speaking learner points to the fair picture; a learner with limited
  movement directs a partner.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to invent a sharing problem (an odd number of
  crackers, a toy that cannot be split) and wonder how to share it fairly.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe a fair and an unfair way to
  share and say which is fair?
- **Performance:** The acted-out fair share, reflected on aloud with the group.

## Home connection

At home, help share a meal or a snack. Try a fair way, and ask someone: is fair the
same amount for everyone, or what each person needs — or both?
