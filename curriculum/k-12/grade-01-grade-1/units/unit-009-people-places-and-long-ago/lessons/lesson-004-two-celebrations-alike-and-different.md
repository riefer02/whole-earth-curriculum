# Lesson 4 — Two Celebrations, Alike and Different

## Summary

Learners compare how two families or communities mark the same kind of event — a
birthday, harvest, or new year — and notice what is different and what is alike,
holding both with equal respect.

## Objectives

- Compare how two families or communities celebrate or mark an event, such as a
  birthday, harvest, or new year, and notice what is different and what is
  alike. _(D08.S2.01.02)_

## Connection

All over the world, people mark the turn of a year. Some families welcome a new year
with a shared meal and a red lantern; others mark it with a spring table of seven
things that begin with the same sound. The foods and words differ — but in both,
people gather with loved ones and hope for a good year. Comparing two celebrations
is a way to see both what makes each one special and what all people share.

## Materials

- Two celebration description cards (two new years, or two harvests, from different
  regions)
- A large "alike / different" chart (two overlapping circles or two columns)

## Preparation

- Choose two gentle celebrations of the same kind of event, from different regions,
  and learn to describe each respectfully with its meaning.
- Draw the "alike / different" chart large.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. **The discipline is equal respect.** Compare, never rank:
do not say one celebration is bigger, better, or more "correct." Describe each from
inside its meaning — a Lunar New Year in an East or Southeast Asian community means
family and a fresh start; a harvest festival in a West African community means
gratitude for the land; Nowruz in a Persian or Central Asian community means spring
and renewal. Both are complete and worthy exactly as they are. Avoid costumes and
caricature entirely; no learner is asked to dress up or "perform" a culture. Honor
adopted, fostered, and chosen families, and let a learner pass on sharing their own.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Think of a celebration you know — a new year, a birthday, a
   harvest. What do people do? (You do not have to share it.)
2. **Meet two celebrations (15 min).** Listen to two celebrations of the same kind of
   event. For example: one community welcomes the new year with a shared meal and a
   lantern; another welcomes it with a spring table and a song. Listen for the people,
   the foods, the words, and the feelings.
3. **Find what is alike (10 min).** What do both do? Both gather. Both share food.
   Both hope for a good year. Place those on the "alike" side, where the two circles
   overlap.
4. **Find what is different (10 min).** What is special to each one? The foods, the
   words, the colors, the songs. Place those on the two outer sides.
5. **Close (5 min).** Different celebrations are different treasures — and alike in
   the heart: people gathering with hope and love. Neither is better; both are real.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on one "alike" and one "different"; offer the words "gathering"
  and "food" as a starting place.
- **Non-speaking:** A learner places pictures or objects on the chart instead of
  speaking.
- **Blind/low-vision:** Describe each celebration's sounds, foods, and smells richly;
  sort findings by placing textured markers on the chart.
- **Deaf/HH:** Tell each celebration with a picture sequence or in sign so it is
  seen; sort findings by placing picture cards or objects on the chart.
- **Motor difference:** Use large, easy-to-grip markers or cards; a partner places
  each finding while the learner directs with a point, a word, or eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Add a third celebration and find something alike across all three.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name at least one thing alike and one
  thing different between the two celebrations?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner describe both with equal
  respect, without ranking?

## Home connection

With a grown-up, think of two different families or communities you know that mark
the same kind of event. What do they do that is alike? What is different?
