# Lesson 6 — Maps and Globes: Home and Another Place

## Summary

Learners use a simple map or globe to find where they live and one other place, and
name one way the two places are different. A map is a picture of a place from above;
a globe is the whole Earth in the shape of a ball.

## Objectives

- Use a simple map or globe to find where they live and one other place, and tell one
  way the two places are different. _(D08.S3.01.02)_

## Connection

Have you ever drawn a picture of your room from above, as if you were a bird looking
down? That is a map — a picture that shows where things are. A globe is the whole
Earth drawn as a ball you can turn. Maps and globes help people find their way, from
the way to the market to the way across an ocean. Today you will find your own home
and one other place, and notice how they are not the same.

## Materials

- A simple map or globe, plus a hand-drawn map of the room or neighborhood
- Two place markers for "home" and "another place"

## Preparation

- Prepare a simple map of the room or neighborhood (drawn is fine) with a clear
  "home" spot.
- Have a world map or globe ready, or a round object to stand in for a globe.
- Choose one friendly "other place" to find together (a place from a story, a far
  city or island, a place someone in the group knows).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. **No travel is required** — the map and globe are found, not
journeyed to. **Never rank places:** do not say the other place is more or less than
home. "No correct place, only the one that is home" applies to every place on the
map. A globe is the Earth as a ball; a map is one flattened view — both are real
tools used by people everywhere. For a learner who is blind or has low vision, make
the map **tactile** — raised lines of string or sand-glue for land, a smooth patch
for water, a bump for home — so the places can be felt. "Another place" may be any
place a learner is curious about; let them choose where possible.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Imagine a bird flying over your room or street. What would it
   see from above?
2. **Meet the map and globe (10 min).** A **map** is a picture of a place from
   above. A **globe** is the whole Earth drawn as a ball. Look at both. Find the
   water and the land on each.
3. **Find home (10 min).** On the room or neighborhood map, put your marker on your
   home — or the spot closest to it. Say or point: "Here is where I live."
4. **Find another place (10 min).** On the world map or globe, find one other place —
   a place from a story, a far island, a city someone in your group knows. Put the
   second marker there. Is it near or far from home?
5. **Name one difference (10 min).** Ask: how are my home and this other place
   different? Is one by the sea and one far from water? Is one hot and one cold? Is
   one busy and one quiet? Name or show one difference.
6. **Close (5 min).** Maps and globes let you hold the whole world in your hands.
   Every place on them is someone's home, and each one is different.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Find only home on the room map today; find the other place together
  as a whole group.
- **Blind/low-vision:** Use a raised-line map and a round object with texture for
  land and water; find the places by touch.
- **Non-speaking:** Place markers and point to the difference, or act it out (shiver
  for cold, fan for hot), instead of speaking.
- **Deaf/HH:** The map and globe are visual by nature — find places by sight and
  sign the difference between the two places as you point to each.
- **Motor difference:** Use large pebbles or a pointing stick to mark places; a
  partner places the marker where the learner points or directs with eye-gaze.
- **Extension:** Find a third place and name one way it differs from both of the
  first two.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place or point to home and one other
  place on a map or globe?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Can the learner name or show one way the two
  places are different?

## Home connection

With a grown-up, look at any map you have — on a phone, in a book, or drawn by hand.
Find where you live and one other place you are curious about. Name one way they are
different.
