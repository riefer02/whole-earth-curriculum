# Lesson 1 — Sequencing My Day

## Summary

Learners put three or four events from their own day in order, using the words
before, after, and next. They practice the first skill of history: telling what
happened in order.

## Objectives

- Sequence three or four events from their own day or week in order, using words
  like before, after, and next. _(D08.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

Every day has a shape: you wake, you eat, you play or work, you rest. Whether you
live by the sea, in a busy city, on a farm, or in the mountains, your day still
unfolds one part after another. Telling the day in order is the very first way
people tell the story of time — the same skill a historian uses for a whole world.

## Materials

- Blank picture cards or paper squares (four per learner)
- A set of "day" picture cards for the worked example

## Preparation

- Draw or gather simple cards for a common day: waking, eating, playing or
  learning, resting.
- Give each learner four blank cards or paper squares.
- Decide on a space to lay cards in a left-to-right line (or top-to-bottom).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. **Sequencing is a foundational skill, so teach it with a
worked example first** (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006): model putting three day
cards in order, saying "first... next... after that," before learners do their own.
Honor every body: a learner who does not use a speaking voice points, acts, or
orders cards; a learner who is blind or has low vision sequences real objects by
touch and says or signs the order aloud. There is no "wrong" day — some learners
wake, then pray, then eat; some help a grandparent before school; some nap midday.
All orders are real.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Close your eyes. Picture your morning. What is the very first
   thing you did today?
2. **Meet the order words (10 min).** Watch the example. Three cards: a child
   waking, a child eating, a child sleeping. Say the order words aloud together:
   **first**, **next**, **then** — and the time words **before** and **after**. "The
   child wakes *first*. *Next*, the child eats. *Then*, the child sleeps."
3. **Guided practice (10 min).** Mix the cards up. Together, put them back in order,
   saying each word as you place it: "Before eating, the child wakes. After eating,
   the child sleeps."
4. **Sequence your own day (15 min).** On your four cards, draw or show four things
   you did today — waking, eating, playing or learning, resting, or anything else
   true for you. Lay them in a line in order.
5. **Tell your day's story (5 min).** Point to each card and tell the order: "First
   I... Next I... After that I... Then I..." You may point, act, draw, or sign.
6. **Close (5 min).** You just did what historians do: you told the story of time in
   order. Every day, everywhere, unfolds before and after.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only three events; model each order word with a clear gesture.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Sequence real objects by touch (a bowl for
  eating, a cloth for resting) and say or sign the order aloud.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Order picture cards or act events out with the body;
  point to "before" and "after" spots instead of speaking.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** The day cards are already pictures — read the sequence with
  your eyes and hands, and sign each order word as you place each card, so the order
  is seen, not only heard.
- **Support (motor-difference):** Use a large, stable surface and bigger cards;
  point or eye-gaze to each card while a partner moves it, or name the order while
  someone else places the cards.
- **Extension:** Sequence four or five events, and add the words "before" and
  "after" to compare two of them ("Before my nap, I ate").

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner place three or four events in a
  sensible order?
- **Formative (oral/performance):** Does the learner tell the order using at least
  two of the words before, after, and next — by speaking, signing, pointing, or
  acting?

## Home connection

At home, ask someone to tell you the order of their own day. Tell them yours, using
first, next, and then. Notice: is your day like theirs, or different?
