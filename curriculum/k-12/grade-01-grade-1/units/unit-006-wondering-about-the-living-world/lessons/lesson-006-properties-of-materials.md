# Lesson 6 — Properties of Materials

## Summary

Learners discover that every thing is made of a **material**, and that materials have
**properties** — hard or soft, shiny or dull, heavy or light — they can observe with
their senses. They sort and describe real objects by their properties.

## Objectives

- Observe and describe the properties of materials (such as hard, soft, shiny, heavy,
  and light). _(D06.S3.01.01)_

## Connection

A spoon, a cup, a leaf, and a stone are all things you can hold. But they are not the
same: the spoon is hard and shiny, the cup might be light, the leaf is soft, the
stone is heavy. What a thing is made of — its **material** — gives it its
**properties**, and properties are why we choose one material for one job and another
for another.

## Materials

- A collection of objects made of different materials (a stone, a cloth, a metal
  spoon, a wooden stick, a leaf, a plastic cup, a paper)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather objects of clearly different materials and properties, safe to handle.
- Plan a simple way to compare heavy and light (hand-feel, or a simple balance).

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Keep it sensory and concrete — this is
not about memorizing material names (wood, metal, plastic) but about *observing
properties*: hard, soft, shiny, heavy, light, rough, smooth. All such words are
welcome, including in learners' home languages. Honor every body: a learner who is
blind or has low vision feels weight and texture; a learner who does not speak points
or sorts; a learner with a motor difference may need objects brought close or a
partner to lift and compare. The technology lens is quiet here: talk about how people
everywhere choose materials for a job — a clay pot for water, a metal pan for
cooking, a wooden bowl for food — and that no material is "better," only better *for
something*. There is no correct answer for how a thing feels; notice differences
without ranking.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Pick up two things near you. Are they the same? How are they
   different? What they are made of gives them their **properties**.
2. **Meet the property words (10 min).** Feel and look at the objects. A stone is
   **hard**; a cloth is **soft**. A metal spoon is **shiny**; a leaf is not. A stone
   is **heavy**; a feather or a paper is **light**. Say each word and find an object
   that shows it.
3. **Sort by a property (15 min).** Choose one property and sort the objects into two
   groups: hard / soft, shiny / dull, heavy / light. Then try another property. Does
   the same object land in a different group? (Yes — an object can be hard *and*
   shiny.) 
4. **Draw two materials (10 min).** Draw two objects and write or say one property for
   each: "The stone is hard." "The cloth is soft."
5. **Close (5 min).** Things are made of materials, and materials have properties. Our
   senses tell us what those properties are — and why each material is good for its
   job.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two properties only (hard/soft) and sort a small pile.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Describe by weight and texture (heavy/light,
  rough/smooth) by hand.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the object that is hard, soft, heavy, or light,
  and sort by moving the objects.
- **Extension:** Sort the same objects by two properties at once (hard and shiny, soft
  and light) and explain.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name (or show) at least two properties
  of a material and sort objects by one property?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "two materials" drawing with a property for each.

## Home connection

At home, find one thing that is hard and one that is soft, one that is heavy and one
that is light. Tell a grown-up what each thing is made of and what it is good for.
