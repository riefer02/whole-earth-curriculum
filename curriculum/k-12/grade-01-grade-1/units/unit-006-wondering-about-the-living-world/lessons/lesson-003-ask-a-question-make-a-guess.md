# Lesson 3 — Ask a Question and Make a Guess

## Summary

Learners practice the next step of science after noticing: asking a question and
offering a **guess** they could check. They learn that a guess is not something to be
scared of — it is the very beginning of finding out, and being wrong is part of
learning.

## Objectives

- Ask a question about something they notice and offer a guess they can check.
  _(D06.S1.01.02)_

## Connection

You notice a seed on the ground and you wonder: *Will it grow?* You notice a shadow
and you wonder: *Why does it move?* A scientist does not stop at wondering — she asks
the question out loud and makes a guess: "I think maybe it needs water." A guess is
not about being right or wrong. It is where finding out begins.

## Materials

- One interesting thing to wonder about (a seed, a shell, a floating leaf, a toy car,
  or a shadow)
- A "wonder board" or large paper
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Choose one thing everyone can look at and wonder about together.
- Prepare the wonder board (or plan to draw one) with two columns: "I wonder…" and "I
  think…".

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Make guessing safe and joyful: say
clearly that a guess does not have to be right — "a wrong guess is still a great
guess, because now we can check it." Never let a guess be mocked or ranked. In
settings where questioning adults is not safe or usual, frame this as *wondering
together*, not challenging anyone, and offer the wonder box so learners can ask
without speaking up (see `docs/contexts.md`). The goal is the habit: notice →
question → guess → a way to check. The check can be as simple as "watch tomorrow and
see." Some questions we cannot answer yet — and that is wonderful, not a failure.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** You have been noticing and describing. Now a scientist asks:
   "I wonder…?" What have you been wondering about?
2. **Notice and question (10 min).** Look at the thing together. Turn what you notice
   into a question: "I wonder why it floats." "I wonder if it will grow." "I wonder
   where it came from." There are no silly questions.
3. **Make a guess (15 min).** For one question, offer your own guess: "I think maybe
   it floats because it is light." Say it, sign it, point to a picture, or have it
   written for you. Then ask: how could we check? "We could drop it in water and
   watch." "We could plant it and wait." A guess you can check is called a
   **prediction**.
4. **Draw your wonder (10 min).** On your paper, draw your question and your guess —
   what you noticed, and what you think.
5. **Close (5 min).** Notice, ask, guess, check — that is the rhythm of science. Some
   questions we cannot answer yet, and wondering about them is wonderful too.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Offer the frame "I wonder ___" and "I think ___" and fill it in
  together.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to a picture of the question and the guess, or act
  the guess out.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Wonder about a thing you can touch or hear (a shell,
  a seed, a bell) instead of a shadow; a partner names it aloud as you explore it.
- **Extension:** Add the third step on paper — "I can check by ___" — and plan when you
  would do it.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner ask a question about something noticed
  and offer a guess (prediction) they could check?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The drawn or scribed "I wonder… I think…" page.

## Home connection

At home, wonder about one thing out loud — "I wonder why the moon changes shape?" —
then tell a grown-up your guess. You can even plan how to check it together.
