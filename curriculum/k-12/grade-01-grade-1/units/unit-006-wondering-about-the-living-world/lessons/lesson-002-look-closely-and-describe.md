# Lesson 2 — Look Closely and Describe

## Summary

Learners practice the scientist's habit of looking closely — at objects, plants,
animals, and the weather — and naming what they notice with describing words. They
discover that careful observation finds details a quick glance misses.

## Objectives

- Use the senses to observe and describe objects, plants, animals, and weather.
  _(D06.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

A leaf is not just "a leaf." Look closely: it has veins like little roads, a smooth
top and a rough bottom, a color that changes with the light. The sky is not just "the
sky" — it might be blue, gray, or gold. When you look closely and say what you see,
hear, and feel, you are doing what a scientist does: **observing** and **describing**.

## Materials

- A set of things to observe closely (a leaf, a flower, a stone, a picture of an
  animal, and a look at the sky or weather)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather a leaf, a flower, and a stone (or local equivalents), a picture of an animal,
  and a place to look at the weather.
- Prepare a simple chart or space to collect describing words.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The heart is *slow looking* — noticing
details, not naming things correctly. There is no "right answer" for what a child
notices; praise noticing itself. If an animal is observed live (a bird, an insect),
model gentle watching: stay still, stay back, and never touch or chase — observe
without harming, and return any found creature gently where it came from. Honor every
body: a learner who cannot see describes by feel, sound, and smell; a learner who
does not speak points, gestures, or draws; a learner who moves differently may need
the object brought to them or a partner to describe with. Describing words differ
across languages and places — welcome words in the learners' home languages.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Yesterday you met your five senses. Today you will use them to
   look even closer. What is one thing you noticed on the way here today?
2. **Look closely together (10 min).** Look at the leaf (or flower or stone) as a
   group. Slow down. What color is it? What shape? Run a finger over it — is it
   smooth or bumpy? What does it smell like? Collect all the describing words.
3. **Describe on your own (20 min).** Choose one thing — an object, a plant, a picture
   of an animal, or the weather outside. Look closely and find three details. Say
   them or show them: "It is green and small." "It feels soft." "The sky is gray and
   the air feels cool."
4. **Draw one detail (7 min).** Draw your thing and its best detail — the veins in the
   leaf, the stripes on the animal.
5. **Close (3 min).** Looking closely finds what a quick glance misses. A scientist
   looks slowly, and then says what they see.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a describing-word frame: "It is ___ (color), it is ___ (shape), it
  feels ___."
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Describe by touch, sound, and smell; a partner reads
  the sky aloud ("the sun feels warm, the wind is moving").
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to the details and draw them; a partner says your
  describing words aloud.
- **Extension:** Compare two things and name one way they are alike and one way they
  are different.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner give at least two describing words for
  a chosen thing, using a sense?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "close-looking" drawing with its details.

## Home connection

At home, choose one thing — a spoon, a leaf, a pet — and look at it very slowly for
one minute. Tell a grown-up three details you had never noticed before.
