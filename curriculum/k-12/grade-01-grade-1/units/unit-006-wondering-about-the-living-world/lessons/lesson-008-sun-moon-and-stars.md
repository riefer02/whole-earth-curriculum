# Lesson 8 — Sun, Moon, and Stars

## Summary

Learners observe the patterns of the **Sun**, **Moon**, and **stars** and discover
that day turns into night because the Earth slowly turns — so the Sun only *seems* to
move across the sky. They honor many peoples' sky stories as stories, side by side,
and hold the big questions as wonder.

## Objectives

- Observe and describe patterns of the Sun, Moon, and stars, and how day turns into
  night. _(D06.S4.01.01)_

## Connection

Every morning the Sun *seems* to climb up into the sky, and every evening it *seems*
to sink down and the sky grows dark. The Moon sometimes looks round and full, and
sometimes like a thin smile. Stars sparkle in the night sky in the same quiet
patterns. People everywhere, for thousands of years, have looked up and wondered about
these lights — and told stories about them.

## Materials

- A ball or globe and a lamp or flashlight
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Set up the lamp or flashlight (the "Sun") and a ball (the "Earth").
- Prepare to tell one or two sky stories from different cultures, as stories.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Two commitments are non-negotiable
here. **First, no geocentrism:** day and night happen because the Earth slowly turns,
so one side faces the Sun (day) while the other faces away (night); the Sun only
*seems* to move across the sky. Use appearance language — "the Sun seems to rise and
set" — never "the Sun goes around the Earth." (This is the factual model taught by
NASA and space agencies worldwide; see the NASA Space Place resource below.) **Second,
inquiry over creed:** the origins of the Sun, Moon, and stars are held as wonder, not
dogma. Honor many peoples' sky stories side by side — an Indigenous story of the Sun,
a story about the Moon from Africa, Asia, or the Pacific, a Greek or Norse tale — as
*stories people tell*, without ranking any as more true. Never mock a family's
beliefs. Honor every body: describe the sky in words for a learner who cannot see it,
and let movement tell the story (the slow turning). Some questions — why the stars
are there — we cannot answer yet, and that is wonderful.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** What did the sky look like last night? What does it look like
   now? Look out the window or remember.
2. **The lights in the sky (10 min).** The **Sun** gives us light in the day. The
   **Moon** and **stars** we usually see at night. The Moon changes shape a little
   each night, over and over — a pattern. The stars stay in the same patterns night
   after night.
3. **Day turns into night (15 min).** Watch the lamp and ball. One side of the ball is
   in light — that is day. The other side is in dark — that is night. The Earth
   **turns** slowly, all the time. As it turns, your part of the Earth comes into the
   light (morning) and then leaves it (evening). The Sun does not really go around us;
   we turn, so the Sun only *seems* to rise and set. Act it out: one friend is the
   Sun, the rest turn slowly like the Earth, saying "day!" when you face the light and
   "night!" when you face away.
4. **Sky stories (10 min).** Listen to a story people have told about the Sun, Moon, or
   stars — from here and from far away. Every people has wondered at the sky and told
   a story about it. No story is the only story; we hold them side by side and keep
   wondering.
5. **Close (5 min).** Day and night follow a pattern because the Earth turns. The Sun,
   Moon, and stars have kept their patterns for a very long time — and people have
   always looked up and wondered.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Focus on the one idea "the Earth turns, so we get day and night" with
  the lamp-and-ball model.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the warm side and cool side of a lamp-lit ball,
  or turn a partner gently to feel "facing the Sun" and "facing away."
- **Support (motor/limited-movement):** Turn a ball or a partner's shoulders gently, or
  point with an arm, instead of spinning the whole body.
- **Extension:** Draw the day sky and the night sky and label one pattern in each (the
  Sun by day; the Moon or stars by night).

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner describe (in words or by acting) that
  the Earth turns, so day turns into night, and name one pattern of the Sun, Moon, or
  stars?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The day-sky/night-sky drawing.

## Resources

- BBC Bitesize (KS1 Science), "What is day and night?" — explains that the Sun is the
  star at the centre of our solar system, that Earth rotates, and that the Sun shines
  on only half of Earth at once, so as Earth turns some places face the Sun (daytime)
  and others face away (night-time).
  https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/topics/zkvv4wx/articles/zyfhxbk
- NASA Space Place, "What Causes the Seasons?" — states that Earth "spins around this
  pole, making one complete turn each day. That is why we have day and night," and
  explains that Earth's tilted axis makes the seasons.
  https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/

## Home connection

At home, watch the sky in the morning and again at night. Tell a grown-up what
changed and what stayed the same — and ask them if they know a story about the Sun,
Moon, or stars.
