# Lesson 1 — My Five Senses

## Summary

Learners discover that they carry the first tools of science in their own bodies:
seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, and touching. They use each sense to observe and
describe a few real things, learning that observing is how science begins.

## Objectives

- Use the five senses to observe and describe objects. _(D06.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

Every day, without thinking about it, you use your body to know the world: your eyes
see the color of a mango, your ears hear the rain, your nose smells warm bread, your
tongue tastes a fruit, your skin feels the sun. These are your **five senses** — and
they are the very first tools of a scientist, wherever you live.

## Materials

- A collection of safe objects to sense (a leaf, a shell, a smooth stone, a piece of
  fruit, a bell or rattle, a soft cloth)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather safe objects that can be touched, smelled, and (for one or two) tasted with
  adult guidance. Check first for allergies before any tasting.
- Set up the objects where learners can reach them, one sense at a time.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Safety first: never put anything in a mouth unless you have
checked it is clean and safe, and be alert to allergies and cultural food rules — the
"taste" step is optional and can be skipped entirely. Honor every body: a learner who
is blind or has low vision observes by hearing, smell, and touch; a learner who does
not use a speaking voice reports by pointing, touching, or acting; a learner with a
motor difference may need a partner to bring objects close. Remind learners gently
that senses differ from person to person — some people see differently, hear
differently, or feel differently — and every way of sensing is a valid way of knowing.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** How did you know what the weather is like today, before anyone
   told you? You looked, you listened, you felt the air. Your body told you.
2. **Name your five senses (10 min).** There are five ways your body knows the world:
   **see** (eyes), **hear** (ears), **smell** (nose), **taste** (tongue), and
   **touch** (skin). Say each one and point to the body part that does it — or touch
   it, if pointing is hard.
3. **Sense stations (20 min).** Move around the objects. For each one, ask: what does
   it tell my eyes? my ears? my nose? my skin? (Only taste the fruit if a grown-up
   says it is safe.) Say or show one thing each sense tells you: "It is smooth," "It
   smells sweet," "It makes a little ring."
4. **Draw what you sensed (7 min).** Draw one object and the sense you used to learn
   about it — an ear by the bell, a nose by the flower.
5. **Close (3 min).** Your senses are your science tools, and you carry them with you
   everywhere. Tomorrow we will use them to look even closer.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Introduce two senses at a time (touch and hear), with one object each.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Focus on hearing, smell, and touch; describe textures
  and sounds aloud with a partner.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Report each sense by pointing to the body part and the
  object, or by acting it out.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** Use seeing, touching, smelling, and tasting; feel a bell or
  drum's vibration and match each sense to its body part by pointing.
- **Support (motor/limited-movement):** Have a partner bring objects close; report by
  a nod, a point, or a chosen sign instead of moving between stations.
- **Extension:** Choose one object and write or draw all five things its senses tell —
  a full "sense report."

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name (or point to) the five senses and
  give one describing word using at least one of them?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** The "sense drawing" of one object and the sense used.

## Home connection

At home, close your eyes and listen for one full minute. Tell a grown-up every sound
you heard. That is your ears doing science.
