# Lesson 7 — How Objects Move

## Summary

Learners explore the ways objects move — **push**, **pull**, **roll**, and **slide**
— and discover that a push or a pull makes something **start**, and a hand, a wall, or
the ground makes it **stop**. They learn by moving real things and their own bodies.

## Objectives

- Describe how objects move (push, pull, roll, and slide) and what makes them start or
  stop. _(D06.S3.01.02)_

## Connection

You roll a ball and it moves away. You push a swing and it starts to go. You pull a
wagon and it follows you. A toy car rolls on its wheels; a box slides across the
floor. Whenever something starts to move, a **push** or a **pull** started it — and
when it stops, something (your hand, the ground, a wall) stopped it.

## Materials

- Objects that move in different ways (a ball, a toy car or wheeled object, a block, a
  smooth ramp or book)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Gather objects that roll (ball, car) and slide (block, book), and a ramp if
  available.
- Clear a floor space so things can move without bumping anyone.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is a **body-first** lesson: the
strongest learning here is learners pushing and pulling real things and feeling the
push/pull in their own muscles. Keep it safe and gentle — no throwing, no rough play;
a soft push is enough to learn the idea. Honor every body: a learner who uses a
wheelchair or has limited movement can push or pull with a hand, a foot, a stick, or a
partner's help — the point is the *idea*, not the strength. A learner who cannot see
feels the object start and stop; a learner who does not speak shows the movement with
hands or body. Introduce the word "force" only lightly if learners ask: "a push or a
pull is a force." The stop matters as much as the start — friction (the ground rubbing
against the object) is the quiet reason most things stop; save the word for later
grades, but let the observation land.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** How did you get here today? You moved! Now think about a ball:
   how does it move, and how do you make it start?
2. **Push and pull (10 min).** Hold a block. Give it a gentle **push** — it moves away
   from you. Now **pull** it back toward you. A push moves something away; a pull
   moves it toward you. Feel the push and pull in your hand.
3. **Roll and slide (15 min).** Try the ball and the block on the floor and down the
   ramp. The ball **rolls** (it turns over and over); the block **slides** (it moves
   flat along the ground). Which one rolls? Which one slides? What did you do to make
   each start? What made it stop — your hand, the wall, or the ground?
4. **Your body moves (7 min).** Be the object: roll like a ball (curl up and turn),
   slide like a block (move flat), then push and pull a partner's hands gently.
5. **Close (3 min).** Push and pull make things start; hands, walls, and the ground
   make things stop. Rolling and sliding are two ways things travel.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use two words only (push, pull) with one object, and act them out.
- **Support (motor difference):** Use a stick or a partner to push/pull; observe and
  describe rather than do if needed.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Feel the difference — a ball turns over in the hand
  (rolls) while a block stays flat (slides); feel the object start and stop under your
  hand.
- **Extension:** Change the ramp to make it steeper or flatter and tell what happens —
  does the object roll farther or stop sooner?

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner show or name a push and a pull, and say
  what made an object start or stop?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** A drawing of one object moving, labeled push, pull, roll,
  or slide.

## Home connection

At home, find one thing you can push and one thing you can pull. Roll a round thing
and slide a flat thing. Tell a grown-up what made each one start and stop.
