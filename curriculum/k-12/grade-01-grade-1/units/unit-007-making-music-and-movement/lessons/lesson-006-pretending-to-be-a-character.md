# Lesson 6 — Pretending to Be a Character

## Summary

Learners become characters in a simple story, showing who they are with **voice**,
**face**, and **body**. Pretending helps a child feel what another person feels. The
body and voice are the costume, and every character can be played many ways.

## Objectives

- Pretend to be a character in a simple story and use their voice, face, and body to
  show it. _(D07.S3.01.02)_

## Connection

When you play make-believe, you get to be someone else for a while — a cook, a
helper, a grandparent, a friend. People everywhere tell stories and act them out to
share ideas and understand each other. Your voice, your face, and your body are your
costume — you carry them with you always. Today you will become a character from a
story.

## Materials

- A simple story with a few characters
- A turn marker (a stone or cloth knot)
- Open floor space

## Preparation

- Choose or tell a short story with two or three clear, kind characters from any
  culture, told respectfully as a story to play with.
- Clear a space for acting; set out the turn marker and any cloths.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Choose a story whose characters are kind and recognizable, and
tell it as a story to play with — never co-opting sacred or ceremonial roles. Use a
neutral **turn marker** (a stone or cloth knot) rather than any single culture's
ceremonial object. Never push a shy learner to perform; watching and copying is full
participation, and a learner may join a group of actors instead of acting alone. A
learner who uses a wheelchair or moves with support plays the whole character from
where they are — a "walk" becomes a lean, a nod, or a hand gesture, and the face and
voice carry the character fully. A learner who does not speak shows the character with
face and body, or signs the character's key action. A learner who is blind or has low
vision receives the story by ear and plays the character fully with voice and body —
seeing is not needed to become the character. A learner who is Deaf or hard of hearing
receives the story through sign, gesture, or pictures and plays the character with
face, body, and movement. Celebrate every character; there
is no correct way to play one.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit and listen to a short story. Who is in it? What do they
   do? Pick a character you would like to be.
2. **Meet your character (10 min).** How does your character stand? How do they talk —
   fast, slow, high, low? Try their **voice**, their **face**, and their **body**.
   You might be a helper carrying something heavy, or a kind neighbor with a slow,
   gentle voice.
3. **Become the character (15 min).** Act a small part of the story as your
   character, using voice, face, and body together. Trade characters and try another
   one. Holding the turn marker means it is your turn to speak or act; passing it
   means a friend's turn.
4. **Show and guess (10 min).** Act your character for a friend. Can they guess who
   you are? Then tell them how it felt to be that person.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: pretending helps us feel what others feel. In every
   story there is a whole world of characters — and you can be any of them.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give one simple character and one action ("you are the helper carrying
  the basket"); let the learner copy.
- **Support (seated/adapted):** Play the whole character from a chair — face, voice,
  and hands; a "walk" becomes a lean or a nod, and that is full participation.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Show the character with face and body, or sign the key
  action.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Receive the story by ear and play the character with
  voice and body.
- **Support (Deaf/HH):** Receive the story through sign, gesture, or pictures and play
  the character with face, body, and movement.
- **Extension:** Invite learners to add a new character to the story and act how that
  character would join in.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner use voice, face, or body to show a
  character in the story?
- **Performance:** The learner's character, observed live (in any adapted form).

## Home connection

With someone at home, pick a person from a story or a memory and both pretend to be
them for a minute. Ask each other how it felt.
