# Lesson 4 — Joining Two Groups: Adding Within 20

## Summary

Learners discover that **adding** means joining two groups into one bigger group.
They join real objects, count the total, practice the "count on" strategy, and meet
the written number sentence with the plus and equals signs — always with real
things in hand before the symbols.

## Objectives

- Add and subtract within 20 using objects, drawings, or counting strategies, and
  explain what the operation means. _(D05.S1.01.02)_

## Connection

A fisher pours two nets of fish into one basket; a beader joins the blue beads and
the red beads she has threaded; a cook stirs two handfuls of beans into one pot. In
every one of these, two groups become one, and the new group is bigger than either
group alone. That joining is called **adding**, and people in every land do it every
day — even without ever writing a sign for it.

## Materials

- Two cups or trays of small objects (up to 10 in each)
- Paper or slate and a writing tool

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair two cups of objects and a writing surface.
- Plan to model one joining problem fully before learners try their own.
- Prepare a few joining story-starters ("Two fish ... then three more ...").

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). Adding is a *skill*, so teach it with
a **worked example** and guided practice before independent work (philosophy §13).
The one new idea is that two groups joined become one group whose total you can
count; the written signs (+, =) come *after* the objects, never before. Keep sums
within 20. Use the "count on" strategy as one tool, not the only one — some learners
count all, and that is fine; both are correct. The number sentence is a *record* of
what the hands did, not a rule to memorize. For learners who are Deaf or hard of
hearing, act out joining in sign or by moving objects; for learners who are blind or
low-vision, join two bowls of objects and count the total by touch and sound. A
learner who does not speak can show the total by pointing to a number card or
holding up that many fingers. Never rank learners by how fast they add; a correct
total, reached any way, is correct.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall counting (5 min).** You can count and say "how many." Today we
   learn what happens when two groups come together into one. That is called
   **adding**.
2. **Worked example — join two groups (10 min).** Watch and help: here is a cup with
   3 pebbles and a cup with 4 pebbles. Pour them together into one pile. Count them:
   1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Seven! Joining 3 and 4 makes 7.
3. **Meet the signs (5 min).** We can write what we just did: **3 + 4 = 7**. The
   plus sign **+** means "join." The equals sign **=** means "is the same as." Read
   it together: "three plus four equals seven."
4. **Count on (10 min).** Try a fast way: start with the bigger group and count on.
   With 5 beans and 3 beans, say "five," then count on — six, seven, eight. So
   5 + 3 = 8. Try "counting on" with your own two groups.
5. **Your turn — join and record (12 min).** Join your two cups, count the total,
   and write your number sentence. Check yourself: split the pile back into two
   groups and see if you get your starting numbers. Do they match?
6. **Close (3 min).** Adding is joining. Two groups become one, and the total is
   always there to count. Tomorrow we learn the opposite — taking away.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Keep both groups small (under 5 each) and count all the objects
  together; write the sentence only after the total is counted.
- **Support (motor):** A partner joins the objects while the learner counts and
  directs; use large, easy-to-grip objects.
- **Extension:** Write three different number sentences that all make the same total
  (for example, 4 + 4, 5 + 3, 6 + 2 all make 8), and join objects to prove each one.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner join two groups, find a total within
  20, and record it as a number sentence?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner explain in their own words that adding means
  joining two groups into one?

## Home connection

At home, join two groups of things — spoons and forks, or two piles of beans — and
tell a grown-up the total, then write the number sentence together.
