# Lesson 10 — Flat Shapes: Compose and Take Apart

## Summary

Learners discover that flat shapes have **sides** and **corners**, and that small
shapes can be **composed** (put together) into bigger shapes and **taken apart**
again — two triangles make a square, two squares make a rectangle. They build with
cut shapes and sticks, then trace the parts to see the whole.

## Objectives

- Compose and take apart two- and three-dimensional shapes and describe their sides,
  corners, and faces. _(D05.S3.01.01)_

## Connection

A tiler fits small square tiles into one big floor; a quilt maker sews triangles
into a square; a child breaks a cracker into two halves and sees how the pieces
once made a whole. From Chinese tangram puzzles to African patchwork and Islamic
tile mosaics, people everywhere build big shapes out of small ones — and take them
apart again. That is the geometry of making and unmaking.

## Materials

- Cut-out flat shapes (triangles, squares, rectangles) in several sizes
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Cut or gather flat shapes — triangles, squares, rectangles — in a few sizes.
- Prepare sticks or stems for building shapes with edges.
- Plan to model composing one shape and taking it apart.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It spirals up from Kindergarten's
"name and match shapes" to **composing and taking apart** shapes and describing
their **sides and corners** — the new skill. Teach with a **worked example**
(philosophy §13): place two triangles to make a square, then pull them apart again.
Use the language of sides and corners with real shapes in hand. Honor geometry from
many traditions — the tangram (China), patchwork quilts, and tile mosaics — without
ranking any. For learners who are blind or low-vision, use tactile shapes (cut from
thick card, felt, or wood) and trace sides and corners with the fingers; for
learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, name shapes in sign and count sides with
the hands. A learner who does not speak points to sides and corners or traces them.
Geometry is exploration, not a contest of speed.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall shapes (5 min).** Last year you named shapes — circles,
   squares, triangles. Today we learn their secret: shapes are made of **sides** and
   **corners**, and small shapes can join to make big ones.
2. **Worked example — compose a shape (10 min).** Watch and help: take two
   triangles. Put their straight edges together. What do you get? A **square**! Count
   its sides (four) and corners (four). Now pull the two triangles apart — the square
   is **taken apart** back into triangles.
3. **Build big shapes (12 min).** With your cut shapes, compose a bigger shape. Two
   squares make a rectangle; two triangles make a bigger triangle. Count the sides
   and corners of your new shape. What did you make, and what did you make it from?
4. **Take apart and trace (10 min).** Take your big shape apart into its pieces.
   Draw the big shape, then draw a line to show how it splits into its smaller
   shapes. Where did the parts fit?
5. **Build with sticks (5 min).** Lay sticks edge to edge to make a triangle, a
   square, and a rectangle on the ground. How many sticks is each side?
6. **Close (3 min).** Big shapes are made of small shapes, and every shape is made
   of sides and corners. To compose is to build; to take apart is to see how it was
   built.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Compose just one shape (two triangles into a square) and trace it;
  count sides and corners together.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use thick tactile shapes; "compose" by fitting
  raised pieces together and tracing their edges.
- **Extension:** Use a tangram set to compose several different shapes (a boat, a
  bird, a house) from the same seven pieces, and draw what you made.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner compose a bigger shape from small
  shapes, take it apart again, and describe its sides and corners?

## Home connection

At home, cut a paper square into two triangles and fit them back together, then show
a grown-up how the small shapes made the big one.
