# Lesson 15 — Coins and Buying a Snack

## Summary

The capstone of the unit. Learners identify coins and their values, then run a
gentle **market** to buy a snack — counting coins, adding the cost, checking they
have enough, and noticing the shapes, patterns, and a simple tally along the way.
All four strands of the unit come together in one real, shared activity, and money
is honored as one way among many that people meet their needs.

## Objectives

- Count, read, and write whole numbers up to 120 and use them to say how many
  objects are in a group. _(D05.S1.01.01)_
- Add and subtract within 20 using objects, drawings, or counting strategies, and
  explain what the operation means. _(D05.S1.01.02)_
- Identify, extend, and create repeating patterns using objects, sounds, or colors,
  and tell the rule of the pattern. _(D05.S2.01.01)_
- Find the missing number in a simple number sentence such as 3 + ? = 7 and explain
  how they know. _(D05.S2.01.02)_
- Compose and take apart two- and three-dimensional shapes and describe their sides,
  corners, and faces. _(D05.S3.01.01)_
- Measure and compare lengths using non-standard units such as hand spans or paper
  clips. _(D05.S3.01.02)_
- Collect and organize data into a simple chart or picture graph and answer
  questions about what it shows. _(D05.S4.01.01)_
- Tell and write time and identify the value of coins, using them in simple real
  situations such as buying a snack. _(D05.S4.01.02)_

## Connection

Everywhere people need things they do not make themselves, they have found a way to
trade. Long ago, people traded goods directly, or used things of shared value —
cowrie shells in Africa and the Pacific, cacao beans in Mesoamerica, salt, beads.
Today most places use **coins and notes** with an agreed value. A coin is a promise:
everyone agrees what it is worth, so it can be traded for a snack, a tool, or a
ticket. Today you will use coins the way people do every day, all over the world —
to buy something you want, fairly.

## Materials

- Coins or play coins in a few values (1, 5, and 10 — adjust to the learners'
  currency)
- Small "snack" items with simple prices (1–20)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Choose coins or make play tokens with agreed values (1, 5, 10 — adapt to the
  local currency, and never rank any country's money above another's).
- Set up a "market" table with simple items and price tags.
- Prepare a small chart to tally what everyone buys.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the unit's capstone — a
celebration, not a test. The goal is that each learner can identify a few coins by
their value and use them in a real "buying" exchange, while the four strands return
together. **Honor money as one system among many**: before coins, people traded
goods directly or used shells, cacao, salt, and beads; even today many exchanges are
barter, gift, and sharing. Use the learners' actual local currency where possible;
if using play tokens, keep values simple (1, 5, 10). Never rank currencies or treat
money as a measure of a person's worth — **having more coins is a fact, not a
worth.** Keep amounts within 20, and frame buying as fair exchange: the cost is
agreed, and you pay what is asked. For learners who are blind or low-vision, make
coins distinguishable by touch — size, edge, or texture — and count by feel and
sound; for learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, carry out the exchange in sign
or with written prices. A learner who does not speak buys by pointing, placing
coins, or using a communication device. Every learner buys and sells at their own
pace, and every exchange is honored.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — how do people trade? (5 min).** Think: how do people get a snack, a
   tool, or cloth? Some trade goods, some share, and many use **coins** — small
   pieces everyone agrees are worth something. Today we open a market!
2. **Meet the coins (8 min).** Look at your coins. What is each one worth? Say the
   values. Notice each coin's **shape** — round like a circle, or other shapes — and
   feel its edge. Sort your coins into groups by value, like you sorted objects long
   ago.
3. **Count what you have (8 min).** Count your coins by value, grouping into tens
   when you can. How much do you have in all? Write the number. This is your money
   for the market.
4. **Buy a snack (12 min).** Go to the market. Choose a snack and read its **cost**.
   Count out coins to pay for it — joining small values to make the cost, just like
   adding. Do you have **enough**? Pay the seller and check: is your count the same
   as the price?
5. **Make change — the missing number (7 min).** Sometimes you pay a bit more than
   the cost and get some back. If the snack costs 6 and you pay 10, how much comes
   back? Count on from 6 to 10 — four! That is the **missing number**: 6 + ? = 10.
6. **Tally the market (7 min).** As a group, tally what everyone bought — one mark
   per snack in a simple chart. Which snack was bought the **most**? Which the
   **fewest**?
7. **Close (3 min).** You counted, added, found missing numbers, used shapes, and
   read a chart — all to buy a snack fairly. Math is not a gate; it is how people
   meet their needs together, all over the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use only one coin value (all "1s") and buy items costing under 10;
  count together, touching each coin.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use coins distinguishable by touch and count by
  feel and sound; a partner reads the price aloud.
- **Extension:** Run a shop where *you* sell, make change for a customer, and check
  your own work; or price items so two coins can buy them and write the number
  sentences.

## Assessment

- **Summative (performance/observation):** Can the learner identify coins by value,
  count out the cost of a snack, and check the exchange, drawing on counting,
  adding, and missing-number reasoning from the unit?
- **Artifact (portfolio):** A record of the learner's market work — their prices,
  their purchases, or their tally — kept as a capstone of the unit.

## Home connection

At home, look at the coins your family uses, say each one's value, and (with a
grown-up) "buy" a small snack or item together, counting the coins out loud.
