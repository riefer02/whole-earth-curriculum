# Lesson 8 — Creating Patterns and Telling the Rule

## Summary

Learners become pattern makers. They create their own repeating patterns from
objects, sounds, and movements, then put the pattern into words — the **rule** — and
translate a pattern from one medium into another (sound to color, movement to
objects), discovering that the rule stays the same even when the materials change.

## Objectives

- Identify, extend, and create repeating patterns using objects, sounds, or colors,
  and tell the rule of the pattern. _(D05.S2.01.01)_

## Connection

A beadworker doesn't just copy patterns — she invents them and can tell you her rule:
"two red, one gold." A musician makes a rhythm and can tap its rule on her knee. A
tile maker designs a new mosaic and can say how it goes. To *make* a pattern and
*tell its rule* is to be an author of order — and the same rule can be carried from
beads into claps into colors, because the rule is the pattern's heart, not its
material.

## Materials

- Collections of objects in two or three kinds
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Prepare collections with two or three clearly different kinds or colors.
- Plan to model creating a pattern and stating its rule in words.
- Clear space for movement patterns.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It is the creative side of pattern
work: the learner makes the pattern and puts its rule into words. Model one example
— create, then state the rule — as a **worked example** (philosophy §13). The new,
powerful idea is **translation**: a rule like "red, blue, blue" can be clapped as
"loud, soft, soft" and stepped as "hop, step, step" — same rule, different material.
Celebrate every original pattern; there is no pattern too simple or too strange, and
creativity is never ranked. For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, use visual
and movement patterns and state the rule in sign; for learners who are blind or
low-vision, create sound and touch patterns and tell the rule aloud. A learner who
does not speak tells the rule by pointing, arranging cards, or demonstrating it.
Every learner's pattern is a work of art and reason.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall the core (5 min).** Yesterday you found the repeating part of a
   pattern — its **core**. Today you become the maker: you create the pattern and
   tell its **rule**.
2. **Worked example — create and name the rule (10 min).** Watch and help: choose
   objects and build a pattern — shell, pebble, shell, pebble. Now say its rule in
   words: "shell, pebble — and it repeats." The **rule** is the pattern told in
   words.
3. **Create your own (12 min).** Build your own repeating pattern with your objects.
   Make the core clear, then extend it. When it is done, tell its rule in words:
   "two seeds, one leaf, and it repeats."
4. **Translate your pattern (10 min).** Now say your rule a different way. If your
   rule is "red, blue," clap it — loud, soft. If your rule is "stone, leaf," step it
   — hop, step. The rule stays the same; only the material changes.
5. **Trade and check (5 min).** Show your pattern to a friend. Can they find your
   rule and say it back? Then try theirs. Agreeing on a rule is how you know your
   pattern is clear.
6. **Close (3 min).** You are a pattern author. You make the pattern, you say the
   rule, and you can carry that rule into any material — just as makers do all over
   the world.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Create one simple two-part pattern and say the rule together; accept
  the rule spoken, signed, or shown.
- **Support (motor):** Create patterns by voice or by directing a partner to place
  objects; the rule is the learner's own.
- **Extension:** Create a three-part pattern, or make two different patterns with the
  *same* rule and show how they match.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner create a repeating pattern and state
  its rule in words (or sign)?
- **Peer assessment:** Can a partner identify and restate the learner's rule?

## Home connection

At home, create a pattern with anything you find — cups, spoons, socks — and teach a
grown-up its rule, then see if they can clap the same rule.
