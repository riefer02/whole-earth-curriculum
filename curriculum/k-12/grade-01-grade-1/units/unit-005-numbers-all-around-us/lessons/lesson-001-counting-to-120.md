# Lesson 1 — Counting to 120

## Summary

Learners review counting to twenty, then stretch all the way to one hundred twenty.
They discover that counting becomes easier when they group objects into tens and
count by tens — ten, twenty, thirty — and then count on the leftovers. Counting is
treated as a rhythm everyone can join, never a race.

## Objectives

- Count, read, and write whole numbers up to 120 and use them to say how many
  objects are in a group. _(D05.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

A market seller counts her fruit so she knows how many she has; a shepherd counts
his animals as they come home; a fisher counts the fish in the net. Long before
written numbers, people in many lands counted with their bodies — fingers, toes,
and knuckles — and many still do. Ten fingers make a group of ten, and counting by
tens is how people in many lands keep a long count from getting lost — though some
peoples counted in other groups, such as twenties, and every way works for the
people who use it.

## Materials

- A collection of about 120 small objects (pebbles, seeds, beans, buttons)
- Ten small cups, bags, or squares of cloth for groups of ten
- String, rubber bands, or strips of grass for bundling

## Preparation

- Give each learner or pair about 120 objects, ten holders, and bundling ties.
- Clear space to move, bundle, and count aloud.
- Prepare (or plan to draw) a simple count-by-tens chant to say together.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. The leap from twenty to one hundred twenty is large, so keep
the tone playful and unhurried. **There is no such thing as "too slow" at counting,
and no learner's speed measures their smartness.** Count in the home language; number
words differ across languages and all are correct. Model the one new skill —
bundling ten and counting by tens — as a worked example before learners try it on
their own (philosophy §13). For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, count in
sign or by tapping each bundle; for learners who are blind or low-vision, count by
moving objects into cups so the number grows by feel and sound. Learners with limited
hand or body movement count by voice or sign and direct a partner to move each
object. Do not rank learners by how high they can count; mastery arrives at its own
pace for each child.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — remember twenty (5 min).** Last year you counted to twenty. Count to
   twenty right now — with your fingers, your voice, or in sign. Today we go much
   further: all the way to one hundred twenty.
2. **Worked example — bundle a ten (8 min).** Watch and help: take ten objects and
   put them in one cup (or tie ten sticks into one bundle). Say "ten." Make another
   group of ten. Now you have two tens — "twenty." We count by tens the way a drummer
   keeps a steady beat.
3. **Guided practice — build tens together (15 min).** Make as many groups of ten as
   you can from your collection. Count them together: ten, twenty, thirty, forty,
   fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety, one hundred. Then keep going — one hundred
   ten, one hundred twenty.
4. **Count on the leftovers (10 min).** Now count the objects that did not fit a full
   ten. If you have one hundred ten and four leftovers, count on: one hundred eleven,
   one hundred twelve, one hundred thirteen, one hundred fourteen. Say your total.
5. **Check with a partner (5 min).** Trade collections. Count your friend's bundles
   and leftovers. Do you both get the same number? If not, count again together —
   kindly. A second count that matches is a sign you counted well.
6. **Close (2 min).** Remember: a long count is just tens, counted over and over,
   with a few left over. Counting by tens is a power you now carry.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Count only to one hundred today; introduce one hundred ten and one
  hundred twenty another day, echoing each decade number.
- **Support (motor):** Bundle with larger, easy-to-grip objects, or have a partner
  move objects while the learner counts and directs.
- **Extension:** Count backward from one hundred twenty by tens, or start counting at
  a number a friend says (for example, "start at forty") and go on.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner group into tens and count by tens to
  one hundred twenty, then count on the leftovers to say the total?
- **Formative (oral):** Can the learner say the total after counting a collection
  close to one hundred twenty?

## Home connection

At home, gather a pile of beans, stones, or buttons, make groups of ten with a
grown-up, and count them by tens to see how many there are.
