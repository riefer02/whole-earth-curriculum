# Lesson 11 — Solid Shapes: Faces, Sides, and Corners

## Summary

Learners meet solid, three-dimensional shapes — cubes, spheres, cylinders, cones,
and boxes — and learn to find their **faces** (flat parts), **sides** (edges), and
**corners** (vertices) by feeling real objects and tracing their faces. They connect
solid shapes to the flat shapes they already know.

## Objectives

- Compose and take apart two- and three-dimensional shapes and describe their sides,
  corners, and faces. _(D05.S3.01.01)_

## Connection

A ball you can roll is a **sphere**; a box of tea is a box-shaped solid; a cooking
pot is a **cylinder**; the roof of a hut or tent can be a **cone**. Solid shapes are
the shapes that fill space — they have a flat part you can trace (a **face**), an
edge you can run your finger along (a **side**), and a pointy or square meeting place
(a **corner**). Builders, potters, and basket makers in every land work with solid
shapes every day.

## Materials

- Solid shapes or everyday objects (a block, a ball, a can, a cone, a box)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Gather solid shapes or everyday objects for each learner or pair.
- Have flat shapes from the last lesson ready to compare.
- Plan to model feeling and counting faces, sides, and corners on one solid.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It extends the last lesson's flat
shapes into **solids**, and the new skill is finding and counting **faces, sides
(edges), and corners (vertices)**. Teach with a **worked example** (philosophy §13):
feel a cube, count its six square faces, twelve edges, and eight corners. Connect
each solid to a flat shape — the cube's face is a square, the cylinder's face is a
circle. Use everyday objects (boxes, balls, cans, clay) so the lesson is universally
available. For learners who are blind or low-vision, this lesson is naturally rich —
the hands are the tool; pass each solid and let the learner feel faces, edges, and
corners, and use a "feely bag." For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, name
shapes in sign and count features with the hands. A learner who does not speak
traces or points to each feature. Solid shapes are felt, not just seen — every body
can study them.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall flat shapes (5 min).** Yesterday you built flat shapes with
   sides and corners. Today we meet shapes you can *hold* — solid shapes that fill
   space, like a ball or a box.
2. **Worked example — feel a cube (10 min).** Watch and help: take a block (a
   **cube**). Run your finger over its flat part — that is a **face**; a cube has six
   faces, each one a square. Feel where two faces meet — that is a **side** (edge).
   Feel where sides meet — that is a **corner**. Count them: six faces, twelve sides,
   eight corners.
3. **Feel each solid (12 min).** Hold each shape: a ball (**sphere** — no flat faces,
   no corners; it rolls), a can (**cylinder** — two circle faces and one curved side),
   a cone (**one** circle face, one point), a box (**rectangular prism** — six
   rectangle faces). Count each one's faces, sides, and corners.
4. **Trace a face (8 min).** Set a solid on paper and trace one of its faces. What
   flat shape did you draw? A cube's face traces a square; a cylinder's face traces a
   circle. The flat face is the bridge between solid and flat shapes.
5. **Find solids around you (5 min).** Look around the room or outdoors. Point to
   one sphere, one cube or box, one cylinder, and one cone. Name its faces, sides,
   and corners.
6. **Close (3 min).** Solid shapes fill the world — and every solid can be described
   by its faces, sides, and corners. Now you can see and feel that structure.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Study just two solids (a cube and a ball); count one feature at a
  time, hand-over-hand if needed.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Use a feely bag to identify each solid by touch and
  count its features with the fingers.
- **Extension:** Count and record the faces, sides, and corners of every solid in a
  simple table, and find a solid with a feature no other has.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner identify a solid shape and describe
  its faces, sides, and corners by feel or sight?

## Home connection

At home, go on a "solid shape hunt" with a grown-up: find a ball, a box, a can, and
a cone, and count the faces, sides, and corners of each together.
