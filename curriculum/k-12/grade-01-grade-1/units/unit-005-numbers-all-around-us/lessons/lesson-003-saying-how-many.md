# Lesson 3 — Saying How Many

## Summary

Learners discover that when you count a group, the last number you say is the
answer to "how many." They practice counting collections of different sizes, saying
the total, and comparing two groups with "more," "fewer," and "the same," making a
guess (an estimate) before they count.

## Objectives

- Count, read, and write whole numbers up to 120 and use them to say how many
  objects are in a group. _(D05.S1.01.01)_

## Connection

"How many?" is one of the oldest questions people ask. A cook wants to know if she
has enough eggs; a child wants to know if she has more shells than her friend; a
farmer wants to know how many seeds he planted. The answer to "how many" is not a
feeling — it is a number you can find by counting, and the last number you say is
the total. Counting turns "a lot" or "a little" into something everyone can agree
on.

## Materials

- Two or three collections of small objects in different sizes
- Two cups or trays for comparing counted groups

## Preparation

- Prepare collections of different sizes: some under 20, some larger, up to 120.
- Give each learner or pair two cups or trays for side-by-side comparison.
- Plan to model estimating ("I think about 40") before counting.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one idea that makes counting
*meaningful* is **cardinality** — the last number said names the whole group — and
it is best taught with a worked example, then practiced with real objects. Always
pair "how many" with a real answer, and treat *estimating* as a respected thinking
move, not a wrong answer: "I think about 40 — let's count and see." Comparing groups
teaches that **more and fewer are facts about quantities, never about a person's
worth.** For learners who are Deaf or hard of hearing, count in sign or by touching
each object; for learners who are blind or low-vision, move objects one by one into
a cup so the count grows by feel and sound. A learner who does not speak can show
the total by holding up a number card, tapping that many times, or pointing. No
ranking by speed or by how high a learner can count; a correct total is correct
however it was reached.

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall tens and ones (5 min).** You can count to one hundred twenty
   and write the numbers. Now we answer the big question: **how many?**
2. **Worked example — the last number is the total (8 min).** Watch and help: count
   a pile together, touching each thing — one, two, three ... seven. The last number
   you said, **seven**, is how many there are. Say it: "There are seven."
3. **Estimate first (5 min).** Before you count your next pile, make a guess — an
   **estimate** — about how many it is. Estimates are not right or wrong; they are
   your thoughtful first guess. Say or sign your estimate.
4. **Count and say how many (12 min).** Count each of your collections, touching
   each thing once. Say the last number as your answer: "There are ___." Label the
   pile with a number card if you have one.
5. **Compare two groups (10 min).** Put two counted groups side by side. Which has
   **more**? Which has **fewer**? Are they **the same**? Count again to be sure —
   a careful recount is how you check yourself.
6. **Close (5 min).** Share: what was your biggest total today? Remember, "how many"
   always has a real answer, and counting is how you find it — for everyone, at any
   speed.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use piles under 10 first, then under 20; say the total together and
  touch each object while counting.
- **Support (motor):** A partner moves each object while the learner counts by voice
  or sign, or indicates each item with eye-gaze or a pointer.
- **Extension:** Compare three groups and order them from fewest to most, or write
  the number sentence "23 is more than 19."

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner touch each object once, say the last
  number as the total, and compare two groups correctly with "more," "fewer," or
  "the same"?
- **Self-assessment:** Can the learner recount a group and notice whether the two
  counts match?

## Home connection

At home, ask "how many?" about three different things — spoons, shoes, books — and
count each one with a grown-up, then say which has more.
