# Lesson 12 — Measuring and Comparing Lengths

## Summary

Learners discover that to measure length you lay equal-size units end to end and
count them, using **hand spans**, **footsteps**, and same-size objects. They compare
lengths with "longer," "shorter," and "the same," and discover why the units must
match — a lesson in fairness and agreement as much as in measuring.

## Objectives

- Measure and compare lengths using non-standard units such as hand spans or paper
  clips. _(D05.S3.01.02)_

## Connection

Long before rulers, people measured with their own bodies: the **cubit** (elbow to
fingertip) in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, the **foot** in Rome, the **hand span**
in many lands, the **pace** everywhere people walk. A fisher measures her net, a
builder measures his beam, a weaver measures her cloth. The secret is always the
same: choose a unit, lay it end to end, and count. That is how everyone, everywhere,
has made length something two people can agree on.

## Materials

- Non-standard measuring units (paper clips, same-size sticks, beans)
- Objects to measure (a book, a pencil, a strip of cloth, a stick)
- Paper or slate and a drawing tool

## Preparation

- Gather same-size units and several objects of different lengths.
- Clear floor space for measuring with footsteps.
- Plan to model measuring one object, laying units end to end with no gaps.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). The one big idea is **unit iteration**
— laying equal units end to end, with no gaps or overlaps, and counting them. Teach
it as a **worked example** (philosophy §13), then let learners measure and compare.
The historical fact that many cultures used body measures (cubit, foot, hand span)
is offered as shared human heritage, not as a reason to rank any system. A wonderful,
natural discovery is that **units must match** — one person's hand span differs from
another's, so we agree on one unit to be fair; this is egalitarianism made concrete.
For learners who are blind or low-vision, measuring by touch is ideal — lay sticks or
count hand spans along an object and count by feel; for learners who are Deaf or hard
of hearing, measure and count in sign or by touch. A learner who does not speak shows
the count on fingers or by placing a number card. Everyone measures with their own
body, and no body's measure is "wrong."

## Procedure

1. **Gather — recall shapes and building (5 min).** You have built and described
   shapes. Today we measure how *long* things are — a skill people have used since
   before written numbers.
2. **Worked example — measure with a unit (10 min).** Watch and help: lay paper
   clips (or sticks) end to end along a pencil, touching but not overlapping. Count
   them: one, two, three ... six. The pencil is **six paper clips long**. Say it:
   "The pencil is six paper clips long."
3. **Measure with your body (10 min).** Try your **hand span**: the distance from
   your thumb to your little finger when your hand is open. How many hand spans long
   is your book? Now use **footsteps**: how many steps across the room? Count aloud
   as you go.
4. **Measure and record (10 min).** Measure each of your objects with one kind of
   unit. Write or draw each length. Which object is **longer**? Which is **shorter**?
   Are any **the same length**?
5. **Compare with a friend (7 min).** You and a friend measure the same object. Did
   you get the same number? If you used your own hand spans, maybe not — your hands
   are different sizes! This is why people agree on one shared unit: so measuring is
   fair for everyone.
6. **Close (3 min).** To measure is to lay equal units end to end and count. To
   compare is to ask: longer, shorter, or the same? And a shared unit keeps measuring
   honest.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Measure one object with one unit and count together; use big, easy
  units like whole hand spans.
- **Support (motor):** A partner lays the units while the learner counts and directs;
  use large, grippable units.
- **Extension:** Measure the same object with two different units (hand spans and
  paper clips) and explain why the numbers differ, or order three objects from
  shortest to longest.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner measure a length by laying equal
  units end to end and counting them, and compare two lengths with "longer,"
  "shorter," or "the same"?

## Home connection

At home, measure the length of a bed, a table, or a room with your hand spans or
footsteps, and tell a grown-up which is longer and which is shorter.
