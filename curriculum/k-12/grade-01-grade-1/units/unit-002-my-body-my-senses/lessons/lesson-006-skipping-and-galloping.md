# Lesson 6 — Skipping and Galloping

## Summary

Learners learn two big traveling movements — skipping and galloping — and practice
them to a steady beat, moving from one mark to the next with balance and control.
Every body joins in its own way, with seated and adapted versions offered alongside.

## Objectives

- Perform skipping and galloping with balance and control. _(D03.S2.01.01)_

## Connection

All over the world, people move to music — dancing, skipping, and stepping in time.
Skipping and galloping are two ways your body can travel while keeping a beat. They
grow your balance and control, and they feel joyful — like a song your whole body
sings.

## Materials

- A beat source (clapping, drum, or song)
- Cones, chalk, or rope to mark a path

## Preparation

- Mark a starting line and a clear path with cones, chalk, or rope.
- Clear enough space for everyone to move without bumping.
- Prepare a steady beat to clap, drum, or play.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you") so it works in a classroom and for a
self-directed learner. Prepare the space and beat, then model each movement slowly
before the learners try. This is movement, not a race — celebrate control, rhythm,
and joy, never speed or distance. Honor every body: a learner who uses a wheelchair
can skip and gallop with their arms and wheels; a learner who needs support can do a
seated or step version. Model the adapted versions as real choices, not second
choices, so no one is left watching. Keep a visual beat going too — a bouncing hand or
a partner tapping — so learners who are deaf or hard of hearing can move in time.
Invite everyone to rest and go at their own pace; breath and joy matter more than
speed, especially for learners with breathing differences.

## Procedure

1. **Warm up (5 min).** March in place to the beat, lifting your knees. Shake out
   your arms and legs. Your body is awake and ready.
2. **Learn to skip (10 min).** A skip is a step and a hop on the same foot, then
   step-hop on the other foot: step-hop, step-hop. Try it slowly at first, arms
   swinging to help you balance.
3. **Learn to gallop (10 min).** A gallop is like a horse: one foot leads in front,
   and the back foot chases it — step-together, step-together. Try leading with one
   foot, then switch to lead with the other.
4. **Move to the beat (15 min).** From the starting mark, skip to the other mark,
   then gallop back. Keep the beat with your feet. If you use wheels, push and glide
   in the same rhythm; if you are seated, do the skip and gallop with your arms
   swinging to the beat.
5. **Close (5 min).** Slow your body down and catch your breath. You can skip and
   gallop now — two new ways to move with balance and control.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Practice the gallop first (step-together is easier to keep) before
  moving to the skip; clap the beat slowly.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to skip in a circle or gallop in a curving path,
  keeping the beat and control.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner skip and gallop with a steady rhythm
  and control, in their own body's way?

## Home connection

Teach someone at home to skip or gallop, and move to a beat together.
