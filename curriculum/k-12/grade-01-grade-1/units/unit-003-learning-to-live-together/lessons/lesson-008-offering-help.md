# Lesson 8 — Offering Help

## Summary

Learners practice the two steps of offering help: notice that someone is struggling,
then offer kindly before being asked. They learn that help should be offered, not
forced, and that "no, thank you" is always an okay answer.

## Objectives

- Offer help to a classmate who is struggling, without being asked. _(D02.S4.01.01)_

## Connection

Sometimes a friend does not ask for help — maybe they are shy, or they think they
should manage alone. When you notice and offer first, you give a gift before it is
requested. Offering help is how care becomes action between people.

## Materials

- "Struggle" scene cards (heavy bag, stuck zipper, tricky puzzle, out-of-reach shelf)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Arrange a circle for gathering and open floor space for acting.
- Set out the scene cards.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is about mutual aid, not pity or rescuing. Teach the difference between
offering help ("Can I help you?") and taking over (grabbing the bag). Honor "no,
thank you" — consent matters even in helping. Emphasize that anyone can need help and
anyone can give it; helping is not a sign of weakness. Read the procedure aloud;
model a gentle offer.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Think: have you ever struggled with something
   and wished someone would notice?
2. **Notice the struggle (10 min).** Look at the scene cards. A struggle is when
   someone is trying and it is hard. Say what you notice: "I see someone trying to
   carry a heavy bag."
3. **Offer, don't take over (15 min).** Practice the kind offer: "Can I help you with
   that?" Offer, then wait for an answer. Do not grab or do it for them. Practice
   hearing "yes, please" and "no, thank you" — both are fine.
4. **Help practice (10 min).** In pairs, one partner acts out a struggle, the other
   notices and offers help. Swap roles.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: anyone can need help, and anyone can give it. Notice
   first, offer kindly, and let the other person choose.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Give a scripted two-line exchange to practice; model waiting for an
  answer. A learner with limited movement can name, point to, or sign a kind offer
  while a partner acts it out — the offer is what matters, not the lifting.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to practice offering help to an adult in a respectful
  way, and discuss when adults need help too.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner notice a struggle and offer help
  before being asked, and wait for the answer?
- **Peer (partner check):** Partners name one kind offer they heard.
