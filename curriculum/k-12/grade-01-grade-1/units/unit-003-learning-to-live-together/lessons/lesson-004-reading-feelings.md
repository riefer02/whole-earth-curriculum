# Lesson 4 — Reading Feelings

## Summary

Learners discover that feelings show up in three places — the face, the voice, and
the body — and practice reading those clues to guess how a classmate feels. They
learn that a guess is a guess, and checking it by asking is the kind, careful step.

## Objectives

- Guess how a classmate feels by reading their face, voice, or body.
  _(D02.S2.01.01)_

## Connection

People show feelings even before they say a word: a frown, a quiet voice, tight
shoulders. When you learn to read these clues, you become a better friend, because you
can notice how someone feels and ask if they are okay.

## Materials

- Feeling face cards (happy, sad, angry, scared, calm)
- Paper and crayons

## Preparation

- Arrange a circle for gathering.
- Set out the feeling face cards where all can see them.

## Facilitator note

Empathy is a skill children build by noticing others (CASEL's relationship and
social-awareness competencies). Keep guessing light and playful, and always add the
safety step: a guess must be checked, because faces can fool us. A learner may carry
feelings this lesson touches; keep the tone warm and give space. No learner needs to
see faces to read feelings: voice and body clues — and asking — are a complete,
non-visual path. For a learner with low vision, lead with voice and body and describe
the faces aloud. Read the procedure aloud; model each feeling with face, voice, and
body.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Think: how do you know when a friend is happy
   without them telling you?
2. **Three clues (10 min).** Feelings show up in three places: your face (a smile, a
   frown), your voice (loud, soft, shaky), and your body (jumping, curled up, arms
   crossed). Meet the feeling cards and say each feeling in all three ways.
3. **Feeling charades (15 min).** One person picks a feeling and shows it with face,
   voice, and body — no words. The others guess. Then the actor says if the guess was
   right.
4. **Check your guess (10 min).** Guess is a guess — faces can fool us. Practice the
   kind question: "Are you feeling sad?" Ask it with a soft voice and wait for the
   answer.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: to understand a friend, look, listen, and ask. Reading
   feelings takes all three.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Limit to two or three feelings; model each clue one at a time. For a
  learner with low vision, use voice and body only and say the face clue aloud (e.g.,
  "her mouth is smiling").
- **Extension:** Have learners show the same feeling two different ways (a quiet sad
  and a loud sad) and discuss how feelings are not all one shape.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner point to the face, voice, or body clue
  that reveals a feeling?
- **Peer (guess-and-check):** Did the learner check their guess by asking the actor?
