# Lesson 2 — Listening Without Interrupting

## Summary

Learners build a "listening body" — a body that shows a speaker they are being
heard — and practice letting a classmate finish speaking before they talk. They
notice the urge to interrupt and learn a way to hold their thought until it is their
turn.

## Objectives

- Listen without interrupting while a classmate is speaking. _(D02.S1.01.02)_

## Connection

When you let a friend finish speaking, you show that their words matter. Listening is
caring with your ears or your eyes and your whole body — there is more than one way to
listen. It is a skill, not just something that happens — and like any skill, it gets
better with practice.

## Materials

- A listening stone (or any small object)
- Story cards or a short picture book

## Preparation

- Arrange a circle for gathering.
- Choose a short "tell me about..." topic for each pair (a pet, a game, a meal).

## Facilitator note

Interrupting is normal at this age; it is excitement, not rudeness. Teach the
"noticing thought" gently: when a comment wants to burst out, the learner can hold a
finger, tap their knee, or keep it "in their pocket" until the speaker finishes. Do
not shame interruptions; celebrate the catching of them. For the eyes, honor each
learner's culture: in many traditions, looking down or to the side while listening is
the respectful way, and that is a real listening body too. Never insist on "eyes on
the speaker." Honor every way of listening and speaking: for a learner who is Deaf or
hard of hearing, watching the speaker's face and hands *is* listening, and "ears open"
is not required. For a learner who does not speak with their mouth, their "voice" may
be sign, a gesture, a picture card, or a device — and their "listening body" keeps
their hands free, not "still." Any way a learner listens or speaks is a real listening
body and a real turn. Read the procedure aloud and model the listening body yourself.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** Sit in a circle. Think: what does it feel like when someone
   lets you finish talking all the way?
2. **Build a listening body (10 min).** Make a listening body together: turn your body
   toward the speaker, open your ears — or your eyes, watching their face and hands.
   Keep your mouth quiet, or let your hands sign if signing is your way to speak. For
   the eyes: different
   families teach different ways. Some teach "look at the speaker"; some teach that
   looking down or to the side is the respectful way to listen. Both are real
   listening bodies. Use the way your family and culture teach. Notice what each part
   of your body does.
3. **Meet the urge to interrupt (10 min).** Think of a time a thought wanted to burst
   out of your mouth while a friend was talking. Practice a quiet way to hold it: tap
   your knee, or keep the thought "in your pocket" until the speaker is done.
4. **Partner listening (15 min).** One partner holds the listening stone and shares
   about the topic — with their voice, with sign, or by pointing to pictures. The other
   partner listens with a listening body, holding their thoughts. When the speaker is
   done, swap.
5. **Close (5 min).** Remember: everyone's words matter. Waiting to speak is a gift
   you give to the person talking.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Shorten speaking turns (one sentence each); give a gentle hand signal
  for "hold that thought." For a Deaf or hard-of-hearing learner, seat them where they
  can clearly see the speaker's face and hands; for a non-speaking learner, offer
  picture cards or sign to share their turn.
- **Extension:** Ask learners to retell one thing their partner said — a first step
  toward showing they really listened.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Does the learner let a partner finish a sentence or
  idea before speaking?
- **Peer (partner check):** Partners give a friendly thumbs-up when they felt listened
  to.
