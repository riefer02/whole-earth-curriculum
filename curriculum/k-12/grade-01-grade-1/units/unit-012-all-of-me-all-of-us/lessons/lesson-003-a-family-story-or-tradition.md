# Lesson 3 — A Family Story or Tradition

## Summary

Learners revisit the opening unit's family story or tradition and now tell it with a
year of growth behind them — naming what it means to them. Sharing is by invitation,
never a requirement: a learner may tell it, show it, act it, draw it, point to it, or
pass, and every family's shape and story is honored.

## Objectives

- Name a family story or tradition and tell what it means to them. _(D01.S2.01.02)_

## Connection

Every family carries stories and traditions — a special meal, a song, a holiday, a
way of saying hello, a tale told again and again. A **tradition** is something a
family passes down, like a treasure. These things belong to *your* family, whatever
shape your family takes — one grown-up, two, grandparents, a foster family, chosen
family, or many generations under one roof. People everywhere keep traditions, and
each one is different and worth telling.

## Materials

- A share-a-bag for each learner (or any small container)
- Drawing paper and crayons
- Chart paper for listing traditions

## Preparation

- Send home (or explain) the share-a-bag: each learner brings one small object that
  stands for a family story or tradition.
- Set out drawing supplies and chart paper.
- Arrange a sharing circle.

## Facilitator note

This lesson is written to the learner ("you"). It **spirals back** to the opening
unit's family-story lesson and deepens it: learners now say what the tradition *means
to them*. Honor every family shape — one caregiver, two, grandparents, guardians, foster
family, siblings, chosen family, several generations under one roof; there is **no
correct family**. Sharing is **by invitation, never a requirement**: a learner may pass, or
draw, point, or act instead of speaking — each of these counts as sharing. Keep the
circle warm and non-competitive; there is no ranking of families or traditions. If a
learner cannot name one — or has no family story to share, or prefers not to — offer
gentle examples (a bedtime routine, a birthday way, a favorite food) or a class,
school, or community tradition; a pass is always a full share.

## Procedure

1. **Gather (5 min).** At the start of the year you shared a family story or
   tradition. Think again, now with a year of knowing yourself: what does your family
   do that is special to you?
2. **Choose one (10 min).** Pick one story or tradition. It can be big, like a
   holiday, or small, like a bedtime song. Draw it, or hold up the object you brought
   that stands for it.
3. **Share by invitation (15 min).** Share only if you want to — sharing is an
   invitation, never a must. Tell it, show the object, act it out, or point to your
   drawing. Listeners ask one gentle question each ("Who taught it to you?" "When do
   you do it?"). A learner may pass, draw, or point instead of speaking; that counts
   as sharing.
4. **What it means (10 min).** Say or draw one thing this tradition means to you:
   "This means ___ to me because ___."
5. **Close (5 min).** There is no correct family and no better tradition. Every
   family's story is a treasure, and yours is worth telling — and so is everyone
   else's.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** Use a picture prompt; scribe the learner's "what it means" sentence.
- **Support (non-speaking):** Point to a drawing or act the tradition; a partner
  voices the words.
- **Support (blind/low-vision):** Tell the tradition aloud or bring a touchable
  object; a partner describes a drawing.
- **Extension:** Ask a family member where the tradition began and share one new
  detail.

## Assessment

- **Formative (observation):** Can the learner name one family story or tradition and
  say something it means to them?
- **Performance:** The learner shares their tradition with the group (by telling,
  showing, acting, pointing, or passing).

## Home connection

Ask someone at home to tell you where one family tradition came from, and share what
you learned next time.
