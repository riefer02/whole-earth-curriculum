# Assessment

How we know learning happened — and make sure the knowing serves the learner. This
document is the assessment counterpart to [`philosophy.md`](philosophy.md) and
[`facilitation.md`](facilitation.md). It defines the modes of assessment, the mastery
scale, and the rules that keep assessment honest, fair, and non-coercive at every
grade and in every context.

> The measure of this curriculum is not test scores but whether learners become more
> self-aware, more self-motivated, more capable of joy and belonging. Assessment
> exists to serve that aim — nothing else.

## 1. Core stance

Assessment serves the learner, not the other way around. Three commitments flow from
that, and every instrument in this document must satisfy all three.

- **Mastery-focused, not ranked.** A learner is measured against a *standard*
  ([`philosophy.md`](philosophy.md) §8: "Mastery is individual; success is
  collective"), never against another learner. There is no curve, no percentile, no
  class rank. Two learners can both be at "mastery" and both be celebrated.
- **Growth-oriented, not scored.** The question is "how far have you come, and what is
  next?", never "how many points did you earn?". Evidence of growth is recorded, not
  converted into a verdict.
- **The end is flourishing, not scores.** The real measure is health and flourishing
  ([`philosophy.md`](philosophy.md) §12): does the learner grow more self-aware,
  self-motivated, and capable of joy, belonging, and care? Assessment is a *window*
  onto that, never a substitute for it.

What is forbidden follows directly:

- **No high-stakes instruments.** No single test that decides a learner's fate,
  placement, or worth.
- **No score-obsessed instruments.** No points-gathering, grade-chasing, or
  deficit-counting that turns learning into a transaction.
- **No ranking instruments.** No bell curves, no comparative labels, no public
  "winners" and "losers."
- **No belief-testing.** A learner is never assessed on *what they believe*, only on
  what they can *do and understand* ([`philosophy.md`](philosophy.md) §11 — inquiry
  over creed). Big questions are held open; the skill of weighing evidence is
  assessed, never the conclusion a learner reaches.

## 2. The five modes and how they interlock

Five modes carry the work of knowing. They are not competing; they interlock, and a
healthy unit uses all of them. Each stays non-coercive by being *low-stakes* (safe to
get wrong), *private or shared by choice*, and *used to adjust teaching, not to judge
a person*.

| Mode | What it is | When to use it | Non-coercive by… |
|------|-----------|----------------|------------------|
| **Formative** | In-the-moment observation and feedback: watching, listening, asking a question, catching a misconception early. | Constantly, throughout every lesson. | Being ungraded and directional — it tells the learner *where to look next*, never *how they rank*. |
| **Summative** | An end-of-unit check that a learner can demonstrate the unit's objectives. | At the close of a unit. | Being non-punitive: a "not yet" is information for the next step, not a penalty. There is always a path to revisit and show mastery. |
| **Performance** | Doing a *real task* that requires the skills in use — building, explaining, solving, making, teaching back. | When an objective is best shown by *doing* rather than telling. | Being authentic and done for an audience who wants the learner to succeed (peers, family, community), not a grader. |
| **Portfolio** | A growing collection of work that shows *change over time*. | Continuously; reviewed at milestones. | Being cumulative — it honors the arc, not a snapshot, and a learner can always add newer, stronger evidence. |
| **Self-assessment** | The learner checks their own understanding and progress. | Every lesson and unit, for every learner. | Being private (or shared by choice) and owned by the learner — it cannot be used against them. |

How they interlock: formative feedback during a lesson shapes practice; a summative
check confirms the objective was reached; the *performance* gives the summative check
its real-world shape; the *portfolio* gathers both into evidence of growth; and
*self-assessment* runs through all of it, so the learner is always a co-author of the
record. A unit's `assessment_plan` (see §7) names which modes carry which objectives.

## 3. Teacher / facilitator assessment

The facilitator's job is to observe what a learner can *do*, and to say back what they
saw — specifically and kindly. Assessment is described in **observable terms**: verbs
like *names, points to, explains, builds, asks, connects, corrects*, never invisible
states like *understands* or *gets it*. A rubric is a short list of those observable
actions at increasing depth (see [Mastery levels](#mastery-levels) below).

- **Observe first, judge never.** Watch the doing, then describe it back: "I saw you
  try three ways and find the one that balanced. That's persistence."
- **No ranking, ever.** Feedback names the learner's own progress ("you did X last
  week; now you do X and Y"), never their position among peers.
- **Specific and kind.** Say exactly what worked and one next step. "Your diagram
  labels the parts correctly — next, show how the parts affect each other." Never
  vague praise ("good job") or vague blame ("careless").
- **Feedback is for growth, not for the record's sake.** In large groups where a
  teacher cannot observe everyone individually, delegate: peer teaching, self-
  assessment, and portfolios carry the load ([`contexts.md`](contexts.md)).
- **Celebrate mastery; never turn it into competition** ([`facilitation.md`](facilitation.md)).

## 4. Learner self-assessment & mastery tracking

A self-directed learner — alone, or in a room without an adult — must be able to check
their own understanding, track mastery of each objective, and know when to move on
([`facilitation.md`](facilitation.md), "Facilitating self-directed learners"). The
curriculum gives them one simple, universal method.

### The mastery checklist

For each objective a learner works on, they keep a checklist with four levels. The
levels are the same scale used in rubrics (see [Mastery levels](#mastery-levels)), and
each is written so the learner can honestly self-place:

| Level | What it means, in the learner's words |
|-------|----------------------------------------|
| **Not yet** | "I can't do this yet, and that's okay. This is my next target." |
| **With help** | "I can do this when someone shows me or talks me through it." |
| **On my own** | "I can do this by myself, most of the time." |
| **Can teach someone else** | "I can explain or show it to another person." |

Concretely, a learner keeps a **per-objective mastery list** mapped to the standards
tree. It can be a page, a card, or marks in the margin of their portfolio:

```text
Objective D01.S1.K.01 — name basic feelings
  [ ] Not yet        [ ] With help        [ ] On my own        [x] Can teach

Objective D05.S2.02.01 — identify and extend growing patterns and describe how they change
  [ ] Not yet        [ ] With help        [x] On my own        [ ] Can teach
```

**How to self-place honestly.** Ask: *"Could I do this right now, alone? Could I
show it to a friend?"* If the answer is no to both, it's "not yet." If yes only with
a prompt, "with help." If yes alone, "on my own." If you could show someone else how,
"can teach." The act of *explaining* is itself the evidence — "the act of explaining
*is* the learning" ([`facilitation.md`](facilitation.md)).

**When to move on.** A learner moves on when a unit's objectives sit at **"on my own"
or "can teach."** "With help" and "not yet" are not failures; they are the map of
what to revisit — a few more practice rounds, a peer guide, or a return to the
procedure. There is no clock and no gate; the checklist is a compass, not a door.

**The "teach someone else" check.** The strongest self-check is explanation: say it
aloud, write it, or teach it to a peer or a younger learner. If the explanation holds
up to a real question, the learner *knows* they know.

## 5. Bias & fairness checks

Assessment is only fair if it measures the skill, not the learner's culture,
language, or access. Before any assessment is used, run these checks.

- **Culture.** Does the task presume one culture's default (a specific holiday, game,
  food, or "common knowledge")? Replace it with something every learner can anchor
  to, or offer choices. Ask "what happens in *your* home?" rather than "what happens
  on the holiday we all know?"
- **Language.** Is the task secretly a reading test? A learner who can *do* the skill
  but not read the prompt must still be able to show it — through speaking, drawing,
  acting, or an oral prompt. Offer response in any language the learner owns.
- **Learning style.** Is the task reachable through more than one modality — body,
  voice, image, movement, building? A single written form silently ranks readers over
  doers. Offer the low-tech path ([`contexts.md`](contexts.md)) and let the learner
  choose the form of their evidence.
- **Low-tech / low-resource.** Every assessment must run on `materials_low_tech`
  (paper, voices, bodies, outdoors). If an assessment *requires* a device, it is
  optional enrichment, never the gate.
- **Never assess belief.** This bears repeating from §1: never grade a learner on what
  they believe, only on skills and understanding ([`philosophy.md`](philosophy.md)
  §11). The skill of *weighing* accounts is assessable; the account a learner settles
  on is their own. Big questions — origins, meaning, right and wrong — are held open,
  always.
- **Stress and shame.** An assessment that makes a learner feel watched, judged, or
  afraid measures fear, not learning. Prefer quiet, private, or shared-by-choice
  formats; let a learner pause or step away ([`facilitation.md`](facilitation.md)).

## 6. The observability rule

> **Nothing is taught without a way to know it was learned.**

Every objective must have at least one observable way to be demonstrated. If an
objective cannot be observed — if no behavior, product, or explanation could show it —
then either the objective is miswritten or it is not an objective at all. This is a
hard rule for content authors:

- Every lesson's `objectives:` (standard IDs) must map to at least one observable
  check in its `## Assessment` section.
- Every unit's `assessment_plan` must cover every objective the unit teaches.
- The rubrics and checklists in §3–§4 are written against those same IDs, so a
  learner, a facilitator, and a validator can all see *which standard* each piece of
  evidence serves.

Assessment is tied to the `objectives:` IDs, not to a vague "understanding" of a
topic. `D01.S1.K.01` is assessed by observing a child name or point to a feeling — a
concrete, watchable act — never by asking whether they "understand feelings."

## 7. Alignment with the schema

Assessment lives in specific fields of the file format ([`schema.md`](schema.md)).
Content authors should know exactly where it goes:

- **`lesson.assessment_type`** — an array naming which modes the lesson uses
  (`formative`, `summative`, `performance`, `portfolio`, `self-assessment`, or a
  subset). Declares intent at a glance.
- **`lesson.objectives`** — the standard IDs the lesson teaches; the `## Assessment`
  body heading then shows *how each is observed* (see §6).
- **`unit.assessment_plan`** — the unit-level plan naming which modes carry which
  objectives across the unit, and where the portfolio and self-assessment checkpoints
  fall.
- **`scope.assessment_plan`** — the year-level plan; it should state how the five
  modes balance across the year and when portfolio reviews happen.
- **`materials_low_tech` / `materials_enriched`** — the material tiers; assessment
  must be possible on the low-tech tier (§5), with enriched tiers optional.
- **`context_variants`** — how assessment changes by context (`large-group`,
  `multi-age`, `self-directed`, `level-grouped`, `outdoor-only`). For example, in a
  `self-directed` context the self-assessment checklist is the primary instrument;
  in `multi-age` the "can teach someone else" level naturally becomes peer teaching.

The required `## Assessment` body heading in every lesson is where the observable
check lives, written to the learner ("you") so a self-directed learner can run it
alone ([`schema.md`](schema.md), "Voice: learner-first").

## 8. Age-appropriateness

Assessment looks different at each band, matched to development
([`development.md`](development.md)). It always stays observable, low-stakes, and
free of ranking — but its *form* matures with the learner.

- **K–2 (ages ~5–8).** Assessment is **observation, play, and portfolio.** The
  facilitator watches what a child *does* — naming a feeling, taking a turn, building
  a pattern — and notes it. Portfolios hold drawings, photos, and dictated words.
  Self-assessment is concrete and immediate ("which face is the happy one?"). No
  written tests; a "test" at this age is a game the child asks to play again.
- **3–5 (ages ~8–11).** Children can *do* a method (predict, test, observe) on real
  things, so **performance** grows: build it, show it, explain it. Checklists stay
  concrete; learners begin to self-place on the four levels with a prompt. Portfolios
  gather real products. Feedback stays about the work, not the child.
- **6–8 (ages ~11–14).** Metacognition begins, so **self-assessment and reflection**
  deepen: "what helped? what would I do differently?" Learners can *evaluate* sources
  and *weigh evidence* — assessment of these skills is about the reasoning shown, not
  the conclusion reached (inquiry over creed). Portfolios become curated, with the
  learner choosing their strongest evidence.
- **9–12 (ages ~14–18).** Learners are co-authors. Assessment is dominated by
  **self-assessment, reflection, and defended performance**: a project, an argument,
  or a body of work the learner stands behind and explains. The four-level checklist
  matures into goal-setting and progress tracking against standards
  (`D01.S3.10.01` is literally "direct their own learning … and check their own
  progress"). The hardest truths are assessed as *reasoning under ambiguity*, never
  as a single right answer.

## Mastery levels

The single, non-competitive scale used everywhere — in rubrics, in the self-
assessment checklist, and in facilitator feedback. Four observable levels, named in
the learner's own voice:

| Level | Observable signal | Evidence looks like… |
|-------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **1 · Not yet** | Cannot yet demonstrate the objective. | "I'm still learning this." No watchable act yet — this is the next target, not a failure. |
| **2 · With help** | Demonstrates the objective *with* a prompt, scaffold, or guide. | Names a feeling when the facilitator points to a picture; solves a step when shown the pattern. |
| **3 · On my own** | Demonstrates the objective independently, in a familiar setting. | Names and points to feelings without prompting; builds and explains without help. |
| **4 · Can teach someone else** | Demonstrates the objective *and* explains or shows it to another person. | Teaches a peer to name a feeling; explains a solution so a younger learner can follow it. |

Notes:

- Levels describe *mastery of one objective*, never a person. A learner can be "on my
  own" for one objective and "not yet" for the next — that is normal and expected.
- "Can teach someone else" is the deepest level, not an extra burden. Teaching is the
  clearest evidence of understanding, and it doubles as the cooperative structure the
  curriculum is built on ([`contexts.md`](contexts.md), peer teaching).
- There is no "failing" level. "Not yet" is an honest, kind, and temporary state.

## Worked example: `D01.S1.K.01`

**Objective (from the standards tree):** *Name and identify basic feelings (happy,
sad, angry, scared, calm) in self and others.* (`D01.S1.K.01`, Domain 1 — Self &
Identity, Strand 1 — Emotional Literacy.)

**"I can" statement** (written to the learner): *"I can name the feelings happy, sad,
angry, scared, and calm, and I can point to them in a picture, a friend, or myself."*

**Observable check** (the `## Assessment` for this objective): give the child a set of
face cards, drawn faces, or real faces and ask, *"Which one is happy? Which one is
scared? Show me a face you made when you felt angry."* The child *points, names, or
shows* — all three count as evidence, so a child who cannot yet read or who speaks a
different language can still demonstrate the objective. Low-tech, body-only, no
device.

**Self-assessment check** (the same objective on a learner's mastery list):

```text
D01.S1.K.01 — name basic feelings
  Not yet    With help    On my own    Can teach
                                      [x]  ← "I named all five, then showed my little
                                            sister which face is scared."
```

The child reaches **"can teach someone else"** when they can, without prompting, name
the five feelings *and* help someone else identify them. This is observable, low-
stakes, culture- and language-flexible, and maps exactly back to the standard ID —
assessment serving the learner, in the smallest possible compass.
