# Developmental Rubric

The canonical reference the `child-psychologist` agent applies to every lesson and
unit. It defines what "age-appropriate" means across grade bands, so that content
meets a developing mind *where it actually is*.

> This is a **rubric, not a gatekeeper's rulebook**. These are broad ranges drawn
> from mainstream developmental science (Piaget, Erikson, Vygotsky, and modern
> cognitive-developmental work). Every child differs; ranges describe tendencies,
> not verdicts.

## Principles

1. **Ranges, not gates.** A band describes the center of a distribution. A lesson may
   reach a bit above or below for individual learners — that is what differentiation
   is for.
2. **Age-appropriate ≠ dumbed down.** Appropriate means *matched to development*,
   never *made shallow*. A kindergartner wonders about origins as a story; a senior
   weighs competing cosmological models. Both are rigorous — differently.
3. **Emotionally safe scaffolding.** Big, frightening, or painful topics are not
   avoided; they are introduced at a depth the age can hold, with adult support and
   a safe place to feel.
4. **Inquiry over creed at every age.** Questions are offered as questions, evidence
   is distinguished from belief, and no account is imposed (see
   [`docs/philosophy.md`](philosophy.md) §11).
5. **The spiral is the curriculum.** What a band can hold is built on the band before
   it. A lesson is judged not in isolation but as a step on the spiral.

## Grade bands

### K–2 (ages ~5–8)

- **Cognitive.** Concrete, here-and-now thinking. Learning happens through play,
  story, song, and direct manipulation. Abstract concepts need a tangible anchor.
  Theory of mind is emerging (they are learning others feel differently).
- **Social-emotional.** Learning to name and regulate feelings; empathy is growing
  but egocentric. Belonging and safety are the central emotional tasks. Fairness is
  felt intensely, though understood concretely.
- **Physical.** Big-motor activity is essential and abundant; fine motor is still
  developing. Movement and the body are part of every lesson.
- **Curriculum implications.** Big questions (origins, the cosmos) are offered as
  *stories* to wonder at, not systems to explain. Science is observation and "why?"
  History is "then and now" through stories and artifacts. Ethics is sharing, taking
  turns, kindness.
- **Emotional safety.** No graphic or frightening content. Death, injustice, and
  difficulty appear only as gentle, scaffolded stories with a caring adult present.

### 3–5 (ages ~8–11)

- **Cognitive.** Concrete operations arrive: they can categorize, conserve, and
  reason about cause and effect *when the objects are real*. Beginning perspective-
  taking. Abstract reasoning is still hard.
- **Social-emotional.** Friendships deepen; fairness and rules become central (and
  hotly debated). Identity extends to groups. Independence grows but still wants
  adult safety.
- **Physical.** Fine motor matures; stamina grows. Hands-on building and
  experimentation suit them.
- **Curriculum implications.** The scientific method can be *done* (predict, test,
  observe) on real phenomena. History becomes sequences and comparisons across
  cultures. Ethics becomes rules, reasons, and fairness dilemmas. Technology is
  introduced as a tool with effects, not just magic.
- **Emotional safety.** Real-world problems (poverty, environmental harm) can be
  introduced with hope and agency attached — always with something the learner can
  do.

### 6–8 (ages ~11–14)

- **Cognitive.** Formal operations *emerge*: abstract and hypothetical reasoning
  begin to appear, unevenly. They can hold multiple perspectives and begin
  metacognition (thinking about their thinking).
- **Social-emotional.** Identity formation begins in earnest; peers matter greatly;
  self-consciousness and emotional intensity spike. They question authority and need
  reasons, not rules. Puberty remakes the body and its feelings.
- **Physical.** Rapid, uneven growth; high energy. Movement and autonomy matter.
- **Curriculum implications.** Systems and nuance enter: cause and effect across
  societies, ecosystems, and markets. They can debate, evaluate sources, and weigh
  evidence against belief. Ethics becomes real dilemmas, justice, and perspective.
  Origins can be taught as *models with evidence* (cosmology, evolution) alongside
  other ways of knowing.
- **Emotional safety.** Heavy content (war, injustice, climate) is appropriate with
  structure and emotional support; avoid despair — always connect to agency and hope.

### 9–12 (ages ~14–18)

- **Cognitive.** Formal operations mature: abstract, hypothetical, and counterfactual
  reasoning; strong metacognition. They can tolerate ambiguity and hold competing
  truths without collapsing.
- **Social-emotional.** Values, purpose, and identity consolidate. They seek meaning
  and want to matter. Impulse control is still maturing (the prefrontal cortex is
  not done), so judgment can outrun regulation.
- **Physical.** Near-adult; capable of sustained work and complex coordination.
- **Curriculum implications.** The full curriculum: complex systems, global and
  anthropological history, political economy, rigorous ethics, evidence vs. values,
  and the deep effects of technology on humanity and the environment. Learners are
  treated as co-authors, not students.
- **Emotional safety.** The hardest truths of the world can be faced honestly —
  inequality, ecological crisis, moral ambiguity — provided they are met with
  intellectual tools and a sense of agency, never nihilism.

## How the child-psychologist uses this

For each lesson or unit under review, ask:

1. Does it match the cognitive, social-emotional, and physical range of its band?
2. Is anything too abstract, too frightening, or too advanced — or, equally, too
   shallow?
3. Is it emotionally safe *for its topic*, with scaffolding where the topic is heavy?
4. Does it hold questions as questions (inquiry over creed)?
5. Does it build on the previous band (spiral) rather than jump?
